# American White Pelican: measurements, late-September plumage, field marks, how it floats, bill-dipping and cooperative foraging, and flight in lines

Date: 2026-09-30
Purpose: source data for the detailed American White Pelican (`amwpel`, *Pelecanus erythrorhynchos*) model, for a pelican swimming and foraging style, and for the flyover rebuilt with detailed birds and spread wings. The scene: Willow Lake, Willamette Valley, Oregon, a morning in late September (about 20-30 September). The player stands on the bank with binoculars (7.5° vertical field) and a telescope (2°: about 1.3-2.9 mm of bird per pixel at 40-90 m on a 1080-pixel-tall view). The pelican appears two ways: a small group swimming, loafing and bill-dipping on open water 40-90 m out, and a flock of nine crossing the sky in a line.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt or a summary of a paywalled page; I could not read the page, so treat it as unverified, and its wording may be the search engine's paraphrase. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo (section 8) and scaled it by the exposed culmen (315 mm, section 2.6) unless stated. "Video" means read off contact sheets of frames from the freely licensed clips of section 7.3; every video figure is an estimate from one to a few birds. "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements** (mean of the sexes; mm): exposed culmen **315**, bill depth **39** and width **40** at the nostril, tarsus **119**, middle toe **101**, wing chord **583**, tail **150** (24 feathers), wingspan **2.67 m**, mass about **5.7 kg**. The sexes differ more than in any earlier species here: the male's culmen is 19% longer (342 against 288), his wing 9% and his mass about 28% (section 2).
- **Model length** (bill tip to tail tip, swimming with the bill down) is **about 0.85 m** on the traced bird (photo-measured), against 1.27-1.65 m for stretched skins. The body, breast to tail tip, is about **two culmens, 0.63 m**.
- **Two versions.** Of 93 birds close enough to age in the valley's late-September photos, 72 were white adults, 13 had the brown-flecked wing coverts of the year's young, and 8 were white-winged with a grey crown and nape (section 3). Paint the **non-breeding adult** (no horn, no crest; bill pinkish to yellow-orange, pouch yellow-orange, feet orange) and a **juvenile** (grey-brown scaled lesser and median coverts, a dusky wash on the crown and hindneck, a paler pink bill). The grey nape patch of midsummer adults is an optional third.
- **Cast.** On the water: **five** (four adults, one juvenile). Swimming groups in the valley photos are 1-7 birds in three-quarters of records, median 2.5. In the sky: **nine** in a line is honest (flying records: median 4.5, a quarter of them 12 or more; published "six to fourteen").
- **How it floats:** high. The top of the folded wings rides **about 0.14 m** above the water, the crown **0.29 m** with the neck in its resting S (0.33 m erect), the tail tip about 0.03 m; the black primaries show only as a thin dark line along the bottom of the folded wing at the stern. About **30% of the body's depth** is under water (computed from mass and plan area; not visible).
- **Bill carriage:** swimming with the head up, the bill points **37-59° below level** with the tip at or just over the water (photo 37°, video median 48°).
- **Foraging:** it never plunge-dives; it swims and dips. A dip takes **1.3 ± 0.7 s** without a catch and 2.0 ± 1.0 s with one, and swallowing 1.2 ± 0.9 s [McMahon & Evans 1992, Table 1]; about **one dip in nine** catches something (computed from the same table). Birds in groups of four or more dip about **6 times a minute**, single birds about 1.3 [Anderson 1987, Fig. 6]. The head and often part of the neck go under; the wings lift in an arch over the back during the dip (photo and video). When one bird of a group dips the rest follow within 3 s [Anderson 1987], 0.25-1.0 s on video.
- **Flight:** wingbeat **2.7-2.9 Hz** (160-175 beats a minute, Snippet from Birds of the World); 3.0 Hz on video in a climb. **The game's 1.6 Hz is too slow.** Lines flap and glide, flapping 60-75% of the time, each bird taking its cue from the one ahead within 0.5 s (Snippet; described in the same words by Ridgway in 1884). Head on the shoulders, bill on the foreneck, feet tucked under the tail. From below the black takes all the primaries and the rear two-fifths of the wing inward almost to the body.
- **Sound:** adults away from the colony are silent. A scheduled call every 200-600 s is not honest (section 7.6).

## 2. Measurements

The species is monotypic. Ridgway's *Birds of North and Middle America* never reached the pelicans. The primary tables I could read are Baird, Brewer & Ridgway's *Water Birds of North America* (1884), Ridgway's *Manual* (1887) and the AVONET skins; the sexed series printed by Birds of the World v2.0 I could read only as a search excerpt (Snippet). Palmer 1962, Pyle 2008, Dunning 2008 and Dorr et al. 2005 themselves were not read.

### 2.5 The length caution, and the model length

Published lengths are stretched skins, bill tip to tail tip with the neck laid straight. A swimming pelican carries its neck in an upright S and its bill slanting down, so the model's `length` is far shorter. Photo-measured, bill tip to tail tip along the water:

| Photo | Culmen, px | Bill tip to tail tip | Breast front to tail tip | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `amwpel-side-1.jpeg` | 558 (0.565 mm/px) | 1503 px, **0.85 m** | 1155 px, 0.65 m (2.07 culmens) | strictly side-on, bill 37° below level |
| `amwpel-side-2.jpg` | 247 (1.28 mm/px) | 843 px, 1.07 m | 675 px, 0.86 m (2.73 culmens) | neck more erect; the head is turned a little toward the camera or the bird is a short-billed female, so the culmen scale reads large |
| Video clip 3, two birds side-on | 300-320 | - | about 580 px (1.9 culmens, 0.60 m) | [Hannawacker 2017a] |

Two of the three agree that the body, breast to tail tip, is about **two culmens (0.60-0.65 m)**; the third bird is the outlier and I do not use its culmen scale. Because the sexes' bills differ by a fifth while their bodies differ by less than a tenth, a culmen scale carries about ±10% of doubt on any single bird of unknown sex. **Adopted: 0.85 m** for the traced bird; a test range of 0.80-1.05 m covers a short-billed female.

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

- **The adult through the year.** Breeding: "Culmen with a narrow median horny excrescence, situated a little anterior to the middle of the culmen", a "pendant crest of long, narrow, silky, pure white or pale straw-colored feathers", bill "chiefly orange", "legs and feet intense orange-red". "Adult, during latter part of the breeding-season: Similar to the above, but maxillary excrescence wanting (having been cast), and the nuchal crest replaced by a patch of brownish gray. Adult, in fall and winter: Similar to the last, but no grayish patch on the occiput (crest also absent), the bill and feet clear yellow" [BBR 1884, vol. II, pp. 133-134].
- **The horn is long gone by September.** It "is dropped before or soon after the young are hatched"; at Pyramid Lake "By the 25th of May not a bird was to be seen with one of these appendages", and of the thousands seen there in August "not one was found possessed of the horny appendage" [BBR 1884, vol. II, pp. 135, 138]. "The horn is shed soon after the eggs are laid" [Bent 1922, p. 288].
- **The grey nape, and when it goes.** With the horn "the nuchal crest falls off, and in its place a patch of short brownish gray feathers appears; this disappears with the fall moult, when the occiput is entirely unadorned" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 135]. "the occipital crest is soon replaced by a mottled gray cap ... the greater number of breeding birds have the gray caps, which are lost at the end of the breeding season, or at the following postnuptial molt" [Bent 1922, p. 288]. Ridgway's *Manual* separates a "Post-nuptial plumage" with "a patch of short grayish feathers" from the "Winter adult: Plumage as in the preceding, but gray occipital patch wanting ... and colors of bill, face, pouch, and feet much less intense, a clear lemon-yellow being the prevailing tint" [Ridgway 1887, pp. 81-82]. Birds of the World: "In Supplemental plumage, crown and nape plumes are shed and replaced by short medium to dark grayish feathers"; the molt that brings it "generally follows egg laying and is completed about the time eggs hatch"; "Definitive Basic plumage extends from late summer or fall until late winter" [BoW-A] (Snippet). No source dates the loss of the grey patch more closely than "the fall moult"; the photo tally (section 3.2) finds a few grey-naped, white-winged birds still present from 23 to 27 September.
- **The young bird.** "Young: Similar to the winter adult, but lesser wing-coverts brownish gray centrally, the pileum similarly marked; jugular feathers short and broad, and pure white ...; bill, pouch, and feet pale yellow" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 134]; "Plumage white, but lesser wing-coverts and feathers of top of head brownish gray centrally ... the bill, pouch, face, and feet pale yellowish" [Ridgway 1887, p. 82]. "juvenile plumage is dusky white with a grayish crown and black wing pattern that resembles the adult by fledging, with the crown apparently fading to mostly white in later stages" [BoW-A] (Snippet). "Immatures are mostly white as well, but the head, neck, and back are variably dusky" [AAB-ID]. "The first winter plumage is acquired in the fall when the young birds closely resemble the adults" [Bent 1922, p. 288]. The young leave the colony "at 10 to 11 weeks" and fly "at about 9 to 10 weeks of age" [AAB-O; Boreal], so the year's young are on the wing and wandering by late summer.
- **Bare parts outside the breeding season.** "the naked skin of the face and feet being fiery orange-red instead of pale straw-yellow, as in August" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 138]; "Bill, facial skin, feet, and legs are yellow-orange in non-breeding plumage to deep orange in breeding plumage. Iris coloration depends upon age and season, ranging from bright white to hazel to blue-gray" [BoW-A] (Snippet); "iris pearl-white ... said to be sometimes hazel" in May birds [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 134 and footnote].

### 3.2 iNaturalist, Willamette Valley box

Taxon 4334 (*Pelecanus erythrorhynchos*), research grade, with photos, box 43.8-45.8 N, 122.6-123.6 W: 225 records in September and October of all years, **143 from 11 September to 12 October** [iNat query 2026]. The box was not widened: it takes in Fern Ridge, Finley (Cabell Marsh), Ankeny, the Tualatin valley wetlands, Sauvie Island and the Portland-Vancouver lakes. I looked at the first photo of each of the 143 on contact sheets, and at larger copies of 36 (appendix).

**Ages**, on the 28 records whose birds were close enough (93 birds):

| Class | Birds | Share | What it looks like on the photos |

| **A**: white adult | 72 | 77% | head and wing coverts white; bill pink to orange, pouch yellow to orange; feet orange |

- **J** are the year's young by BBR's and Ridgway's description (lesser coverts brownish grey centrally). They were seen from 11 September to 4 October. The traced profile bird itself (`amwpel-side-1.jpeg`) has faint grey-brown flecks on its coverts and a wholly white head: the dusky crown had already faded, as Birds of the World says it does.
- **G** are most simply adults still carrying the grey patch of the chick-feeding season (five of the eight are in one flock with bright orange bills, 24 September 2022), but a young bird with a dusky head and clean coverts cannot be ruled out on a photo; I keep them apart for that reason. BBR's "disappears with the fall moult" fits their being a small minority by late September.
- Counting is biased toward close, tame birds; the shares are rough.

### 3.3 Recommendation

- **Two versions.**
  - `adult` (the default): the non-breeding adult. All white; no horn, no crest, no yellow breast patch worth painting at this range; bill pink to yellow-orange with an orange nail, pouch yellow-orange, bare skin round the eye yellow; iris dark; legs and feet orange. The black primaries show only at the stern (section 5.3).
  - `juvenile`: the same bird with grey-brown, pale-fringed lesser and median coverts (a scaled brown shoulder patch covering the upper half of the folded wing's front two-thirds), a grey wash on the crown and hindneck, and a paler, pinker bill and pouch.
  - Optional later: `greyNape`, the adult with a dark-grey flecked crown and nape.
- **On the water: five birds, four adults and one juvenile.** Three-quarters of the swimming records are of 1-7 birds; five makes a group that can forage together (Anderson's groups do best at three to six, section 7.2) and loaf together, and one in five is close to the photos' 14% of juveniles.
- **In the sky: nine, all the adult version.** A flyover flock is one version in this game; at several hundred metres the juvenile's coverts do not read, and from below the two are alike.

### 3.4 Status in the valley in autumn

- Thirty years ago the species was a rarity west of the Cascades: "Rarely seen in western Oregon historically, non-breeding American White Pelicans have been summering in variable numbers in the Willamette Valley for about 10 years" [Gillson 2010, after Marshall et al. 2003]. Fern Ridge Reservoir's conservation account lists it as "Present, not breeding" in the breeding season and "Casual" in migration and winter, and says "American white pelicans, not normally found in western Oregon, occur year round" [ABC 2010, pp. 1, 3].
- It has since become expected: "Fifteen years ago, White Pelicans in the Portland area were a pretty big deal, but now they are expected in the larger wetlands in summer" (July 2016); "Lots of American White Pelicans are in the Willamette Valley right now" (9 September 2019); "Now you can expect 100 or more around Sauvie Island in the late summer" (August 2020); "They don't nest here, but summer brings large numbers of young birds and post-breeding adults" (August 2021); "now common in the Portland area in late summer" (September 2021); "There are still a few American White Pelicans around. They will be gone soon" (27 October 2022) [Rakestraw 2016-2022].
- "Pelicans make a stop in the Eugene area in spring and fall during annual migrations. Fern Ridge Reservoir and Kirk Pond are favorites" [Pietsch 2026]. The state account still describes the species as a bird of eastern Oregon, "locally common to abundant March through October in Klamath, Lake and Harney counties" [ODFW]; western populations "move south from early September to late November" [BirdWeb].
- **What I could not reach:** eBird bar charts and high counts, Marshall et al. 2003 (*Birds of Oregon*) itself, and *Oregon Birds* field notes. The numbers above (a hundred or more at Sauvie Island; photographed flocks of up to about 50 on the water and 60 in the air in the box) are the best I have.

## 4. Field marks

Colour words are the sources'; I have not invented hex values. "(photo)" marks my observation on the reference photos of section 8. The sexes are alike in plumage [BBR 1884; BoW-A].

### 4.1 What the sources say

- **A huge white bird with black in the wing.** "Color white, the primaries blackish" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 133]; "primaries dull black, their shafts white toward the base; secondaries dusky, edged both externally and internally with ashy-white" [p. 133]; "Adult: Entirely white, with quills entirely black, the secondaries also chiefly black" [Ridgway 1887, p. 81]; "all feathering is white except for black primaries, distal secondaries, and alula which is highly conspicuous on the white forewing; medial secondaries are black but tipped white with progressively more white until proximal secondaries are all white" [BoW-A] (Snippet).
- **The black hides on a resting bird.** "Adult American White Pelicans are snowy white with black flight feathers visible only when the wings are spread" [AAB-ID]; "All white when on the ground, but note black flight feathers when wing is spread" [AAB-ID, photo caption].
- **The bill.** "an enormous orange bill" [ODFW]; "Large waterbird with a long yellow bill with an extendable pouch" (non-breeding adult) [AAB-ID, photo caption]; "The bill and legs are yellow-orange" [AAB-ID]; in fall and winter "the bill and feet clear yellow" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 134].
- **Shape.** "thick bodies, short legs, and short, square tails"; "very broad wings, a long neck" [AAB-ID]. "It looms up large and white even at a great distance. Its color pattern is somewhat similar to that of three other large birds, the gannet, the whooping crane, and the wood ibis, but in size and shape the four are distinctly different" [Bent 1922, p. 290].
- **In flight.** "During flight, the American White Pelican's long neck is folded back on its body, and its black primaries and outer secondaries contrast prominently with the rest of the wing" [BirdWeb]; "flies with neck withdrawn" [ODFW]; "the head is drawn back upon the shoulders" [Bent 1922, p. 290].

### 4.3 What reads at telescope range, and in the sky

On the water through the telescope (1.3 mm per pixel at 40 m, 2.9 at 90 m):
- **The bill:** 315 mm, 110-240 px long: the largest single mark on any bird in the game. With its pouch it is 25-55 px deep at the base, pink above and yellow-orange below.
- **The white bulk:** a body 0.65 m long riding 0.14 m out of the water, 220-500 px long. Nothing else on the lake is this white or this big.
- **The neck column:** about 120 mm thick fore and aft where the folded S sits in its plumage, 40-95 px.
- **The black:** a line 10-15 mm deep along the bottom of the folded wing at the stern, 4-10 px: it reads at 40 m and is at the limit at 90 m. Paint it, but the bird must read as all white.
- **The juvenile's shoulder:** the scaled grey-brown patch is about 250 × 100 mm, 85-190 px long: it reads plainly at every distance in the zone.
- **The eye:** about 11 mm across, 4-8 px, in a patch of yellow skin about twice that.
- **The feet:** orange, seen at the waterline under the stern when the bird paddles (photo).

In the sky at 150-400 m through binoculars (7.5°: about 18-50 mm per pixel) a bird with a 2.67 m span is 55-150 px across:
- **White wings with black tips and a black trailing edge**, the black taking the outer third of the wing whole.
- **The shape:** head sunk on the shoulders, the bill a long wedge in front; no neck; a short tail; no trailing legs. Body and bill together are half the wingspan (photo-measured, section 7.5).
- **The flight:** slow beats and long glides, the flap passing down the line.

## 5. Structure

### 5.1 Bill and pouch

- **Shape:** "Bill greatly elongated and excessively depressed, the terminal unguis very prominent and strongly hooked" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 132]. In profile the culmen is straight from the forehead to the nail, which turns down in a short hook (photo).
- **Profile, photo-measured on `amwpel-side-1.jpeg`** (0.565 mm/px; depths taken square to the bill's axis):
  - the upper mandible alone is about **23 mm** deep at the feather line and the lower jaw's rim a strip about 10 mm deep under it;
  - at two-thirds of the way out the closed mandibles together are about **20 mm** deep;
  - the nail is about **14 mm** deep and its tip hooks about **6 mm** below the lower outline;
  - AVONET's 38.8 mm at the nostril is the closed bill at its base [Tobias et al. 2022]. So the bill tapers little: about 39 mm at the base, 20 mm at two-thirds, 14 mm at the nail.
- **Width:** 39.6 mm at the nostril [Tobias et al. 2022]. I have no measured width along the bill; on the rear-view photo with the bill open (`amwpel-rear.jpeg`) the upper mandible is a flat blade of nearly even width, a little broader in its outer third (photo, not measured). **Estimate:** parallel-sided at 40 mm to about 0.85 of the length, then rounding in to the nail.
- **The pouch at rest:** "which they have the power of contracting, when empty, into a small compass, so that it hardly hangs below the bill, though when fully expanded it is of great size" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 137]. On the profile photo the pouch hangs deepest at its rear corner, under the eye, where bill and pouch together are **about 75 mm** deep; its lower edge then runs nearly straight to the bill tip, where it has no depth (photo-measured). "Malar region completely feathered" [BBR 1884]: the white feathering of the cheek comes down to the lower jaw, so the pouch's rear edge is a clean curve against white.
- **The pouch when scooping and stretched:** it balloons below and to the sides of the lower jaw, whose two rami bow apart. On `amwpel-pouch.jpeg` and `amwpel-rear.jpeg` (a bird stretching its pouch with the bill open) the lower jaw drops about 45-50° from the upper and the pouch hangs as a deep bag (photo). "Because of the suction effect created by the expansion of a pelican's pouch during a strike it is possible that more than one small fish were taken" [Anderson 1987, p. 5].
- **The face:** the eye sits in a patch of bare skin that runs forward as the lores to the bill; the forehead feathering meets the culmen about 30 mm in front of the eye's centre (photo-measured: eye (1449, 343), feather edge (1502, 362)). The eye's dark disc is about **11 mm** across (20 px), so the model's eye radius is **about 5.5 mm**.

### 5.2 Head and neck

- **The resting S.** A swimming pelican holds its hindneck upright and its head drawn back, the bill slanting down in front of the foreneck. The folded neck sits inside loose plumage, so the S does not show: the neck reads as one thick white column, about **124 mm** fore and aft at mid-height on the profile photo, with the head hardly wider (photo-measured). Goss saw them "swimming on the water with partially opened wings, and head drawn down and back, the bill just clearing the water" [Bent 1922, p. 288].
- **Bill angle, head up:** 37° below level on the profile photo, 50° and 53° on the two birds of `amwpel-side-pair.jpeg` (photo-measured); 37-59°, median 48°, on six readings from two swimming birds [Hannawacker 2017a] (Video). The tip is at or just over the water: 60 mm up on the profile photo.
- **Does the bill rest on the foreneck?** Swimming with the head up, no: there is a clear gap between bill and neck (photo; Video). At rest it does: on `amwpel-rear-rest.jpeg` a loafing bird has its head sunk on its shoulders and the bill laid down across its breast with the tip at the water; a standing bird in [Bohlke 2018] held the bill pressed to the foreneck at about 60° below level (Video). In flight the bill rests on the folded foreneck (section 7.5).
- **Crown heights above the water** (photo-measured; section 6): resting S **0.29 m**; erect and alert **0.33 m**.
- **Asleep:** the head is turned back and the bill laid along the back and buried in the scapulars, only the crown and eye showing: on the water on two valley photos (205183931, 94788206) and standing in most loafing flocks (photo; Video, section 7.4).

### 5.3 Body, folded wing and tail

- **Body:** broad and flat-bottomed. On the one rear view with a usable scale (`amwpel-rear.jpeg`, its open upper mandible 640 px, taken as side-on: 0.49 mm/px) the body is 780 px, **about 0.38 m**, wide at the water with the folded wings; the same photo's proportion of width to back height (1.8) applied to the profile bird's 0.144 m of back gives 0.26 m. **Estimate: 0.32 m** wide at the water, give or take a fifth.
- **The folded wing on a swimming bird.** The white coverts, scapulars and tertials cover the whole side; the loose tertials and inner secondaries stand up a little at the rear as a ragged "sail" whose tips are 70-105 mm above the water. **The black primaries do show, but only just:** as a dark line 10-15 mm deep along the bottom edge of the folded wing from mid-body to the stern, at the waterline, and as black feather tips under the white tertials at the stern (photo: `amwpel-side-1.jpeg`, `amwpel-side-pair.jpeg`, `amwpel-rear.jpeg`). When the bird lifts its wings to dip, or preens, the black shows broadly (photo: `amwpel-sleep.jpeg`).
- **Tail:** "very short, nearly even, or slightly rounded" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 132]; white; on the water its tip lies about 30 mm above the surface, beyond the wing tips (photo-measured).

### 5.4 Legs and feet

- "Toes fully webbed, the outer almost as long as the middle, the inner much shorter" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 132]: all four toes are joined by web (totipalmate), the hind toe turned inward and forward and webbed to the inner toe. On the landing photo (`amwpel-land.jpg`) the spread foot shows three webs.
- Tarsus 119 mm, short and stout; the legs set far back. Colour orange in the valley's late-September adults, duller in the young (photo).
- On a swimming bird the feet show only now and then, as an orange heel at the waterline under the stern (photo: `amwpel-side-1.jpeg` at (835, 810); `amwpel-adult-sail.jpeg`).

**Method.** On `amwpel-side-1.jpeg` (a strictly side-on bird on calm water, its reflection nearly symmetrical, so the camera was close to water level) I read points off grid-overlaid enlargements. - **Shape of the back line:** it rises from the mantle (90 mm) to the top of the folded wings at mid-body (145 mm) and falls to the tail (30 mm): the bird is highest in the middle, unlike a dabbling duck whose stern rides high.
- `amwpel-side-2.jpg`, scaled by its body instead of its culmen (breast to tail tip set to 0.65 m: 0.96 mm/px), gives a crown 337 mm and a back 176 mm above the water with the neck erect: the same picture, low confidence.

**Draft cannot be seen;** it comes from buoyancy.
- "On the water it floats lightly as a cork, on account of its great displacement"; "its light hollow bones and the large air sac under its skin give it great displacement" [Bent 1922, pp. 289-290]. "These birds are more buoyant than Brown Pelicans" [Boreal]; they "have subcutaneous air sacs in the region of their breast that give them buoyancy" [ADW].
- A 5.7 kg bird displaces 5,700 cm³. Taking the hull under water as half an ellipsoid on a waterplane 0.57-0.65 m long and 0.30-0.34 m wide, its depth is **50-65 mm** (computed). With 145 mm of back above the water, about **30% of the body's depth is under water** (0.26-0.31, computed; an Estimate, since the body's width and underwater shape are assumed).
- **Recommendation:** set the waterline so that the traced back stands 145 mm clear at mid-body and give the hull 55-60 mm of draft. The block model's `draft: 0.35` is already close.

**Swimming speed.**
- Travelling with the head up: median **0.88 body lengths a second** (0.53-1.2; 15 one-second windows, two birds) [Hannawacker 2017a] (Video), which at 0.65 m from breast to tail tip is **about 0.57 m/s** (0.35-0.8, computed).
- A foraging group driving forward while dipping: **about 0.5 body lengths a second** (0.45-0.65), **0.3-0.4 m/s** [Schulenburg 2013] (Video; the scale is weak, the birds being angled to the camera).
- Loafing: no clip shows it. **Estimate:** under 0.05 m/s of its own motion, plus the wind's drift.
- In words: "it swims rapidly and easily" [Bent 1922, p. 290]; "strong swimming" [AAB-LH]; beaters "glide smoothly out, so as not to frighten the life beneath" before a drive, then go "rapidly striking the water with their wings" [Goss 1888, in Bent 1922, p. 288]. No source gives a speed.

**Wake.** No measurement. The travelling birds on video and on `amwpel-side-pair-2.jpeg` trail a low V from the breast; a 0.57 m waterline at 0.57 m/s is well under its hull speed, so the wake is slight (Estimate). The dip leaves a ring where the head went in and a second where the bill drains (photo: `amwpel-drain.jpeg`).

## 7. Behaviour

### 7.1 Foraging: what the sources say

**Method.**
- "The white pelican does not dive for its food like the brown pelican, but catches it on or near the surface by swimming or wading in shallow water" [Bent 1922, p. 288]. "The American White Pelican obtains food by dipping its bill into the water and scooping up prey. It does not plunge-dive from the air like the Brown Pelican" [BoW-I]. "No evidence of diving activity was recorded during the course of the study"; "In seven seasons of observing White Pelicans I have yet to see a dive" [Anderson 1987, pp. 16, 44].
- "American White Pelicans normally feed by moving on the water surface and dipping their bill down into the water column. When prey is captured, the forager raises its head well above the water surface to swallow" [McMahon & Evans 1992, p. 101].
- "when so fishing, if they ran into a shoal of minnows, they would stretch out their necks, drop their heads upon the water, and with open mouths and extended pouches scoop up the tiny fry" [Goss 1888, in Bent 1922, p. 288]. "They take in both water and fish, and then hold their bills vertically to drain out the water before swallowing the food" [Boreal]. "They forage by swimming on the surface, dipping their bills to scoop up fish, then raising their bills to drain water and swallow their prey" [AAB-LH]. "the fish is grasped transversely, when it is tossed into the air and invariably received with its head foremost in its descent into the pouch" (Hatch 1892, in [Bent 1922, p. 289]).
- "They often upend, like a very large dabbling duck, in this process" [AAB-ID]; "or tip-up like an oversized dabbling duck" [AAB-O]. None of the 58 valley photos of birds on the water and none of the four foraging clips shows a full upending (section 7.3).
- "Strikes ranged from a relatively slow probing thrust, with the bill held closed to a rapid stabbing motion"; "Pelicans frequently probed at the base of partially submerged vegetation and it seems likely that the birds are using a combination of tactile and visual cues" [Anderson 1987, pp. 30, 15]. "Diurnal foragers were frequently seen moving their heads as though scanning the water prior to dipping their bills" [McMahon & Evans 1992, p. 108].

**Timing of the dip** (Dauphin River, Manitoba; focal birds timed from "the times that the bill struck the water and was subsequently raised", the "dipping time", and from the bill's leaving the water "until the prey was fully swallowed", the "swallowing time") [McMahon & Evans 1992, pp. 102, 104, Table 1]:

| | Day: mean ± SD (n) | Night: mean ± SD (n) |
|---|---|---|
| Dipping time, no capture | **1.3 ± 0.7 s** (1,131) | 1.6 ± 0.8 s (4,814) |
| Dipping time, with a capture | **2.0 ± 1.0 s** (140) | 1.8 ± 1.7 s (46) |
| Swallowing time | **1.2 ± 0.9 s** (140) | 1.9 ± 1.2 s (46) |

- "Dipping time ... averaged from 1-2 sec" [p. 104].
- **Catch rate per dip** (computed from the sample sizes): by day 140 captures in 1,271 dips, **0.11**; by night 46 in 4,860, 0.01. "dip rates were well in excess of capture rates" [p. 107]. In Nevada "probing and striking ... did not result in prey capture in the majority of cases" [Anderson 1987, p. 30].
- **Prey size by day:** very small (under a quarter of the bill's length) 67.1%, small (a quarter to a half) 23.2%, medium 8.0%, large 1.5%, very large (over a bill's length) 0.2% [McMahon & Evans 1992, Table 3]. "Small and Very Small fish were often hidden in the gular pouch" [p. 103]: most catches show only as a swallow.

**Dip rate.**
- Strikes per bird per minute against flock size, read from the figure [Anderson 1987, Fig. 6, p. 32]: one bird about **1.3**; two 4.1; three 5.7; four 6.6; five 6.4; six 6.0; seven 6.8; eight 5.8; nine 6.5; ten 6.1. "Single birds struck least, and an upper asymptote was observed between flock sizes of 3 and 4"; "the maximum number of strikes per minute falling between 6 and 8" [pp. 30, 62]. The published version: "The rate of bill dipping is highly variable, increasing with flock size to about 6/min for 4 or more foragers" [Anderson 1991] (Snippet).
- "Rate of bill dipping increased significantly as foragers approached shore" [McMahon & Evans 1992, p. 106].
- **Captures per bird per minute** [Anderson 1987, Table 2, p. 27]: one bird 0.035; two 0.065; three **0.128**; four 0.078; five 0.097; six 0.056; seven and eight 0.063; nine 0.020; ten 0.040. "Pelicans in groups of size 2 through 6 caught more fish than single birds" [abstract]. These are head-tosses, at "at least one fish capture per head toss" [p. 5]; they are far lower than McMahon & Evans's one in nine dips, a different place and prey (carp and tui chub in turbid Nevada marshes against perch and minnows in a Manitoba river).

**Where.** "Most foragers were located in shallow water (≤ 1.5 m)"; "Most prey were captured in water less than 1 m deep during the day (99.4% of captures)" [McMahon & Evans 1992, pp. 105-106]. In Nevada groups "foraged almost exclusively in water less than 2 m deep", though some drove chub in from "water over 7 m deep" [Anderson 1987, pp. 16, 22]. "Where late summer temperatures bring sunning fish near the surface, these pelicans can forage on deeper lakes" [AAB-LH].

### 7.2 Cooperative foraging

- **Two forms** [Anderson 1987, pp. 17, Figs. 2-3]:
  - toward a shelving shore: "members of the flock formed a line or arc facing the shore and moved in toward the bank. As the birds reached the shallows it was often possible to see the disturbance caused by fish swimming ahead of the flock";
  - in open water: "flocks would move in double or treble file, occasionally probing with their bills. The rear segment of the flock would then sweep round to one side, and gradually move around and ahead of the leaders. The leaders would fan out in a line or arc, still oriented in the original direction of movement, at which point the breakaway section would turn to face them. As the groups moved together, both segments would commence probing and striking. In some cases the groups would disintegrate after a brief interval of striking, in others the whole group would reform and repeat the sequence." His figure labels this pattern for "large (usually > 20) groups".
- **Synchrony:** "in flocks of up to 10 birds initiation of striking by any member of the flock was followed in less than 3 sec by striking by the rest of the flock ... In flocks larger than 10 individuals coordination of striking within the entire flock decreased noticeably" [Anderson 1987, p. 30]. "once one bird begins to strike the other flock members have little choice but to join in" [p. 62].
- **Group size:** "Herding groups generally consisted of less than 10 birds", with flocks of up to 150 and 300 at times; "The tendency of large groups (>50 birds) to rapidly sub-divide and reform into local clusters precluded an accurate estimate of effective group size" [Anderson 1987, p. 23]. On the Dauphin River foraging flocks by day averaged 9.8 ± 25.5 birds in late spring (n = 95) and 3.8 ± 35.9 in late summer (n = 1,220); "Capture rates were highest for flocks of up to 100 foragers in the daytime" [McMahon & Evans 1992, Table 4 and abstract]. A flock there was "any group containing pelicans that were within approximately 5 m of each other" [p. 102].
- **The old accounts:** birds "form into a line in accordance with the sinuosities of the beach, each facing shoreward and awaiting their leader's signal to start. When this is given, all is commotion; the birds rapidly striking the water with their wings, throwing it high above them, and plunging their heads in and out, fairly make the water foam, as they move on in an almost unbroken line, filling their pouches as they go" [Goss 1888, in Bent 1922, pp. 288-289].
- **Modern summaries:** "Coordinated flocks of swimming birds encircle fish or drive them into the shallows where they become concentrated and can be more easily caught with synchronized bill dipping" [BoW-I]; "a flock will form a circle or semi-circle and, using coordinated bill dipping and wing beating, drive prey toward shore" [Boreal]; "groups of birds dip their bills and flap their wings to drive fish toward shore" [AAB-LH]; "foraging behavior tends to shift toward cooperation when prey aggregations are located" [Boreal].
- **Local enhancement:** "white pelicans make use of local enhancement, shifting positions to join currently successful foragers" [McMahon & Evans 1992, p. 108]. **Theft:** "They steal from other pelicans trying to swallow large fish and are successful about one-third of the time" [AAB-O]; it was rare on the Dauphin River, "occurring in only 8 of 469 (1.7%) flocks" [McMahon & Evans 1992, p. 104].

### 7.3 Video estimates

No source describes the dip's posture, the wings' part in it, or spacing in a foraging group in numbers, so these come from freely licensed video on Wikimedia Commons. A helper agent measured the clips on my instructions (frames extracted at 4-12 a second, tiled into time-labelled contact sheets and read by eye; camera pans removed by correlating the background); I checked its method and its report, not every frame. Times are good to about ±0.1-0.2 s and angles to about ±10°. **Everything in this section is an Estimate from video**; the birds are spring and autumn birds in Utah, California, Alberta and Wyoming, not late-September Oregon ones.

- The clips: [Schulenburg 2013] (about 12 birds in a tight clump, side-on, 19 October, California, 40.8 s); [Hannawacker 2017b] (8 birds seen from behind on open water, Utah, May, 35 s); [Hannawacker 2017a] (2 birds close and side-on, Utah, May, 29 s); [Synchronized Swimmers 2022] (8-9 birds in a river, from above, June, 23.6 s); [Wherry 2015] (a distant flock, some taking off, Florida, October); [Koerner 2017a, 2017b] and [Bohlke 2018] (4-5 birds standing in shallows).

**The dip.**
- **Bill starting down to entering the water:** median **0.9 s** (0.3-1.4, n = 8); the short ones are birds whose bill was already hanging from the last dip.
- **Bill in the water** (strike to clear, McMahon & Evans's dipping time): median **2.0 s** (1.5-2.7, n = 16 dips in four clips). The head itself was fully under for 1.0, 1.4, 1.5 and 1.9 s (n = 4). The published 1.3 ± 0.7 s is shorter than the video's median though within a standard deviation; the video's dips are mostly group dips.
- **Depth:** the usual dip takes the whole head and part or all of the neck under; some take only the bill and head, with the neck stretched forward just under the surface. No full upending; in the deepest (one dip) the body tips forward and the stern lifts.
- **Bill angle going in:** 23-30° below level with the neck reaching forward in three clips; 55-65°, straight down from the hanging position, in the fourth (the river group).
- **The wings lift.** During the dip the folded wings are raised in an arch over the back, not spread: all 8 birds in all 3 dips of [Hannawacker 2017b], from about 0.2 s after the bill enters until the head comes up; both birds in both dips of [Hannawacker 2017a]. The valley photos show the same (`amwpel-dip.jpg`, `amwpel-dip-wade.jpg`, and the juvenile `amwpel-juv-side.jpeg` just before a dip): on `amwpel-dip.jpg` the top of the raised wings stands about 225 mm above the water against 145 mm at rest (photo-measured, culmen scale from the neighbouring bird; low confidence).
- **Drain:** the bill comes up hanging, near vertical to about 60° below level, the pouch bulging, and is held **about 1.0 s** (0.6-1.8, n = 9).
- **Swallow, head up:** rare. None in 24 bird-dips of [Hannawacker 2017b], none in the two other clips; four in 40 s among about 12 birds in [Schulenburg 2013], each **0.5-0.7 s** with the bill 15-50° above level. That is of the order of the published one capture in nine dips.
- **Dip interval, same bird:** 10.4-11.0 s (5.6 a minute) in the line of eight; 2.65-3.6 s (17-20 a minute) in the two tight clumps; 21 s for the travelling pair. Anderson's ceiling of 6-8 a minute is exceeded threefold by the clumps over these short bouts.

**On the photos** (photo-measured): on `amwpel-dip.jpg` the left bird's bill goes in at 58° below level with about 43% of it under (the tip about 115 mm down); on `amwpel-dip-2.jpeg` two birds' bills enter side by side at about 70°. `amwpel-drain.jpeg` and `amwpel-drain-2.jpeg` show the drain: the bill 35-45° below level with its tip at the water and water or weed running from it.

**Synchrony and spacing.**
- **A line of eight** [Hannawacker 2017b]: line abreast, in two halves that turn inward and dip toward each other. First bill to last bill in: **1.0, 0.8, 0.7 s** (three dips); 8 of 8 dipped each time; heads up within about 0.5 s of each other. Neighbours about 0.9 body length apart, centre to centre (0.25-1.4).
- **A clump of eight or nine** [Synchronized Swimmers 2022]: bodies touching, all facing one way; first to last in **0.25-0.5 s**; 8 of 8 in three dips, 7 of 8 in the fourth. After four dips they stop and swim on as a wedge behind one bird.
- **A clump of about twelve** [Schulenburg 2013]: two or three ranks, bodies touching or overlapping, about a body length nose to tail within a rank; dipping in loose rolling waves rather than strict unison, a wave every **2.8 s** (median; 1.5-4.6, n = 12).
- **A pair in file** [Hannawacker 2017a]: the follower's bill 0.3 body length behind the leader's tail; the follower dips 0.5 s and under 0.2 s after the leader.
- **The valley photos** agree: foraging groups are tight clumps with bodies touching (62335329, 62232088, 247019746, 136532027, 81101509); a travelling group of five swims in a clump a body length across each (400053436); one flock of about fifty swims in a single long line, neighbours about one body length apart (`amwpel-forage-line.jpg`).

### 7.4 Time of day, and what they do on a September morning

- **Much foraging is at night in the breeding season.** "From two to three times as many pelicans foraged at night as in the daytime ... In contrast, more pelicans were present at adjacent loafing sites during the day" [McMahon & Evans 1992, abstract]. In late summer (23 August-5 September 1985) the river held by day 292.5 ± 252.7 foragers and **1,092.5 ± 561.1 loafers** per survey, by night 622.1 foragers and 986.7 loafers [Table 2]: by day about one bird in five was foraging (computed). Loafers there were birds "standing on a solid substrate, including exposed sandbars or in shallow water adjacent to shore" [p. 102].
- **Outside the breeding season they feed by day.** "They forage almost exclusively by day on their wintering grounds, but during breeding season, they commonly forage at night" [AAB-O]; Audubon's claim that they do not feed at night "referred specifically to the behavior of white pelicans on wintering grounds" [McMahon & Evans 1992, p. 107].
- **A winter day's budget:** pelicans in Louisiana and Mississippi "at catfish ponds spent about 4% of their day foraging and 96% loafing, whereas pelicans foraging in other habitats ... spent about 28% of their day foraging and 72% loafing" [King & Werner 2001, via ICWDM] (Snippet).
- **Loafing:** "Pelicans used low mud islands for loafing areas between feeding bouts" [Anderson 1987, p. 15]; after a drive they "wade and waddle into line again upon the beach, where they remain to rest, standing or sitting" [Goss 1888, in Bent 1922, p. 289]; "When gorged with food it dozes on the water" (Richardson, in [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 135]); birds "alight upon the water, where they often remain for hours" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 138].
- **Travel waits for the sun.** "flocks depart from and return to the colony during daylight hours, with peaks of travel near midday, when birds make use of thermal updrafts" [McMahon & Evans 1992, p. 101]; at Anaho Island arrivals and departures peaked "between 1100 and 1300 hrs" [Anderson 1987, abstract]. Yet Ridgway saw them leave at dawn: "In the morning, at sunrise, lines of these birds in hundreds could be observed flying from the island to their feeding-grounds ... in single lines" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 137]. A low flapping line in the morning is honest; a soaring kettle belongs to late morning and midday.
- **The valley photos** (section 3.2, any hour of day): 58 records on the water, 48 standing on logs, bars and shallows (preening in at least 9, asleep in at least 5), 37 flying. Of the birds on the water about a third of the records show bills down or heads under.
- **For the scene:** a late-September morning group mostly loafs: swimming slowly, preening, sleeping with the bill in the back, with a bout of group dipping now and then. **Estimate:** about a quarter of the time foraging (the day shares above are 4-28% in winter and about a fifth in late summer), the rest loafing and travelling.

### 7.5 Flight

**Posture and manner.**
- "The effort to rise seems labored at first, and is accomplished by rapid flappings, with a great swishing of powerful pinions beating the air; the great wings are thrown well forward at every stroke; the feet are dangling and the neck is only half extended. In a moment, as the bird gains headway, the feet are held out straight behind, the head is drawn back upon the shoulders, and the bird proceeds upon its way with slow majestic wing strokes. At intervals it sails for long distances on motionless, decurrent wings" [Bent 1922, p. 290].
- On the valley photos the head lies back on the shoulders with the bill resting on the folded foreneck, pointing about **25° below the line of flight** (`amwpel-above.jpg`); the orange feet are tucked under the tail, the toes reaching about to its tip, not trailing beyond it (`amwpel-above.jpg`, `amwpel-below-2.jpg`, `amwpel-below.jpg`); the tail is short, white and fanned a little.
- Landing: "spreading their wings and sliding onto the water on their feet" [ADW]; "water-ski landings" [AAB-LH]; `amwpel-land.jpg` shows it, the feet thrust forward with the webs spread.

**Wing shape and the black.** Photo-measured on `amwpel-below-2.jpg` (a bird directly overhead, wings spread; the silhouette cut out by threshold and measured along its long axis):
- **Bill tip to tail tip is 0.52 of the wingspan** (527 against 1,022 px).
- **Chord:** 0.145-0.155 of the span over the inner wing (0.3-0.5 of the way out), 0.10 at three-quarters out, 0.04-0.07 at the fingered tip; mean chord over the wing 0.121 of the span, so the **aspect ratio is about 8.3** (computed; the whole silhouette with body, head and tail gives span² / area = 7.0).
- **Wing area:** no published figure found. At a 2.67 m span and a 0.121 mean chord the area is **about 0.86 m²** including the body strip (computed), and the wing loading of a 5.7 kg bird **about 65 N/m²** (computed). Treat both as Estimates.
- **The wing is not fully stretched in flight.** The photo's span is 7.1 culmens, 2.25 m at a 315 mm culmen, 84% of the published 2.67 m, which is a stretched measurement (computed; the bird's sex is unknown). Build the spread wing to the published span and expect it to be carried a little flexed.
- **From below:** every primary black, the fingers (five or six show separately at the tip) wholly so; the secondaries black for the rear **0.4-0.45 of the chord**, their white bases showing as pale lines running into the black; the black runs inward along the trailing edge to within **about 0.15 of the half-span from the centreline**, that is, nearly to the body, the innermost few feathers white. On the hand the black takes the rear 0.7-0.8 of the chord. All the under wing-coverts, the body and the tail white. Over the whole silhouette about 35% of the area is dark (computed from the cut-out).
- **From above** (`amwpel-above.jpg`, `amwpel-land.jpg`): the primaries and the greater primary coverts black, the feather shafts white; the alula black ("highly conspicuous on the white forewing" [BoW-A] (Snippet)); the secondaries black-grey with ashy-white edges, the black narrowing inward to a line and gone at the innermost feathers; every other covert, the back and the tail white.

**Wingbeat.**
- "Wingbeat frequency is stable at about 160-175 beats/min" [BoW-B] (Snippet): **2.7-2.9 Hz** (computed).
- On video two birds climbing just after take-off beat at **3.0 Hz** (8 beats in 2.69 s; 3 in 1.0 s) [Wherry 2015] (Video).
- Pennycuick's formula for cruising flight [Pennycuick 1996; the formula itself was not visible in the abstract I could read and is quoted from memory, so this is a cross-check only]: frequency = m^(3/8) g^(1/2) b^(−23/24) S^(−1/3) ρ^(−3/8), which for 5.7 kg, 2.67 m, 0.86 m² and sea-level air gives **about 2.3 Hz** (computed).
- **The game's 1.6 Hz is too slow.** Use **2.7 Hz** while flapping.

**Flap and glide; the wave down the line.**
- "their manner of flight being a succession of slow, regular flappings of the wings, which at intervals are extended to their full length, the birds sailing thus for a few rods, and the flapping being then resumed. The flock preserves the utmost method and order in its flight. The leader is always the first to extend or flap his wings, and is followed in these movements by each one in the line in succession" [BBR 1884, vol. II, pp. 137-138]. "flapping and sailing alternately, and as uniformly as if by word of command" [p. 136].
- "Each bird seems to take its cue from the one in front of it, beginning to flap and starting a glide when its predecessor does" [Boreal].
- Birds of the World, after O'Malley & Evans 1982: "the lead bird usually initiates phase shift between flapping and gliding, then the phase shift passes back through the flock in a wave, with response time usually less than 0.5 seconds between successive birds"; "alternate flapping (60%-75%) and gliding flight, usually at moderate height of 12-15 m"; "Percent time flapping and wingbeats/hour tend to decrease behind leader"; "Birds in line sometimes rise and fall in succession creating a wave-like effect"; "Flocks observed near the colonies were characterized by low, horizontal, flap-gliding flight, and further enroute they flew higher and showed greater frequencies of gliding and ascending flight associated with increasing use of thermals" [BoW-B] (Snippet).
- For the Great White Pelican (*P. onocrotalus*), a close relative of the same size: "the typical flight pattern of pelicans in formation—a few wing-beats, followed by a short, 1-2-second glide—in which 42.2% of the time is spent flapping" [Weimerskirch et al. 2001] (Snippet); the abstract I could read says only that birds trained to fly in a V "save a significant amount of energy by flying in formation".
- A rod is 5 m: "a few rods" of sailing at 11-14 m/s is a glide of 1-2 s (computed), which agrees.
- No clip showed a flap-glide cycle, so the bout lengths in section 11 are built from these figures.

**Formation.**
- "It flies low and heavily, usually in flocks of from six to fourteen, sometimes abreast, at other times in an oblique line" (Richardson, in [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 135]); "in single lines, one behind the other" [p. 137]; "forming a wedge-shaped column, with skirmishers on their flanks" [p. 136]. "Formation flocks typically form long lines, often with apex forming Vee or Jay (unequal length of arms), also in irregular patterns" [BoW-B] (Snippet). "often flying in the familiar V-formation" [Boreal].
- **Spacing:** "Distance between aerial flock members variable, ranging down to 1-2 m when in formations" [BoW-B] (Snippet). Photo-measured, centre to centre in the picture plane, in units of the birds' own spread wingspan: **0.6-0.8 spans** between neighbours in a five-bird echelon seen from below with the wings overlapping in the picture (`amwpel-line-2.jpg`: 266-368 px against spans of 450-465 px); **1.05-1.75 spans** in a five-bird line seen from further off, the gaps widening toward the rear (`amwpel-line.jpg`: 294-492 px against about 280 px). Both lines slant: each bird sits behind and to one side of the one ahead.
- **Flock size:** at Anaho Island 88% of 2,289 departing flocks held 20 or fewer birds; the mean departing flock was 11.1 (SD 24.0), low departures in April-May averaging 5.3-6.3 and high (thermal) ones 12.9-18.7 [Anderson 1987, pp. 40-42, Table 4]. In the valley's flying records the median photo holds 4.5 birds, a quarter hold 12 or more, and the soaring flocks 25-60 (section 3.2).
- **Height:** low lines "at moderate height of 12-15 m" [BoW-B] (Snippet); soaring birds far higher: "pelican soaring flight is confined to the thermal layer", cross-country birds flying "mainly within the middle of the thermal layer" and those wandering near foraging sites in its lower part [Shannon et al. 2002, abstract]; "apparently a mile above the lake" (Cooper, in [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 136]). The game's 40-80 m lane sits between the two and is fair for a flock crossing a valley lake.
- **Speed:** tracked migrants averaged **39.5 km/h in spring and 33.2 km/h in autumn** over hourly steps, 11.0 and 9.2 m/s (computed), circling in thermals included, so straight flight is faster [Illán et al. 2017]. No source I could read gives an airspeed for a flapping line. **Estimate: 12-14 m/s**; the game's 14 m/s can stand.
- **Soaring:** "this bird soars much more than the Brown species ... mounting in gyrations until almost out of sight" (Salvin, in [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 136]); "At destination, they set wings and circle downward without flapping, often from great heights" [BoW-B] (Snippet). Not needed this round.

### 7.6 Sounds

- "White pelicans are particularly silent birds; the only notes that I have heard them utter are the low-toned grunts or subdued croaking notes heard on their breeding grounds and not audible at any great distance" [Bent 1922, pp. 290-291]. "Adults are usually silent. In aggressive and sexual encounters at the colony site, they emit frequent low, brief grunts" [AAB-S]. "Usually silent; grunts or croaks on nesting grounds" [Boreal]. "Adults are usually silent" [BirdWeb].
- A flock on migration "occasionally uttering a croaking sound" (Cooper, in [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 136]) is the one record of a call away from a colony.
- What a watcher does hear is the wings: a flock rising made "a sound like that of the distant surf"; passing overhead, "the motion of their wings sounding like the rush of a meteor through the air" (Cooper, in [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 136]); and the water: "wings splashing water during bathing and when herding prey" [search summary of BoW, Sounds] (Snippet).
- **So the game's call every 200-600 s is not honest.** Recommend no voice for the pelican, on the water or in the sky. If a sound is wanted, the honest ones are the splash and wing-slap of a driving group and the rush of wings as a line passes low.

## 12. Open questions and confidence

**High confidence**
- The adult's late-September dress: white, no horn, no crest; black primaries and outer secondaries; bill and feet yellow to orange. Three old authorities, the modern guides and 72 photographed adults agree.
- The young bird's brown-centred lesser coverts and greyish crown [BBR 1884; Ridgway 1887], confirmed on 13 valley birds.
- No plunge-diving; bill-dipping from the surface; cooperative driving in lines and arcs; synchronised dipping.
- The dip's timing [McMahon & Evans 1992, Table 1, read in full] and the dip rate against group size [Anderson 1987, read in full].
- Flap-and-glide lines with the flap passing from the leader back, described alike in 1884 and in the modern account.
- Adults are silent away from colonies.

**Medium confidence**
- **Linear measurements.** The sexed means are a Snippet; BBR and AVONET, which I read, agree with their mean within 2-5%.
- **Heights on the water** (back 0.145 m, crown 0.29 m): one strictly side-on bird, supported by two others; every length scaled by a culmen whose true length is known only to ±10%.
- **The wing's black pattern and planform:** one overhead photo measured, four others looked at.
- **Ages in the tally:** 93 birds classed by eye on small photos; whether the eight grey-naped birds are adults or young is not settled.
- **Wingbeat 2.7-2.9 Hz:** a Snippet, a two-bird video in a climb, and a formula quoted from memory bracket it at 2.3-3.0.

**Low confidence or not verified**
- **Model length.** 0.85 m on the traced bird; a second bird reads 1.07 m by its culmen. The sexes' bills differ by a fifth, so a mean-of-the-sexes body with a 315 mm bill is somewhere in 0.85-0.95 m.
- **Draft (30% of body depth)** and **body width (0.32 m):** computed from mass and one rear view.
- **Bill width along its length:** only the base is measured.
- **Wing area and aspect ratio:** from one photo; no published value found (Pennycuick's tables were not readable).
- **Flight speed** of a flapping line; **flap and glide bout lengths**.
- **All video numbers:** one to twelve birds a clip, none from Oregon in September, measured by a helper agent; the fast dip runs (every 3 s) come from two clumps over 13-40 s.
- **The time budget** for a late-September morning.
- **Status numbers** for the valley: blog posts and one newspaper piece; no eBird counts.

**Could not source at all**
- A published swimming speed, wake, or waterline for any pelican.
- Published spacing between birds in a foraging line or a swimming group.
- A published airspeed for the species, and a published wing area.
- When, to the week, adults lose the grey nape patch.
- Whether the valley's late-September birds feed mainly by day or by night.

**Sources I could not read** (not relied on for any number except where marked Snippet): the Birds of the World account behind its paywall (only the introduction is open); Palmer 1962; Pyle 2008; Dunning 2008; Dorr et al. 2005; Anderson 1991 (the journal paper; I read the 1987 dissertation it came from); O'Malley & Evans 1982a, 1982b (the publisher's page refused me); King & Werner 2001; Pennycuick 1983, 1990 and 1996 beyond the 1996 abstract; Weimerskirch et al. 2001 beyond the abstract; Marshall et al. 2003; eBird.

**Worth doing if higher confidence is needed**
- A side-on photo of a swimming adult of known sex, or with a ruler-scaled object beside it, to fix the body's size against the bill.
- A top view of the bill for its width along the length.
- Timing a flap-glide line from fixed-camera video: beats per bout, glide length, the lag between birds.
- eBird's September counts for Fern Ridge, Finley and Sauvie Island.
