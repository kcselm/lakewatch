# Great Blue Heron: measurements, late-September plumage, field marks, standing posture and neck shapes, and foraging

Date: 2026-09-27
Purpose: source data for the detailed Great Blue Heron (`grbher3`) model and its settings in the `heron` foraging style on `wade`. The cast stands in water on a shallow shelf along the far waterline of Willow Lake, 60-66 m from the vantage, seen through binoculars (7.5°) or, mostly, the telescope (2°: about 2 mm per pixel at 63 m on a 1080-pixel-tall view) on a Willamette Valley morning, about 20-30 September. The shelf slopes from about 5 cm deep at the shore to about 30 cm; nothing disturbs the birds, and they do not fly.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt of a paywalled page; I could not read the page, so treat it as unverified. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo and scaled it by the exposed culmen (or, where stated, by another part). "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements** (mean of the sexes, the valley's west-coast birds; mm): exposed culmen **135**, bill depth at the feathering **27.5**, width **17** (Estimate), tarsus **165**, bare tibia **105**, middle toe **102**, wing chord **477**, tail **184**; mass about 2.3 kg (section 2.8). Midpoints of Dickerman's two nearest populations (California and the western-Washington intergrades) and Oberholser's two Oregon skins.
- **Model length** (bill tip to tail tip in the traced posture) is not a published number: **about 0.93 m** on the side-on hunting bird of section 8 (photo-measured), 0.78 m on an upright one; stretched skins run 1.07-1.27 m.
- **Not *fannini*.** The valley's herons are *herodias* or the pale western *wardi* ("*hyperonca*"): paint the ordinary mid blue-grey, not the dark coastal race (section 3.3).
- **Two versions: `adult` and `juvenile`.** Of 120 records sampled from the valley between 11 September and 12 October, 37 could be aged: **22 adults** (second-years among them) and **15 juveniles**, about 60:40 (section 3.2). Cast **one of each**.
- **The adult** in September: white forehead and centre of the crown between broad black stripes that run back into black occipital plumes (short in autumn: they grow from August to February [Pyle & Howell 2004]), white face and throat, grey neck with a black-and-white streaked foreneck, blue-grey back and wings, black "shoulder" patch at the bend of the wing, rusty thighs; bill dusky above and yellowish below after breeding [BBR 1884, p. 16].
- **The juvenile:** the whole crown dark slate, no plumes, no shoulder patch, duller brownish-grey upperparts with rufous edges to the wing coverts, a greyer neck streaked buff and chestnut, and a darker, duskier bill [Bent 1926, p. 108; Oberholser 1912b, p. 534].
- **The neck** has 17 cervical vertebrae by Fleming & Black's count and a kink at the C6-C7 joint, **about a third of the neck down from the head** on the photos (sections 2.7, 6). Its visible length is about 0.33 m on an upright bird.
- **Neck shapes, photo-measured** (crown above the top of the back or shoulders): **hunch about 30 mm**, the bill 20° down; **hunting S about 210 mm**, the bill 21-26° down; **upright about 330 mm**, the bill 10° down; **erect about 455 mm**, the bill level; **strike**: the bill **70-90° down**, the neck looped in front of the breast, the body tipped forward about 25°; **swallow**: the head tipped up (no photo; an Estimate from the egret's 20-25° and the sources).
- **Standing height**, feet to crown: about **0.73 m** hunting, **0.79 m** upright, **1.0 m** erect.
- **How deep it wades:** most often **15-25 cm** (commonest 18 cm) in New Jersey [Willard 1977, Fig. 4], "usually ≤ 50 cm" [Short & Cooper 1985, p. 1]; its whole leg is 39 cm [Powell 1987]. On the valley photos 12 cm to belly-deep (about 27 cm).
- **Foraging:** upright stand-and-wait and slow wading, "more slowly than any other species" [Willard 1977]; **0.2-0.3 strikes a minute** and **about 40% success** for an adult on a lake [Willard 1977; Bovino & Burtt 1979]; juveniles catch about half as much, through lower success [Butler 1995; Quinney & Smith 1980].
- **Spacing:** an individual distance, defended when another heron comes too close [Grau & Parris 1980]; clumped where food is dense [Butler 1995]; indifferent to egrets, which feed within 1-3 m of it on the valley's photos (section 3.2). Settings: **10 m** from another Great Blue Heron, **3 m** from an egret.

## 2. Measurements

The primary tables I could read are Baird, Brewer & Ridgway's *Water Birds of North America* (1884; 17 adult skins, extremes only), Oberholser's 1912 revision of the species (sexed means by race, and every specimen listed, including two from Oregon), Dickerman's 2004 review (214 males and 189 females, sexed means by population), Butler's 47 dead herons from the Fraser River valley (1995), and AVONET's four skins. **Ridgway's *Birds of North and Middle America* never reached the herons** (Bull. U.S. Natl. Mus. 50 ended with Part XI, Friedmann 1950, without the Ardeidae). Bent 1926 gives egg measurements only. Palmer 1962, Hancock & Kushlan 1984, Pyle 2008 and the Birds of the World measurement appendix (Vennesland & Butler 2011) could not be read.

### 2.7 The neck: cervical vertebrae and the kink

- **Count and regions.** In the CT and laser-scan survey of pelecanimorph necks by Fleming & Black (2026, Fig. 2), *Ardea herodias* has vertebrae C2-C17 scored after the atlas (C1), "cervical vertebrae [being] defined as those without articulated ribs": so 17 cervical vertebrae by that definition. The regions: Region 1 is C2, Region 2 is C3-C5, **Region 3 is a single vertebra, C6**, Region 4 is C7-C14 (eight vertebrae, more than in any other group sampled), and Region 5 is C15-C17. "All members of Ardeidae had only one vertebra in Region 3" and "Region 4 exhibited substantial variation, with 2 vertebrae in frigatebirds (Fregatidae) and up to 8 vertebrae in Ardeidae" [Fleming & Black 2026, Results]. Counts that include the last cervicals with floating ribs come out higher (19-20 are often quoted for herons; I found no primary count for this species under that definition).
- **The kink.** "A pervasive—yet untested—hypothesis is that a single mid-cervical vertebra, C6 in ardeids and C8 in anhingas, plays a crucial role" [Fleming & Black 2026, Introduction, citing Dönitz 1873, Forbes 1882, Böker 1937, Král 1965]. "Boas (1929) and Král (1965) documented that a strong dorsal elastic ligament (ligamentum elasticum interlaminare), present along most of the avian neck, is interrupted in ardeids between C6 and C7, a modification interpreted as permitting greater angular displacement" [Discussion]. In ardeids "cervical Region 3 is reduced, producing an abrupt transition between Regions 2 and 4. Extra flexion may be facilitated at regional boundaries" [Discussion]. *A. herodias* sits at the "elongate end" of the vertebral-length axis in Regions 1-4, with some of the longest Region 2 vertebrae among the ardeids [Results].
- **So the kink is the C6-C7 joint**, between the short upper neck (C1-C6: the head end) and the long lower neck (C7-C17). The rig's kink joint belongs there. Section 6 gives where it falls along the traced neck.

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

**Three age classes can be told apart in the field in autumn.**
- "Some markings to look for in the field are the white crown, long body plumes, grey-blue wing coverts, and black 'shoulder patch' worn by adults (>24 months old). Juveniles (<12 months old) have grey crowns, no body plumes or shoulder patches, and chestnut-coloured edging to wing coverts. Yearling herons (12-24 months old) wear an intermediate plumage in which the crown stripe is small, body plumes are short, a few coverts are edged with chestnut-brown, and a small shoulder patch is present. Juveniles can be easily distinguished in the field from other age-classes, often without the assistance of binoculars or telescope. Yearlings are more difficult to separate from adults than from juveniles" [Butler 1995, p. 19].
- **Juveniles wear their first plumage all autumn.** "The above plumage is worn through the first fall and winter without much change before February, when the first spring plumage begins to show advance toward maturity; at this season one or two occipital plumes may appear, but the crown remains black" [Bent 1926, p. 108]. Pyle & Howell found active preformative moult in first-year birds from 14 September to 24 February [Pyle & Howell 2004, p. 290], so a late-September juvenile is at most starting it.
- **Second-year birds are near-adult by late September.** "At the first postnuptial molt, which is complete, the following summer and fall, further advance toward maturity is made; the forehead becomes partially white, some occipital plumes are acquired; the black shoulder tufts appear, somewhat mixed with white; many long, narrow plume-like feathers appear in the back and breast; the thighs are purer cinnamon ... After the next complete postnuptial molt, when the young bird is over two years old, the plumage becomes fully adult, though signs of immaturity are still to be seen, such as dusky markings in the white crown and white markings in the black shoulder tufts" [Bent 1926, p. 108]. BBR's "younger specimens (probably in the second year) ... have the forehead dusky-slate, there being only a few white feathers in the crown; the cheeks strongly tinged with buff" [BBR 1884, p. 16].
- **Adults are in their annual moult, and their plumes are at their shortest.** "Among adults, active prebasic molt occurred in birds collected from 15 June to 23 November (n = 77)" [Pyle & Howell 2004, p. 290]. Ornamental plumes "emerge during the later stages of the definitive prebasic molt in August-November, grow slowly through January-February, and are retained until replacement during the following prebasic molt" [p. 287]; "the ornamental plumes of adult Great Blue ... herons emerged in September or October ... and continued to grow through January or February"; "plume length of adults appeared to shorten during June-October, presumably due to wear" [p. 290]. Section 5.1 reads their Fig. 1 for lengths.
- **The bill and bare parts after breeding.** "After the young are hatched, these plumes are dropped, the bare skin around the eye has changed to a yellowish-green hue, and the upper mandible become almost wholly dusky blackish-olive, with only the tomia and lower mandible yellowish" [BBR 1884, p. 16]; the same in [Oberholser 1912b, p. 533].
- **Adults and juveniles use the same places in September.** On the Fraser River delta "most juvenile and adult herons foraged on beaches from March to October and in marshlands and grasslands from November to February" [Butler 1995, p. 3]; more juveniles than adults used grasslands and ditches in winter [p. 20].

### 3.3 Subspecies: the valley's birds are not *fannini*

Every treatment I could read puts the Willamette Valley outside the dark Pacific race.

- **Oberholser 1912** named the large California birds *hyperonca*, "like *Ardea herodias herodias* in color, but in size decidedly greater throughout", ranging "north to western Oregon" [Oberholser 1912b, pp. 550-551]. "There does not seem to be any constant difference in color between *Ardea herodias hyperonca* and *Ardea herodias herodias*" [p. 551]. His two "Oregon" skins "must be referred to the present form, although rather darker above than birds from northern California. They are somewhat vergent toward *Ardea herodias fannini*, and probably came from near the mouth of the Columbia River ... since all the great blue herons we have seen from Washington are *Ardea herodias fannini*" [p. 552]. *Fannini* is "similar to *Ardea herodias hyperonca*, but wing, exposed culmen, and middle toe shorter, the tarsus still more so; tail longer; upper parts and neck darker", south only to "Cape Flattery, northwestern Washington; and the Nisqually Flats" [p. 554]. Bent 1926 follows him: the "California heron" reaches "north to western Oregon", with egg dates from "California and Oregon" [Bent 1926, p. 130]; the "northwest coast heron" ranges "from northwestern Washington to the Alaskan Peninsula" [p. 117].
- **Payne 1979** recognised only three North American races. *Fannini*: "Pacific coast of North America from southeastern Alaska (Yakutat Bay) south through Queen Charlotte Islands and coastal British Columbia to coastal Washington. Wanders inland." *Herodias*, with *hyperonca*, *treganzai*, *wardi* and others as synonyms, breeds from "Alberta and Washington east of the Cascades ... south through Oregon, California" [Payne 1979, pp. 198-199]. "Southern birds average larger than northern birds, but the eastern variation in size is clinal, and no subspecies need be recognized in continental North America except in the Pacific northwest and in Florida"; "Western birds have pale necks" [p. 198, footnotes].
- **Dickerman 2004** measured 403 adults and compared unwashed skins side by side. He keeps *herodias* ("moderately gray"), *wardi* ("paler", sinking *treganzai*, *hyperonca* and *sanctilucae* into it), and *fannini* ("darker gray"), and restricts *fannini* to "the Queen Charlotte Islands north to Prince William Sound" [Dickerman 2004a, p. 244]. "I know of no specimens of *A. h. fannini* (as here defined) from south of the Queen Charlotte Islands. *A. h. fannini* intergrades southward with *A. h.* '*hyperonca*' and perhaps *A. h.* '*treganzai*' ... in southwestern British Columbia (including Vancouver Island) and western Washington ... that population is paler gray as in *A. h. wardi* (sensu latu), but it is closer to *A. h. fannini* in the shorter exposed culmen, tarsus, and male wing chord" [pp. 248-249]. The *hyperonca* type "is somewhat intermediate towards *A. h. fannini* and indeed inseparable from *A. h. herodias* in color" [p. 248]. The famous darkness of *fannini* is partly an artefact: "the slaty-black coloration of the holotype (Chapman 1901) is abnormally dark, apparently due to washing that removed the powder down coating and thus the paler bloom of the plumage" [p. 244].
- **Canadian and Washington agencies** give *fannini*'s southern limit as Puget Sound: "confined to the Pacific Coast from Prince William Sound, Alaska south to Puget Sound, Washington"; it is "darker plumaged, smaller in size" than continental birds [COSEWIC 2008, chs. 2 and 5]; it "differs from inland herons and from herons near south-coastal Washington (*A. h. herodias*) in that they are smaller in size" [Azerrad 2012, p. 1].
- **Genetics.** "Genetic characterization of Great Blue Herons in North America has not been conducted" [COSEWIC 2008, ch. 5]. The one range-wide microsatellite study since then found that the Florida Bay herons "form a group distinct from blue forms on Florida Peninsula and elsewhere in North America" [McGuire et al. 2019, abstract]; a search excerpt of the same paper says its dark group took in *fannini*, *herodias* and *wardi* together (Snippet). It did not sample Oregon.
- iNaturalist: no research-grade record in the box is identified to *fannini* (and 2 to "*wardi*"); these are observers' labels, not evidence.

**What this means for the model.** The valley's herons are *herodias* (Payne) or the pale western *wardi* / "*hyperonca*" (Dickerman, Oberholser), sitting in the west-coast cline between the long-billed California birds and the intergrades of western Washington. They are **not the dark, short-legged coastal *fannini***. Paint them the ordinary mid blue-grey of the species (Oberholser's *hyperonca*: "neck all around very deep drab gray", upper surface "slate-gray" [Oberholser 1912b, pp. 550-551]), with the pale bloom of powder down, not the slaty back and dusky neck of a *fannini*; and give them the long bill and legs of the middle of the cline (section 2.8), not *fannini*'s short tarsus.

### 3.4 Recommendation

- **Two versions:** `adult` and `juvenile`. The second-year birds are adult-like enough by late September (section 3.1) to paint as adults.
- **Cast: one adult and one juvenile.** The tally's 60:40 rounds to one of each for two birds, and the pair shows the ageing marks side by side.
- **The adult's plumes** are short in September: grown in the late prebasic moult of August-November and still growing through the winter [Pyle & Howell 2004, p. 290]. A plume part a third to half of the breeding length reads on the photos (section 5).

## 5. Structure

### 5.1 Head and bill

- **Bill:** a long, deep, straight dagger: "stout, deep, straight" in the genus [BBR 1884, p. 4]; depth 27.5 mm at the base falling to about 60% at mid-length; the culmen almost straight, curving down a little near the tip (photo).
- **The forehead feathering** meets the culmen close to the eye: on `grbher3-adult-eugene.jpg` the culmen (215 px, 0.63 mm/px) is **0.953 of the distance from the eye's centre to the bill tip** (computed), which scales the other photos (section 6). The bare lores are short.
- **The eye:** about 10-11 mm across as it shows (photo), so a model eye radius of **about 5 mm**, high on the head, just above the gape line; iris yellow.
- **Occipital plumes (the adult's version-only part):** two or three black plumes trailing back from the nape's black stripes. In breeding dress they reach about 200 mm; in September they are short, regrowing [Pyle & Howell 2004]: **about 80-120 mm** on the valley's autumn adults (photo, `grbher3-adult-plumes.jpg`, `grbher3-hunch.jpeg`), hanging back and down at **about 15-30° below the horizontal** with the head level (photo).

### 5.2 Neck, body, wing, tail, legs

- **Neck:** long, thicker than the egret's; the lower neck's shaggy plumes hang over the breast; the kink is about a third of the neck down from the head (section 6).
- **Body:** deep and narrow; in the hunting S the back runs level or down to the tail, and upright or at rest the body tilts up (section 6).
- **Folded wing:** covers the side of the body from the shoulder to the tail tip; long pale back plumes lie over it in the adult.
- **Tail:** short and square (184 mm), mostly under the wings.
- **Legs and feet:** long; the feathered rusty thigh hangs over the top of the bare tibia; long toes.

## 7. Foraging behaviour

### 7.1 What the sources say

**How it hunts.**
- "In *stand and wait* ... a heron stands motionless in water or on land waiting for prey to approach. There are two basic postures. In *upright* posture the body is held erect, head and neck are fully extended angled away from the body. In *crouched* posture, the body is held horizontal to the perch or the water, legs are bent, and the head and neck are partially retracted. Upright stand and wait is epitomized by the Great Blue Heron" [Kushlan 1976a, p. 87]. In *walk slowly* "a heron moves slowly, stalking prey. Walking becomes slower as the heron examines items or areas of interest, and just before striking the heron may walk so slowly that this behavior merges with stand and wait. Alternately herons feeding by stand and wait may periodically walk very slowly for several steps and then resume stand and wait. Merging of the two behaviors is best seen in species such as the Great Blue Heron" [p. 87]. "The largest, the Great Blue, and the smallest, the Green Heron, have the fewest feeding behaviors attributed to them. Both rely primarily on stand or stalk techniques" [p. 90].
- New Jersey, four years, 1,380 hours: "These herons used upright stand-and-wait and slow-wading equally often. They waded more slowly than any other species and often stopped for long periods before striking or moving on. They usually did not retrace their steps ... Active pursuit was infrequent and rarely successful" [Willard 1977, p. 465]. "The very long periods of standing motionless that are typical of the Great Blue Heron should facilitate the finding and capture of many of its large prey"; "When a Great Blue Heron took bottom-dwelling sedentary fish such as flounders and sculpins, the strike was always preceded by regular slow wading" [p. 468].
- "It fishes by night as well as by day and employs two very different methods, still hunting and stalking ... Standing as still as a graven image in shallow water, where fish are moving about, it waits patiently until one comes within reach, when a swift and unerring stroke of its well trained bill either kills or secures the fish. Usually the fish is seized crosswise between the mandibles; if it is a small one, it is tossed in the air and swallowed head first ... Slowly and carefully, with stately tread, it walks along in water knee deep, its long neck stretched upward and forward; its keen eyes are scanning the surface and an occasional quick turn of the head indicates a glimpse of a fish; suddenly it stops ... at last comes its chance, as in a crouching attitude the long neck darts downward, quick as a flash" [Bent 1926, pp. 108-109].
- British Columbia: "The heron catches fish by slowly wading in the shallows and standing motionless for many minutes until prey comes within reach of its formidable bill. With lightning speed, its neck uncoils, and a fish is caught between its mandibles. The wriggling fish is tossed into position and swallowed whole before the heron returns to stalking another fish" [Butler 1995, p. 9].
- Tennessee, winter mornings on a lake inlet: "When foraging, they used the upright stand-and-wait posture (Kushlan 1976) under all weather conditions" [Bovino & Burtt 1979, p. 628]. "A Great Blue Heron must strike within reach; hence, it searches a small area only 0.5-1.0 m from its eyes" [p. 629].
- Grasslands: in California, "it is no uncommon sight to see a heron standing motionless for hours at a time in an alfalfa field waiting for a gopher to make its appearance" (Tyler 1913, in [Bent 1926, p. 129]); on the Fraser delta, "the average heron required about 160 minutes to catch a vole (12 voles caught in 1938 minutes)" [Butler 1995, p. 16]. In Washington "voles making up a major portion of their winter diet" [BirdWeb]. The Willow Lake birds stand in water, so voles are out of scope.

- Butler's intertidal fish runs are unusually rich (a breeding heron strikes "about 100 times each day during the summer" [p. 9]); on a lake the Willard and Bovino & Burtt rates, **about 0.2-0.3 strikes a minute and about 40% success for an adult**, are the better guide. "Juveniles caught fish at about half the adult rate" [Butler 1995, p. 17], mainly through lower success, since pace and strike rate are similar [Quinney & Smith 1980, abstract].
- Weather matters: strikes were more frequent and more successful under overcast skies and on calm water [Bovino & Burtt 1979, pp. 628-629]. A late-September Willamette morning is often overcast or foggy and calm.

**How deep it wades.**
- New Jersey: "I estimated hunting depth by how far the water was above or below the foot, ankle or leg feathers, and later converted this to actual depth by comparison with leg measurements of heron specimens" [Willard 1977, p. 462]. His Fig. 4 (read by me on the page scan; dot sizes are frequency classes): in every month most Great Blue Herons hunted in **6-10 inches (15-25 cm)**, the commonest depth 7 inches (18 cm), with a few from 0 to 5 inches and at 11-13 inches; in September (n = 46) 0 inches (11-20% of birds), 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10 inches (11-20% each), 4 and 11 inches (6-10% each) [Willard 1977, p. 464, Fig. 4]. "Great Blue Herons and Great Egrets hunted in similar depths, and both hunted in deeper water than the small species" [p. 464].
- Florida Bay: "Custer and Osborn (1978b) demonstrated that the water depth used by wading birds was dependent on leg length. To allow for maximum foraging access ... I used total leg length as the maximum foraging depth"; Great Blue/White Heron **39 ± 2.1 cm** (Great Egret 28 ± 1.6) from study skins [Powell 1987, p. 742, Table 1]. That is a ceiling, reached only with the water at the belly.
- Florida experimental ponds at 10, 19 and 28 cm: the Great Blue Heron was one of five species that used the deepest ponds and "showed a decidedly weaker response to one or the other component of prey availability" than the shallow-water specialists [Gawlik 2002, abstract].
- Belly-deep birds happen: on the British Columbia coast "it stands belly deep moving slowly, or not at all" over eelgrass (Brooks, in [Bent 1926, p. 117]); "it walks along in water knee deep" [Bent 1926, p. 109]; "the bird will stand by the hour on a sinking raft of kelp fronds, though it leave him submerged to the belly" (Dawson, in [Bent 1926, p. 129]). In Colorado, wetland herons stood "ankle-deep, peering into the water for prey" [Magee & Zareba 2019, p. 21].
- "Stands motionless, or walks slowly, when searching for prey in shallow water (less than 30 cm; 12 in)" [CWHR, Granholm], a secondary summary.
- "Great blue herons ... primarily [catch] fish in shallow water, usually ≤ 50 cm deep (Bent 1926; Meyerriecks 1960; Bayer 1978)"; "The usual foraging behavior of great blue herons is successful only in shallow (up to 0.5 m deep), clear water with a firm substrate" [Short & Cooper 1985, pp. 1, 5].
- Section 6 adds the depths on the valley photos.

**Spacing, territories and company.**
- **Individual feeding territories, sometimes.** "Great Blue Herons, for example, in different situations can hold permanent territories (Bayer 1978), switch between intertidal and terrestrial foraging (Krebs 1974), or show temporal variability in feeding sites" [Kushlan 1981, pp. 153-154]. On the Fraser River "all 38 territorial herons along riverbanks ... were adults"; densities were **1.30 territorial herons per km of river-edge marsh**, 0.41 per km of forest edge and 0.10 per km of industrial or farmed bank [Butler 1995, pp. 21-22, Table 12]: roughly one heron per 770 m of the best bank (computed). "Nine territories that could be viewed both day and night were occupied by solitary herons every month of the year" [p. 22]. Butler dropped bank segments "less than 200 m long ... unlikely to be large enough to hold a heron territory (Bayer 1978)" [p. 20].
- **Individual distance, more often.** In the Lake Erie marshes, "the Great Blue Herons ... did not show evidence of being territorial at the feeding site ... The aggression shown at the feeding site was apparently released by violations of individual distance rather than crossing fixed boundaries" [Grau & Parris 1980, p. 48]; "aggressive interactions at the foraging site usually resulted from violations of individual distance when one heron strayed too close to another foraging heron or when one heron approached a heron handling prey"; chases followed 86% of 35 displays; interaction rates rose with the number of herons within 50 m (their "group size" [p. 39]), from 0 for a lone bird to 0.048 per minute with more than seven [pp. 46-47, Table 13]. "Loafing herons did not have aggressive interactions even though the herons were close together" [p. 46].
- **Loose flocks where food is dense.** On the Fraser delta foraging herons were clumped (aggregation index 0.53-0.98 on 30 September to 12 November) [Butler 1995, p. 22, Table 10]; in flocks after shiner perch "displaced birds flew a few metres away and resumed feeding about 30 seconds later"; "interference by conspecifics maintains individual foraging distances" [p. 24]. "It feeds solitarily when prey density is low but in aggregations when prey density is high" [Kushlan 1976b, p. 471]. In a Florida pond "all species except White Ibis, Wood Storks, and Little Blue Herons maintained both inter- and intraspecific feeding territories, i.e. individual distances, while feeding in the aggregation. Generally larger birds predominated over smaller ones. Encroachment from the air by similarly sized or larger herons was successful; encroachment on the ground by a similarly sized heron was thwarted" [Kushlan 1976b, pp. 470-472].
- **Toward other herons.** "The attitude of the great blue heron towards other species of herons with which it is associated on its breeding grounds or its feeding grounds is usually one of dignified indifference or haughty disdain. It never seems to molest the smaller herons" [Bent 1926, p. 111]. In New Jersey Great Blue Herons were in 30% of mixed feeding flocks and sometimes at their centre with Great Egrets [Willard 1977, pp. 467-468, Table 6].
- Solitary on a lake shore: in Tennessee "herons foraged solitarily along the inlet's uninhabited southern shore, arriving soon after sunrise and departing 30-90 min later" [Bovino & Burtt 1979, p. 628].

**Time budget.** No autumn time budget for a lake heron exists that I could read. What there is:
- Adults foraging in Colorado wetlands in the breeding season spent 62% of their time foraging, 8.6% in locomotion and 26% in self-maintenance (n = 12 focal birds) [Magee & Zareba 2019, p. 21, Table 6].
- On San Juan Island beaches in August the herons "spent 83.8±26.1% of their time standing or walking slowly" (50 twenty-minute focal samples; a student report) [Clark et al. 2021, p. 4].
- On the Fraser delta in autumn the average adult met its daily energy needs in 55-85% of the low-tide time available, the average juvenile in 73-100% or more [Butler 1995, p. 24].
- In the Lake Erie region, radio-tagged breeding herons spent 37-63% of daylight hours at the colony [Grau & Parris 1980, p. 45, Table 12]; this includes nest duties and says little about a September bird.

| Setting | Value | Status |

| Peer during a wait | a third of waits, 1-3 s, the bill 0.25 rad further down | **Estimate** |
