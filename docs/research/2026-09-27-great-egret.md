# Great Egret: measurements, late-September plumage, field marks, standing posture and neck shapes, and foraging

Date: 2026-09-27
Purpose: source data for the detailed Great Egret (`greegr`) model and its settings in the `heron` foraging style on `wade`, in `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-27-herons-design.md`. The scene: Willow Lake, Willamette Valley, Oregon, a morning in late September (about 20-30 September). The egrets stand in the water on a shallow shelf along the far waterline (the new `far-shallows` zone), 60-66 m from the player, seen through binoculars (7.5° vertical field) or, mostly, the telescope (2°: about 2 mm of bird per pixel at 63 m on a 1080-pixel-tall view). Nothing disturbs them and they don't fly.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt or a summary of a paywalled page; I could not read the page, so treat it as unverified. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo and scaled it by the exposed culmen (110 mm, section 2.5) unless stated. "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements** (mean of the sexes, *A. a. egretta*; mm): exposed culmen **110**, bill depth at the feathering **19**, width **13** (Estimate), tarsus **149**, bare tibia **100**, middle toe **99**, wing chord **382**, tail **140**; mass about 0.97 kg. The one large sexed sample is Herring et al.'s 76 live Everglades adults (via [BoW-A]); BBR, Palmer and AVONET agree within their ranges (section 2.5).
- **Model length** (bill tip to tail tip in the traced posture) is not a published number: **about 0.76 m** on the side-on stalking bird of section 8 (photo-measured), against 0.94-1.04 m for stretched skins.
- **The neck** is about **0.45 m** along its curve from the top of the breast to the occiput, on the erect bird (photo-measured). Nineteen cervical vertebrae in the Grey Heron; the kink is the C6-C7 joint, **about a third of the neck's length down from the head** on the photos (section 6).
- **One version.** In September juveniles and non-breeding adults are both all white with a yellow bill, black legs and no plumes; a dusky bill tip hints at a young bird but is not a clean mark [BoW-A; BBR 1884]. The model paints the non-breeding bird: yellow bill with the gape running back past the eye, yellow-green lores, pale yellow iris, black legs and feet.
- **Cast: two egrets.** Great Egrets are fewer than Great Blue Herons in the valley's September records (279 iNaturalist records against 486, same box and window) and usually forage alone or in twos and threes at a small lake; two keeps the shelf honest with the style's spacing.
- **Neck shapes, photo-measured** (crown above the top of the back): **hunch about 20 mm** (the head sunk on the shoulders, the body tilted up nearly upright); **hunting S 90-120 mm**, the bill 14-22° below level; **erect about 375 mm**, the bill level; **swallow** erect with the bill **20-25° above level**; **strike**: the bill **60-80° below level**, the head going under to about the eye, the neck sometimes straight forward, sometimes looped.
- **Standing height** (feet to crown): hunch 0.58 m, hunting S 0.52-0.60 m, erect about 0.88 m, swallowing 0.96 m.
- **How deep it wades:** 2-15 cm on the valley photos; similar depths to the Great Blue Heron's, most often 15-25 cm, in New Jersey [Willard 1977, p. 464, Fig. 4]; 28 cm is its whole leg [Powell 1987, via the Great Blue Heron document].
- **Foraging:** slow walking most of the time (60-90%, 71% in one study), then stand-and-wait; walking at **0.04 m/s** while foraging [Brzorad et al. 2004]; **0.4-0.9 strikes a minute** [Mincey 2006; Willard 1977]; catches about **half to two-thirds** of strikes (0.52-0.68), fewer in late summer when young birds are learning [Moreno et al. 2005; Mincey 2006; Brzorad et al. 2004]; forages 63-69% of the day in Africa [BoW-F].
- **Spacing:** usually solitary and defends a feeding spot, but tolerates birds 1-15 m away in groups where food is dense [Wiggins 1991; BoW-F]; neither avoids nor seeks Great Blue Herons [BoW-B]. Settings: **5 m** from another egret, **3 m** from a Great Blue Heron (which keeps the same 3 m from an egret; the Great Blue Heron document, section 11).

## 2. Measurements

The North American subspecies is *Ardea alba egretta* (Gmelin 1789), the "American Egret" of the older books (*Herodias egretta*, *Casmerodius albus egretta*). Ridgway's *Birds of North and Middle America* never reached the herons (the series ended with Part XI, Friedmann 1950, on the diurnal raptors), and Bent 1926 gives egg measurements only. The primary tables I could read are Baird, Brewer & Ridgway's *Water Birds of North America* (1884), Ridgway's *Manual* (1887), the sexed series that Birds of the World v2.0 reprints from Palmer 1962, the Smithsonian (USNM) skins and Herring et al. 2008 [BoW-A], and the AVONET skins. Palmer 1962, Hancock & Kushlan 1984, Pyle 2008 and Herring et al. 2008 themselves could not be read; their numbers below are as Birds of the World prints them.

### 2.6 The neck: vertebrae and where the kink sits

No source I found counts or measures the Great Egret's own cervical vertebrae. What is known comes from its congeners and from comparative studies of the heron neck:

- **Count.** All four Grey Herons (*Ardea cinerea*, the Old World counterpart of the Great Blue Heron, same genus as the egret) skeletonised by Golob et al. had **19 cervical vertebrae**; Boas (1929) had counted 16 or 17 in the same species, which Golob et al. put down to his counting the last two cervicals (those with incomplete ribs) as thoracic [Golob et al. 2016, pp. 61-62]. Wikipedia's "Heron" article gives "20 or 21" cervicals for herons generally, citing the *Handbook of the Birds of the World* family account [Martínez-Vilalta & Motis 1992, pp. 376-403, not read]. For the model: **19, as in the congener (Estimate)**. The chain does not need the count, only where the bends are.
- **The kink is at the sixth cervical.** "A pervasive—yet untested—hypothesis is that a single mid-cervical vertebra, C6 in ardeids and C8 in anhingas, plays a crucial role (Donitz 1873; Forbes 1882; Böker 1937; Král 1965)" [Fleming & Black 2026, Introduction]. In a CT-based study of 24 pelecanimorph species (ten herons), "all members of Ardeidae had only one vertebra in Region 3", the mid-neck region; "Region 3 vertebrae were selected as the first vertebra in this region in all species, which corresponds to C6 in ardeids"; Region 2 (above it, toward the head) has 2-5 vertebrae and Region 4 (below it) "up to 8 vertebrae in Ardeidae"; and "In ardeids and anhingas, cervical Region 3 is reduced, producing an abrupt transition between Regions 2 and 4. Extra flexion may be facilitated at regional boundaries where vertebral shape changes abruptly ... Birds with higher vertebral counts in Region 3, in contrast, likely distribute bending across more mid-neck joints, resulting in a smoother overall curvature" [Fleming & Black 2026, Results and Discussion]. "Boas (1929) and Král (1965) documented that a strong dorsal elastic ligament (ligamentum elasticum interlaminare), present along most of the avian neck, is interrupted in ardeids between C6 and C7, a modification interpreted as permitting greater angular displacement" [Fleming & Black 2026, Discussion]. In the Grey Heron, "Cervical vertebrae are characterized by stronger calcification of the sixth vertebra and its rigid link to the fifth" [Golob et al. 2016, abstract and p. 62, Fig. 6].
- **What that means for the silhouette.** The neck is two stiff-ish columns joined at a sharp hinge: an upper neck of about four vertebrae (C2-C5, with C6 locked to C5) carrying the head, and a long lower neck (C7 to the last cervical, about 13 vertebrae) rising from the body. The hinge is between C6 and C7. With about 5 of 19 vertebrae above it, the kink sits roughly **a quarter to a third of the way down the neck from the head** by count (computed); the ardeid mid-neck vertebrae are also the elongated ones (Regions 2-4 [Fleming & Black 2026]), so by length it is nearer a third. Section 6 measures where it sits on photos: about a third of the neck's length down from the occiput, on the hunting-S photos.
- **Neck length.** No published measurement. Photo-measured on `greegr-erect.jpg` (1.15 mm/px): from the neck's base at the top of the breast (1080, 483) up its front curve to the occiput (1205, 118), four chords of 191, 113, 85 px, about 389 px, **about 450 mm**. The published figure for the whole bird, 0.94-1.04 m stretched, makes the neck a little under half of it.

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

- **Juveniles look like non-breeding adults.** "Juveniles are similar to nonbreeding adults, but the yellow bill has a blackish tip" [BoW-ID]; "Juvenile Plumage is completely white, with no scapular plumes or elongated feathers. Body feathers can be shorter and softer than in later plumages. Juvenile flight feathers are narrower than basic feathers, the outer primaries are tapered or pointed" [BoW-A]; "This plumage is all pure white, without any trace of plumes" [Bent 1926, p. 139]. Juvenile plumage "is present primarily from June-October in northernmost populations", followed by a partial preformative moult "primarily September-April", mostly on the wintering grounds [BoW-A]. None of this is visible at 60 m, and the narrower primaries hardly even on a photo.
- **The dark bill tip is not a clean age mark.** Birds of the World says the bill of juveniles and first-year birds is yellow, "often with a dark tip" [BoW-A], but BBR describe the adult's bill as yellow with "the culmen usually black near the tip, sometimes nearly the entire maxilla black" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 24], and breeding *egretta* show "occasional variable amounts of black on the culmen" [BoW-A]. A dusky tip in September points to a young bird but does not prove it.
- **Adults have lost their plumes by September, mostly.** The train "is shed soon after the breeding season is over, in June and July"; the complete postnuptial moult of adults runs "in July and August", and winter adults are "not always wholly devoid of plumes; but the plumes, if present, are fewer in number and much shorter than in spring" [Bent 1926, pp. 139-140]. In North America the prebasic moults "occur primarily in May-December" [BoW-A]. The bright green lores are gone "When incubation begins" [BoW-ID].
- **Who wanders north in late summer.** "This and several other species of herons are much given to northward wanderings in summer and fall; it is usually, if not always, the young birds that indulge in these erratic journeys after the nesting season is over" [Bent 1926, p. 143]. "The northward dispersal of juveniles occurs from June to October, with peak numbers in August and September; the status of adults in these dispersals is unknown" [BoW-M]. The Willamette Valley's autumn egrets arrive from July, peak in early September and stay through the winter [counts, section 3.4]. It is not known where they come from (section 3.4): "it is completely unknown what proportion of these birds may be from local populations versus distant ones" [Hunter 2023, OB 49(1), pp. 27-29].

### 3.2 iNaturalist, Willamette Valley box

Taxon 144455 (*Ardea alba*), research grade, box 43.8-45.8 N, 122.6-123.6 W, with photos: 525 records in September and October of all years, **279 from 11 September to 12 October** [iNat query 2026]. For the same box and window the Great Blue Heron has 486 (its document, section 3.2). I did not age the egrets: the age classes look alike in September (section 3.1), and a dusky bill tip, the one hint, is too small to read on most record photos. The appendix lists the photos used.

### 3.3 Recommendation

- **One version,** the non-breeding bird: white, yellow bill, black legs, no plumes. A dusky tip to the bill (about the last fifth of the culmen) would mark some young birds; it is optional, and a later round could add it as a second version.
- **Two egrets** in the cast. The valley's egrets feed alone or a few together on small waters; two on a 34 m shelf, with a Great Blue Heron or two, keeps each at its spacing (section 11) and shows the size difference between the species side by side.

## 4. Field marks

Colour words are the sources'; I have not invented hex values. "(photo)" marks my observation on the reference photos of section 8. Sexes are alike in all plumages [BoW-A].

### 4.1 What the sources say

- **All white, at every age and season.** "Plumage entirely pure white at all stages and seasons" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 22]; "Color entirely pure white at all seasons and at all ages" [p. 24]; "All feathers on Great Egrets are white" [AAB-ID]; "All four taxa have entirely white plumage; there are no darker color morphs" [BoW-ID].
- **Bill yellow, long and dagger-like.** "Nonbreeding adults have a yellow bill" [BoW-ID]; "In nonbreeding adults [*egretta*], the bill is dull, streaked yellow. In breeding adults, the bill becomes brighter orange ... with occasional variable amounts of black on the culmen" [BoW-A, Bare Parts]; "Their bills are yellowish-orange, and the legs black" [AAB-ID]; "Bill and lores rich chrome-yellow (the latter sometimes tinged with light green), the culmen usually black near the tip, sometimes nearly the entire maxilla black" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 24]; "In all subspecies, the bill becomes yellow in juveniles and during the first year of age, often with a dark tip" [BoW-A, Bare Parts].
- **The gape runs back past the eye.** The Great Egret "can generally be differentiated from other white-plumaged herons and egrets by its long and thin bill and legs, gape that extends distally past the eye, lack of head and chest plumes, and overall size"; in the intermediate egrets "the gape does not extend behind the eyes" [BoW-ID]. How far behind the eye: section 5.1 (photo-measured).
- **Lores and eye.** "In all populations, the cere and facial skin are typically pale to golden yellow, with the orbital skin tinged green during the nonbreeding season. It becomes more uniformly green, varying somewhat by subspecies, from dull green or lime green in subspecies *egretta* ... during the breeding season"; "the iris is pale yellow" [BoW-A, Bare Parts]; "iris naples-yellow" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 24]. "A patch of skin on its face turns neon green" in the breeding season [AAB-O]: so the bright green lores are a spring mark, not a September one.
- **Legs and feet black.** "legs and feet entirely deep black" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 24]; "The legs and feet of adults of *egretta* are slate gray to blackish. During the breeding season, the legs and feet of *egretta* become blacker" [BoW-A, Bare Parts]; "long, predominantly black legs and feet" [BoW-ID]. American birds have black tibiae too ("tibiae black" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 23]); the Old World races have paler, yellowish or pinkish tibiae [BoW-A], which the model must not copy.
- **No plumes in September.** The aigrettes (30-50 elongated scapulars) are grown for breeding; "When incubation begins, the bill, lores, and eyering fade to a yellowish hue, and the plumes are reduced in length and number" [BoW-ID]. Bent: the train "is shed soon after the breeding season is over, in June and July ... The adult winter plumage, acquired by a complete postnuptial molt in July and August, is not always wholly devoid of plumes; but the plumes, if present, are fewer in number and much shorter than in spring" [Bent 1926, pp. 139-140]. Juveniles have "no scapular plumes or elongated feathers" [BoW-A]. There are never head or chest plumes [BoW-ID; BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 23: "Jugular plumes slightly lengthened, with broad and undecomposed webs"]. The iNaturalist tally (section 3) checks how many late-September birds still show short plumes.
- **Shape.** "Tall and lanky", "long and thin bill and legs" [BoW-ID]; "long, S-curved necks and long, dagger-like bills" [AAB-ID]; "Slightly smaller and more svelte than a Great Blue Heron" [AAB-O]; Bent recognised them "at a great distance by the long neck and slender body and at a considerable distance by the large yellow bill" [Bent 1926, p. 144].

### 4.3 What reads at 2 mm per pixel

At 63 m through the telescope (about 2 mm per pixel):
- **The bill:** 110 mm, 55 px long and 9-10 px deep at the base: its yellow reads plainly against the white.
- **The legs:** black, 5-8 px wide, showing from the belly to the water (section 6).
- **The gape past the eye:** about 4-5 px behind the eye and the yellow lores a 10 px wedge: at the edge of what reads. Paint it clearly; it is the mark that parts this bird from the smaller white egrets, though none are in the guide.
- **The eye:** about 8 mm across, 4 px: a pale-yellow dot with a dark pupil at best.
- **No plumes:** nothing on the back or breast should stand out from the body's outline.

## 5. Structure

### 5.1 Head and bill

- **Bill shape:** "Bill moderately slender, the upper and lower outlines almost parallel to near the end, where gently curved, the culmen more abruptly so than the gonys, though the curve is quite gradual" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 22]; "a long, dagger-like bill" [BoW-ID]. So a straight, deep-based dagger: the two edges run nearly parallel for most of the length and close to a point in the last part, the top edge curving down a little more than the bottom one rises. Depth 17.8-20.3 mm at the base [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 24], width 11.8 mm at the nostril [Tobias et al. 2022]: laterally compressed.
- **Feathering at the bill base:** "Mental apex reaching a point about midway between the tip of the bill and the eye; malar apex decidedly anterior to the frontal apex, and extending to beneath the posterior end of the nostrils" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 22]. The chin feathers run far forward under the lower mandible, and the cheek feathers reach forward past the forehead feathering to below the back of the nostril: the bare skin of the face (the lores) is a narrow wedge above the gape.

- **The gape, photo-measured.** On the side-on `greegr-S-side.jpeg` (0.98 mm/px) the eye's centre is at (752, 494) and the gape line, the dark seam between the mandibles, runs back to (744, 504): **about 9 mm behind the eye's centre and 10 mm below it**. The bare yellow lores fill the wedge between the gape, the eye and the forehead feathers.
- **The eye:** about 8 mm across as it shows (photo), so the model's eye radius is **about 4 mm**, high on the head, level with the top of the bill's base.
- **The forehead feathering** meets the culmen 36 px, 35 mm, in front of the eye's centre on that photo; the exposed culmen is **0.774 of the distance from the eye's centre to the bill tip** (computed), which this document uses to scale photos where the feather edge can't be read.
- **Bill profile:** in side view the culmen is straight for about four-fifths of its length and curves down a little to the tip; the gonys rises gently to meet it (photo; "almost parallel to near the end" [BBR 1884]). Depth falls from 19 mm at the base to about 12 mm at mid-length (photo, about 0.6 of the base).

### 5.2 Neck, body, wing, tail, legs

- **Neck:** long and thin, the upper neck white and smooth; the kink sits about a third of the way down from the head (section 6). No plumes.
- **Body:** slim and deep-keeled; in the hunting S the back runs about level or a little down to the tail, and at rest the body tilts up (section 6).
- **Folded wing:** covers the side of the body from the shoulder to the tail; the primaries end at about the tail's tip (photo).
- **Tail:** short and square, almost hidden under the folded wings (photo).
- **Legs and feet:** black, long; the belly feathers hang over the top of the bare tibia on live birds, so less of it shows than the skins' 100 mm (section 6). Long slender toes.

## 7. Foraging behaviour

### 7.1 What the sources say

**How it hunts.**
- "This heron's primary hunting method was slow wading. Its pace was faster than that of the Great Blue Heron. Egrets sometimes struck while moving and often swallowed prey as they continued walking. Hunting postures were very similar to those of the Great Blue Heron. Upright stand-and-wait, another frequent hunting method of Great Egrets, was used mostly amid flooded grass at high tide and when birds were part of large flocks. Great Egrets rarely used other feeding methods" [Willard 1977, p. 465; Brigantine NWR, New Jersey, 1,380 hours over 1971-1974]. Great Blue Herons, by contrast, "used upright stand-and-wait and slow-wading equally often. They waded more slowly than any other species and often stopped for long periods before striking or moving on" [p. 465].
- In Willard's monthly chart of feeding methods (Fig. 5, p. 466; read from the figure), the Great Egret's **September** observations (N = 107) were mostly "wade slowly" (the largest dot, 0.41-0.50 or more), with "stand still" in the 0.06-0.10 class and active pursuit 0.01-0.05. In August stand-still rose to about 0.41-0.50, in flocks.
- "The majority of its foraging time (60-90%) is spent slow walking (Kushlan and Hancock 2005), followed by stand-and-wait and peering techniques (Willard 1977, Rodgers 1983)"; "At Lake St. Lucia, South Africa, the Great Egret employed slow walking 71% of the time (Whitfield and Blaber 1979)"; stand-and-wait "accounts for 19% of the foraging behaviors"; flushing behaviours 10% [BoW-F].
- Definitions: "In stand and wait ... a heron stands motionless in water or on land waiting for prey to approach. There are two basic postures. In upright posture the body is held erect, head and neck are fully extended angled away from the body. In crouched posture, the body is held horizontal to the perch or the water, legs are bent, and the head and neck are partially retracted." "In walk slowly ..., a heron moves slowly, stalking prey. Walking becomes slower as the heron examines items or areas of interest, and just before striking the heron may walk so slowly that this behavior merges with stand and wait. Alternately herons feeding by stand and wait may periodically walk very slowly for several steps and then resume stand and wait" [Kushlan 1976, p. 87]. Of the typical day herons, "the Little Blue Heron and Great Egret are more circumscribed than the Snowy Egret, Louisiana Heron, and Reddish Egret" [p. 90]. Birds of the World adds that stand-and-wait birds hold "the neck fully extended and nearly vertical, or ('lean and wait') in a crouch or lean with neck extended more horizontally", and that "Birds ambulate slowly (i.e., ≤ 1 step per second; Rodgers 1983) and methodically while stalking prey"; quick walking is "> 1 step per second, but ≤ 2 steps per second, and running ... ≥ 2 steps per second" (Rodgers 1983) [BoW-F].
- Head-swaying: "a bird sways its head back and forth in an arc with its bill pointed toward one spot, particularly just before striking"; "Several individuals used head-swaying" [Willard 1977, pp. 465, 465 Table 4]; "thought to be used to obtain parallax" [BoW-F].
- Wing-flicking: "A slowly walking egret will suddenly extend and retract its wings in a flicking action that is usually repeated several times. Often, the bird then stops walking and looks around before resuming its slow walk" [BoW-F, after Kushlan 1976, p. 88].
- Older words: "On foot its movements are graceful, its step measured, its long neck being gracefully retracted and curved" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 26, after Audubon]; "It will watch for a rat or a mouse with complete immobility, when suddenly, and with a surprising rapidity of movement, it seizes its prey" [p. 25]; "Their movements are stately and the strokes of their rapierlike bills are quick and sure" [Bent 1926, p. 140]; "They typically stand still and watch for unsuspecting prey to pass by. Then, with startling speed, the egrets strike with a jab of their long neck and bill" [AAB-ID].

**Speed while foraging.** "Snowy Egrets averaged 0.13 m/s while foraging, while Great Egrets moved at a third of that speed (0.04 m/s)"; "Snowy Egrets moved faster in August than in May, but Great Egrets maintained or slowed their foraging speed" [Brzorad et al. 2004, abstract; Arthur Kill, New York-New Jersey, 2002 breeding season]. In coastal Georgia and South Carolina, foraging Great Egrets moved 4.6-9.3 m per 5-minute bout (means by habitat; ponds 4.62 ± 1.04 m) [Mincey 2006, p. 44, Table 5], that is **0.015-0.031 m/s** averaged over standing and walking (computed). In Kansas, "the percentage of time spent standing was twice as high at weirs as in rivers" [Maccarone & Brzorad 2007, abstract].

**Strike rate.**

**The strike.** No study times a Great Egret's strike. The one kinematic study of a heron striking at submerged prey, a Western Reef Heron (*Egretta gularis*) in captivity, found "Two distinct phases ... during head movement: i) 'Pre-strike' (mean path angle 60° to the vertical, mean velocity 52 cm/s). ii) 'Strike' (mean path angle 33°, mean velocity 270 cm/s). When prey was unsubmerged the two phases were less distinct ('pre-strike' 47°, 62 cm/s; 'strike' 42°, 154 cm/s)" [Katzir & Intrator 1987, abstract]. So the head comes down steeply in the strike, at about **57° below horizontal** (computed: 90° − 33°), after a slower, shallower approach; at 2.7 m/s a head travelling 0.25-0.35 m takes **0.09-0.13 s** (computed, with the egret's neck and bill lengths from sections 2 and 6). For Little Egrets striking at submerged prey in the field, "Prey capture success was significantly related to strike angle, being highest at the most acute angles. Strike depth had no effect on capture success" [Lotem et al. 1991, Snippet].

**Handling and swallowing.** "Fish are usually captured crosswise in the bill, held for a few seconds, and then swallowed headfirst (Whitfield and Blaber 1979)"; "Prey are often shaken until subdued and positioned for consumption; larger prey may be dipped in water" [BoW-F]. Willard's egrets "often swallowed prey as they continued walking" [p. 465]. Prey: mainly small fish, also frogs, crayfish, insects, snakes and small mammals [Bent 1926, p. 140; BoW-F].

**Time budget.** "Two studies in Africa found that the species forages 63-69% of the day, with breaks between mid-morning and mid-afternoon (Whitfield 1979, Kasoma 2000)"; in tropical Africa "69% of daylight hours foraging, 29.7% loafing or performing self-maintenance, and 1.3% engaging in other activities"; "Foraging bouts are interrupted by breaks of 1-2 h for loafing and self-maintenance"; "In a foraging area, individuals engaged in self-maintenance either in the water or on the shore, or the individual may fly to a nearby tree or bush"; it forages "especially at dawn and dusk"; in coastal China 20-40% of the daytime [BoW-B; BoW-F]. Egrets roost communally at night and "scatter out over the surrounding country early in the morning" [Bent 1926, p. 141]. No autumn time budget exists for North America.

**Spacing.**
- "The Great Egret is typically a solitary forager and defends its feeding territory aggressively, although when food is abundant, it may feed in flocks of hundreds of birds; more typically, 10-20 foraging egrets make up these aggregations" [BoW-F]. "The Great Egret maintains individual distance from other herons and egrets throughout the year" [BoW-B].
- In the Everglades, "individuals were regularly spaced, suggesting solitary foraging (Bancroft et al. 1995)"; in Uganda and South Africa "individuals were observed defending foraging sites against conspecifics, resulting in regular spacing of individuals along the water's edge" [BoW-F].
- In a Texas salt marsh, egrets were grouped by nearest-neighbour distance into 1-15 m, 15-30 m and 30+ m; "Birds feeding solitarily (30+ m) chased conspecifics more often and for greater distances than did those feeding in groups (1-15 m)" [Wiggins 1991, Snippet]; "individuals chased conspecifics more often (7.1 chases/h) than when feeding in aggregations (2.9 chases/h). The distance chased also increased for solitary versus aggregated foragers (23.0 m vs. 11.0 m)" [BoW-F, after Wiggins 1991].
- "Although we did not measure it, the distance between individual Great Egrets appeared much less in eelgrass than elsewhere. As eelgrass is available for only a few hours at low tide, it may be inefficient for egrets to defend large feeding areas in this habitat" [Custer & Osborn 1978, p. 741].
- **Toward Great Blue Herons:** in Louisiana crayfish ponds "the Great Egret neither avoided nor preferred foraging near conspecifics or other ardeids, such as Great Blue Heron" (Green 2006) [BoW-B]; in New Jersey the two big herons shared depths (hunting-depth overlap 0.69-0.94 by month) and sat at the centre of mixed flocks [Willard 1977, pp. 465, 467]; "Intraspecific interactions are more frequent than interspecific ...; the outcomes of the latter are largely determined by comparative body size" [BoW-B]. The larger Great Blue Heron wins.
- An egret in winter Florida: "sometimes as many as 15 or 20 of them" in "a small pond hole" [Bent 1926, p. 144]; in Trinidad "found usually singly, or not more than two or three together" [BBR 1884, vol. I, p. 25].

**A bout, as a script** (Estimate): wait 4-20 s (peering now and then) → stalk 0.5-3 m at 0.04 m/s → wait → (draw back 0.3 s → strike 0.1 s → hold 0.25 s → recover; half the time swallow 1-3 s with the bill up) → ... for 5-15 minutes → hunch 2-8 minutes → alert or wait.

## 12. Open questions and confidence

- **Heights rest on one photo per shape.** The hunch, erect and swallow are each one bird; the crown heights have ±15% bounds for that.
- **Scale from the eye.** Where the forehead feathering can't be read, the photos are scaled by the eye-to-tip distance, calibrated on one side-on bird; a head turned toward the camera reads larger than it is.
- **The hunch's body.** The photographed hunch stands on a post with its body upright; how far a hunched egret standing in water tilts its body is an estimate.
- **No timed video.** Wait, alert, bout and rest lengths are estimates; walking speed, strike rate and catch rate are sourced.
- **Ages not tallied.** One version is honest to the field marks; a dusky-tipped juvenile version is left for later.
- **Snippet-only claims:** the Birds of the World sentences [BoW-A, BoW-B, BoW-F, BoW-ID, BoW-M] and Wiggins 1991 were read only as excerpts.
