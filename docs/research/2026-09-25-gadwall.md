# Gadwall: measurements, late-September plumage, field marks, structure, floating posture, and dabbling

Date: 2026-09-25
Purpose: source data for the detailed Gadwall (`gadwal`) model and its `dabbler` swim style. The cast is three Gadwalls on `swim` in the `open-water` zone of Willow Lake (Willamette Valley, Oregon), 15-60 m from the player, on a late-September morning, `"plumage": "mixed"`. The key identification pair this round is **female Gadwall against female Mallard**; the Mallard is researched in parallel in `docs/research/2026-09-25-mallard.md`.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt of a paywalled page; I could not read the page, so treat it as unverified. "Abstract only" means only the abstract was readable. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo and scaled it by the stated reference (the exposed culmen, or a printed millimetre rule where the photo has one). "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements, mean of the sexes** (mm): exposed culmen **41** (male 42.5, female 40.5), bill width **16.5** and depth **13.7** at the nostril (about 15 at the feathered base), tarsus **39**, middle toe without claw **46**, wing chord **257**, tail **≈ 80**; mass **≈ 865 g** in the hunting season (male 907, female 822; n = 122 and 101) [Ridgway 1887, p. 95; BBR 1884, p. 507; Phillips 1923, pp. 139-140; Tobias et al. 2022; Kortright 1942, p. 383]. The sexes differ by only 5-8% in linear size.
- **Against the Mallard** (same authors and methods, computed): length **0.90**, mass **0.74-0.76**, culmen **0.74**, bill width **0.79**, bill depth **0.77** of the Mallard's. The Gadwall is 10% smaller but its bill is 25% shorter and 20% narrower: "The bill is noticeably thinner than a Mallard's" [AAB-ID].
- **Bill planform** (photo-measured on a museum skin against a printed millimetre rule): parallel-sided within ±1% from 13 mm behind the tip to the base; the tip a round arc that reaches 90% of full width 8 mm behind the tip. The nail is small, dark and hooked, about 6-7 mm long.
- **Floating** (photo-measured on seven side-on photos, scaled by the culmen): bill tip to tail tip at rest **0.31-0.34 m** (use **0.325 m**, 64% of the published stretched length); top of the back **47-74 mm** (mean 62) above the water, crown about 105 mm, tail tip about 40 mm, tail cocked 0-17°; the waterline runs from just under the breast's forward point back over 75-85% of the body. **Draft 0.45 of the body depth** (0.36-0.59), confirmed by a buoyancy check.
- **Late-September plumage**: in 173 research-grade iNaturalist records from the Willamette Valley (11 September-12 October, 2017-2026), **110 of 118 males (93%) were already in full or near-full alternate plumage**, 4 were moulting and 4 eclipse-like; males were **47%** of the 249 sexable birds. No bird could be called a juvenile with confidence (eight records hold possible juveniles). The sources expected more moult: "I have seen birds in this molt from September 8 to November 23" [Bent 1923, p. 82]; "By the end of October most birds are in full Alternate dress" [Oring 1968, p. 372].
- **Recommendation: two variants, `male` (adult alternate) and `female`** (the adult female; juveniles and moulting males read as female-types at 15-60 m). **Flock mix: 50:50 per bird**, ideally a pair (one male, one female) plus a third bird of either sex. The current uniform pick between the two existing variants already gives this.
- **Female Gadwall against female Mallard**: a grey ridge running unbroken to the nail over an orange side panel (the Mallard's orange is crossed by a dark saddle); a thinner, parallel-sided bill; a steep forehead rising 26-47° off the culmen line; and a white speculum bar 20-41 mm long at the rear of the flank, showing on at least one floating bird in two-thirds of the local records [Kaufman 2020; AAB-ID; sections 3.2, 4.3].
- **Feeding**: the Gadwall tips up least of the dabblers. On Lake Constance 71% of feeding Gadwalls fed swimming with the head under water and 13% upending, against 44% and 42% for Mallards [Szijj 1965, p. 50]; in October in Alabama 93% of feeding time was surface-pecking, 5% head down, 2% tip-up [McKnight & Hepp 1998, p. 675]. Gadwall upends on video last **3.1 s (1.1-4.5, n = 23)** with the body 80-90° from level and the feet paddling at the surface, with 2-4 s level between them (**Estimate**, video); Mallards average 4.2 s [Szijj 1965, p. 46].
- **Swimming**: no Gadwall measurement exists; free-swimming Mallards chose 0.48 m/s, the speed of minimum cost of transport [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970, pp. 771-772]. Recommended Gadwall cruise **0.40 m/s** (**Estimate**, from its shorter waterline: hull speed about 0.62 m/s against the Mallard's 0.71).
- **Time budget**: 44% of time feeding in October [Paulus 1984, abstract only]; for a late-September morning about 45% feeding, 20% swimming, 20% loafing, 5% preening, 10% alert (**Estimate**).
- **Tracing references** (scratchpad): `gadwal-m-1.jpg` (male profile), `gadwal-f-2.jpg` (female profile), `gadwal-f-1.jpg` (female plumage, CC BY), and local Oregon birds from 21 September to 9 October (`gadwal-m-sept.jpg`, `gadwal-m-local.jpg`, `gadwal-f-local.jpg`). The harvested `gadwal.jpg` shows an angled male: use it for colour, not for lengths.

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

**Status in the region.**
- "Gadwalls are short-distance migrants, most wintering north of the tropics. Many do not migrate at all, and they can be found year round in both western and eastern Washington ... Fall migration lasts from late August to October and peaks in September" [BirdWeb].
- The Willamette Valley Birding Trail checklist (2009) marks the Gadwall as breeding in the valley and "occasionally detected" in spring, fall and winter, "rarely" in summer [WVBT 2009]. iNaturalist research-grade records in the Willamette box (all years) run 155-367 a month, 161 in September and 172 in October; June still has 150 [iNat query 2026]. So the late-September birds are a mix of local breeders and their young and early migrants.
- Breeding is late: "In all parts of its range the Gadwall breeds a little later than the Mallard, perhaps averaging nearly three weeks later"; "In Washington and Oregon it breeds not earlier than June" [Phillips 1923, p. 151]. Downy young in June and July give juveniles of 10-14 weeks by late September (computed from the 24-day incubation and 50-56-day fledging [Oring 1968, pp. 357, 363]).

**Adult males.**
- "Adult males begin to molt into the eclipse plumage about the last of May or first of June ... I have seen males in full eclipse as early as August 10 and as late as September 8; this plumage is an almost complete reproduction of the female's, or young male's, excepting, of course, the wings, which are molted only once in August. The molt out of the eclipse plumage consumes about two months; I have seen birds in this molt from September 8 to November 23, but I am inclined to think that old drakes usually attain their full plumage by November 1st or earlier" [Bent 1923, p. 82].
- "Soon after the Prebasic molt of each body papilla is completed, its Prealternate molt begins. Indeed, Alternate body feathers are visible before the wings are fully grown. Late in August drakes are a mass of growing pennae and down papillae, all tracts molting practically at once. By the end of October most birds are in full Alternate dress, down and pennae. Basic tertials are molted between 10 November and 14 December or later" [Oring 1968, p. 372]. "In the Gadwall feathers grown from 18 August to 5 May or later are Alternate" [p. 376]. Wing moult: "By September nearly all drakes have completed their new tails and wings" [p. 369].
- "In early autumn the male commences a continuous moult out of the female-like plumage of the eclipse, into the full winter dress ... In early stages the male may be identified by a scattering of the vermiculated, grey feathers of winter plumage on the back and scapulars; also by the appearance of new crescent-marked feathers on the chest, and black ones on the tail-coverts; the bill still resembles that of the female, except that it is not spotted. During October, gradually increasing numbers of grey back-feathers, and of crescent-marked ones on the chest, become apparent. By November most of the birds are in full winter plumage" [Kortright 1942, p. 175].
- The eclipse itself: "a reproduction of that of the female except that the feathers of the chest are more finely marked with transverse bars; the bill becomes like that of the female, except that it is not spotted" [Kortright 1942, p. 175]. "At that stage, most of the black feathers near its tail are replaced by brown, and the sides of its bill are orange, similar to the female's pattern" [Kaufman 2020].
- Note the tertials: an adult male's long, pointed, silver-grey alternate tertials are not renewed until November-December [Oring 1968, p. 372], so a late-September drake shows **basic tertials**: "shorter and less tapered at the tip than those of Alternate plumages" [Oring 1968, p. 372].

**Adult females.**
- The hen's body moult into alternate follows breeding: "By the time the young are 2 or 3 weeks old (mid-July at Delta), the hens are in general body molt. Alternate body plumage is completed in late October or early November" [Oring 1968, p. 372]. Her wing moult: "Adult females shed their wing feathers about 6 weeks after their young hatch" [p. 371], i.e. August-September for June broods; "Basic tertials are acquired as early as 5 August and as late as early October" [p. 371].
- So a late-September adult female is a mix of worn basic and fresh alternate body feathers, with a fresh or growing wing. Basic: "Chin and throat are buffier and body feathers more broadly edged with buff than in Alternate or Juvenal plumages ... about a third of the females are heavily streaked or spotted ventrally" [Oring 1968, p. 372]. Alternate: "Feathers of upper parts are dark brown narrowly edged with buff; the throat and chin are usually darker than in the Basic plumage. The venter is nearly always immaculate" [p. 373].

**Juveniles.**
- "Juvenal plumage.—Characterized by square-tipped rectrices, narrow body feathers, and dull wing color. Dorsal body and tail feathers are dark brown, edged and barred buff. Though venters usually have brown spots (single or double) or streaks, immaculate-bellied juveniles of both sexes are found occasionally" [Oring 1968, pp. 365-366].
- Moult: Basic I feathers appear on "the chest, sides, and lateral upper back in both sexes" from about 65-80 days [Oring 1968, p. 366]; Alternate I "Scapulars, as well as chest and vent feathers, push through the skin by 72 days"; head and neck feathers at 81-83 days; "most feathers are renewed by the end of November (approximately 150 days)" [pp. 366-367]. Juvenal lower back, rump and wing feathers are kept through the first winter [p. 368].
- Phillips: "Young Female in First (Juvenal) Plumage: Resembles adult female, but is thickly spotted all over lower side. The white of the speculum is gray and there is no black patch on the greater coverts." "Young Male in First Plumage: Very closely resembles female at a similar stage of development but can probably always be told by the light bars which occur on the feathers of the mantle." "Young Male beginning to assume Adult Plumage: A few of the black and white feathers appear upon the upper breast and the lower abdomen begins to lose the spots ... Flank feathers more or less vermiculated. Long tertials not developed. Rump and upper tail-coverts still brown. Adult plumage not complete till spring" [Phillips 1923, p. 140].
- Kortright: "JUVENILE. By September plumage of both sexes resembles that of adult female but is darker and much more heavily streaked on underparts. Young male can be told from young female by light bars on feathers of foreback, lacking in young female, and by presence of vermiculated feathers on flanks" [Kortright 1942, p. 176].
- Wing: juvenile tertials of both sexes "are short, bluntly pointed, and often frayed at their tips. They are quite similar to those of adult females"; immature females "usually have little or no cinnamon color and black is often restricted to two rows of coverts" [Carney 1992, p. 16].
- Bent: "Young birds gradually develop, during the first fall and winter, a plumage closely resembling that of the adult; by the month of March this first winter, or first nuptial, plumage is generally complete; there are usually a few spotted feathers scattered over the under parts, as signs of immaturity and the wings are much duller than those of adults, with little if any chestnut in the coverts" [Bent 1923, p. 82].

**What the sources predict for late September (my reading).** Adult drakes part-way from eclipse to alternate: grey vermiculated feathers, crescent-marked breast feathers and black tail coverts coming in, the bill still orange-sided, the tertials short and basic. Adult females in mixed basic and alternate plumage, looking like every other female Gadwall. Juveniles like dark, heavily streaked females, young males starting to show grey vermiculated flank feathers. **The local photos (section 3.2) show the drakes further on than this**: 93% of the males photographed from 11 September to 12 October were already grey with a black stern.

### 3.2 iNaturalist tally, Willamette Valley box

Query: taxon 558439 (*Mareca strepera*), research grade, box 43.8-45.8 N, 122.6-123.6 W, 11 September-12 October of every year: **173 records** (2017: 1, 2018: 1, 2019: 4, 2020: 18, 2021: 8, 2022: 21, 2023: 28, 2024: 39, 2025: 43, 2026: 10) [iNat query 2026]. Every photo of every record was examined (enlarged where the birds were small) and each Gadwall classed as:
- **M-ALT**: adult male in full or near-full alternate plumage (grey vermiculated flank, crescent-scaled breast, sharply cut black stern);
- **M-MOULT**: male part-way out of eclipse (brown, buff-edged feathers mixed with grey; stern incomplete);
- **M-ECL**: male in eclipse (female-like body, but an all-dark bill, a large speculum or a plain grey head);
- **F-AD**: adult female (orange-sided bill with a dark ridge; broad, dark-centred, buff-edged feathers);
- **JUV**: juvenile (narrow feathers, streaked or spotted underparts, duller bill), only when it could be argued;
- **FT**: female-type not further ageable (adult female, juvenile or eclipse male);
- **NA**: a Gadwall not assessable (distant, backlit, in flight).

The criteria follow section 3.1. 120 records (groups A and B, 2017-2024, less the last 13) were classed by a sub-agent on these written criteria and 53 + 13 by me; I checked the sub-agent's notes and its calls on the moult and eclipse birds but did not re-view every photo. Results (computed):
- **Males: 118 of 249 sexable birds (47%).** Female-types (F-AD + FT): 131 (53%).
- **Of the 118 males, 110 (93%) were already in full or near-full alternate plumage**, 4 (3%) were visibly moulting out of eclipse and 4 (3%) looked eclipse-like. The earliest full-alternate males are on 12-16 September (records 204651309, 180957902); the moulting and eclipse-like males fall between 16 September and 11 October (eclipse-like: 183587344, 268554118, 185083008, 136360412; moulting: 402098509, 95918010, 205279298, 247575919). At photo resolution a male with a few brown feathers left can pass for M-ALT, so "near-full" is the fair reading.
- **No bird was confidently a juvenile.** Eight records hold female-types with juvenile-like features (dense spotting or streaking on the breast and belly, neat narrow flank feathers, an unspotted or narrow orange bill edge: 204494416, 247575897, 318110844, 317789429, 320003051, 400827236, 402803878, 402482823), but a third of adult females in basic are also "heavily streaked or spotted ventrally" [Oring 1968, p. 372], so these stay FT. Juveniles are surely present (the Gadwall breeds in the valley [WVBT 2009]); at the resolution of these photos, and at the game's distances, they are female-types.
- **The speculum on the water**: it showed as a white bar on at least one floating bird in **76 of 116 records** where it could be judged (66%), and was hidden on every bird in 40 (34%). Counted by bird in groups A and B (the sub-agent's count), it showed on **72 of 117 floating birds at rest (62%)**; relaxed full-alternate males often hide it completely. Kaufman's "sometimes visible when the bird is swimming" and Kortright's "seldom being visible" both fit individual birds; in a group, someone's usually shows.
- **Behaviour in the photos**: swimming 114 records, loafing 30, on land (logs, mud) 29, preening 17, surface dabbling 17, asleep 8, head-dip 8, tip-up 5, flying 2. Of the 30 records that show feeding, surface dabbling is 57%, head-dips 27% and tip-ups 17% (computed; photographers favour birds with the head up, so this undercounts head-dips and tip-ups).
- **Groups**: records show single birds, pairs and loose groups of 3-12, often with American Coots, Mallards, American Wigeons and Northern Shovelers. One record (246353350, 8 October 2024) shows exactly the game's cast: two males and a female swimming together within 1-2 body lengths.
- **Photo bias**: bright adult males are photographed more readily than female-types, and distant flocks (the NA birds, 135) could not be sexed. The 47% male share among sexable birds may therefore be a few points high. Some birds also appear in more than one record (the same pair photographed twice at one site on one day), which does not change the proportions much.

### 3.3 Recommendation

- **Model two variants: `male` (adult, full alternate) and `female` (adult female).**
  - Late-September Willamette males are already grey with a black stern: 93% of the males photographed. The sources' expectation of widespread moult in late September [Bent 1923, p. 82; Oring 1968, p. 372; Kortright 1942, p. 175] describes the start of the season; in these records the drakes were largely done by mid-September. One allowance for the date: the male's bill may keep some orange along the sides ("bluish black, with usually trace of dull orange at base and along edge" [Kortright 1942, p. 175]; `gadwal-m-sept-bill.jpg`), and his tertials may be the shorter basic ones [Oring 1968, p. 372].
  - A third variant is not worth its texture. Juveniles and moulting or eclipse males are female-types to a birder at 15-60 m; the juvenile's differences (streakier underparts, most of them below the waterline, and a narrower orange bill edge) are too small to read at that range.
  - If a third variant is wanted anyway, make it the **juvenile** (section 4.4): about a third of the female-types in late September are probably young of the year, going by the local breeding (**Estimate**).
- **Flock mix: 50:50 per bird.** The sexable birds were 47% male. With three birds, the most natural arrangement is **one pair (male and female, the male beside or just behind the female) plus one bird of either sex**; pairs are forming in autumn ("greater than 90% of females are paired by November" [LeSchack et al. 2020]). The spawner's uniform pick over `male` and `female` already gives 50:50; pairing them is a behaviour choice (section 11).

## 4. Field marks of each recommended plumage

### 4.1 Female (adult and juvenile alike at 20-50 m)

- **Overall**: "Females are patterned with brown and buff" [AAB-ID]; "a mottled brown" [Audubon]; "Back, scapulars, chest and sides, brown, feathers broadly margined, and with transverse bars of buff; breast, white" [Kortright 1942, p. 176].
- **Head**: "Head, with slight crest, and neck, pale buffy, streaked with dusky, darker on crown; chin and throat, whitish, faintly spotted with greyish" [Kortright 1942, p. 176]. "Colors chiefly brownish dusky and brownish white, in longitudinal streaks on head and neck" [BBR 1884, pp. 506-507].
- **Bill**: "bill, dull orange, spotted with dusky, and dusky along ridge, shorter than head; nail, black" [Kortright 1942, p. 176]; "Bill with a dark area down middle of culmen and light orange on the sides" [Phillips 1923, p. 140]; "the best mark is bill color: gray, with a neatly defined orange side panel" [Kaufman 2020]; "orange along the sides, neatly separated from the gray upper ridge" [Kaufman 2020]; "Females have a thin orange edge to their dark bills" [AAB-ID]. Seasonal: "Bill is dusky with dull orange or yellow sides ... Spotting is heaviest in late July through September, fading after October" [Oring 1968, p. 373].
- **Speculum**: "the wing is the same as in the male, except that the chestnut patch is reduced to the tips of a few of the median wing-coverts" [Phillips 1923, p. 139]; "The small white square on the trailing edge of the wing is sometimes visible when the bird is swimming" [Kaufman 2020]; "the white patch consists of just a few secondary feathers, and this mark can be smaller or absent on a few individuals" [Kaufman 2020]. Juvenile female: "The white of the speculum is gray" [Phillips 1923, p. 140].
- **Belly**: adult female "breast, white; belly, pale, often whitish, more or less faintly streaked with dusky" [Kortright 1942, p. 176]; "in adult plumage always whiter on abdomen" than a female Mallard [Phillips 1923, p. 139]; juvenile "thickly spotted all over lower side" [Phillips 1923, p. 140]. On a floating bird the belly is under water.
- **Feet**: "feet, dull yellow, webs, dusky" [Kortright 1942, p. 176]; "Legs and feet are straw-yellow to orange-brown; webs dusky" [Oring 1968, p. 373]; "yellow orange, paler than in the male" [Phillips 1923, p. 140].
- **Iris**: "dark brown" [Oring 1968, p. 373]; "dark brown to brown" [Kortright 1942, p. 176].

### 4.2 Adult male in late September

- **Alternate** (the plumage he is growing into): "Head, with low crest, pale brownish or grey, marked with small dusky streaks and dots; crown, darker ... bill, bluish black, with usually trace of dull orange at base and along edge ... Back, scapulars, sides and flanks, dusky, finely vermiculated with buffy or whitish, longer scapulars narrow and pointed, dusky, with cinnamon-orange margins; chest, greyish black, with whitish or buffy crescent-markings, giving a scaled effect; rump, black with slight greenish gloss; breast, white ... Tail, brownish grey, narrowly edged with whitish; upper and under coverts, glossy black. Wings. ... remaining lesser coverts and middle coverts, chestnut ... greater coverts, black ... secondaries, inner ones white ... tertials, pale greyish buff" [Kortright 1942, p. 175]. "Tertials long and pointed and light gray in color" [Phillips 1923, p. 139].
- "The black rump, uppertail coverts, and undertail coverts combine to create a contrasting back end to an otherwise gray-brown duck, a pattern that's diagnostic at any distance" [Kaufman 2020].
- At rest, the chestnut and black coverts are hidden: "When the bird is at rest, the wing is almost completely tucked in among the body plumage, buried under the scapular feathers from above and the breast feathers coming up from below" [Kaufman 2020].
- **Bill of the moulting male**: "Bill is slate gray, occasionally with orange or pale yellow sides" (adult male) [Oring 1968, p. 373]; in autumn moult "the bill still resembles that of the female, except that it is not spotted" [Kortright 1942, p. 175].
- **Feet**: "bright orange yellow, with blackish webs" [Kortright 1942, p. 175]; "grayish yellow (occasionally straw-yellow or dusky)" [Oring 1968, p. 373].

### 4.4 Juvenile in late September

- Sources (section 3.1): "Characterized by square-tipped rectrices, narrow body feathers, and dull wing color ... venters usually have brown spots (single or double) or streaks" [Oring 1968, pp. 365-366]; "Resembles adult female, but is thickly spotted all over lower side. The white of the speculum is gray" (young female) [Phillips 1923, p. 140]; "By September plumage of both sexes resembles that of adult female but is darker and much more heavily streaked on underparts. Young male can be told from young female by light bars on feathers of foreback ... and by presence of vermiculated feathers on flanks" [Kortright 1942, p. 176]. Bill: "gray with yellowish edges"; "may or may not have a few small brown spots" (young female) [Oring 1968, p. 373]. Tertials "short, bluntly pointed, and often frayed at their tips" [Carney 1992, p. 16].
- On a bird annotated juvenile at Vancouver on 28 September 2024 (`gadwal-juv-1.jpg`, section 8; photo observation): the neck and breast are **densely and finely streaked** dark brown on buff rather than scaled; the flank feathers are long and narrow with dark centres, giving a **streaky** rather than scalloped side; the undertail coverts are **boldly spotted**; the tertials are plain grey; the bill is grey-dusky over the upper half to two-thirds of the side with a **narrower, unspotted orange edge** along the cutting edge; no speculum shows. Its heights above the near-side waterline (back 52, crown 96, tail tip 43 mm, scaled by a 41 mm culmen) fall inside the adults' ranges.
- At 20-50 m a juvenile reads as a darker, streakier, plainer-billed female. A birder would call it a female-type Gadwall; the bill pattern (grey ridge, orange edge) still separates it from a female Mallard.

### 5.1 Bill

- **Side view**: "culmen gently concave in the middle, straight before and behind" [BBR 1884, p. 487]; "shorter than head" [Kortright 1942, pp. 175-176]. On the photos the upper outline is straight to slightly concave over the middle and the nail turns down at the tip in a small hook (photo).
- **Top view**: parallel-sided, round-tipped, width 0.40 of the culmen (section 2.6, photo-measured).
- **Depth**: 13.7 mm at the nostril [Tobias et al. 2022]; **15-18 mm** at the feathered base, perpendicular to the culmen (photo-measured on `gadwal-f-2.jpg` and `gadwal-m-1.jpg`; the lower bound is the better estimate, as the upper includes feathering at the chin); about 5-6 mm just behind the nail (photo). Depth falls roughly linearly from base to nail.
- **Nostril**: an oval about 4.5 mm long, its front edge about 9.5 mm in front of the feathers along the culmen (photo-measured on `gadwal-m-1.jpg`), matching Ridgway's rule that the tip is more than three times as far from the nostril as the feathers are [Ridgway 1887, p. 94].
- **Nail**: a small, dark, down-hooked nail at the tip, about **6-7 mm** long in side view (photo-measured on `gadwal-m-1.jpg` and `gadwal-f-1.jpg`, low confidence) and, from the rounded tip of the NHMO skin, about 6-7 mm wide, about 40% of the bill's width (**Estimate**; the nail's edge is not visible on the skin). "Nail, black" on the female [Kortright 1942, p. 176].
- **Lamellae**: "lamellae numerous, fine, more than 30 being visible from outside" [Ridgway 1887, p. 94]; "lamellae distinctly exposed" [BBR 1884, p. 487]; visible along the whole edge of the upper mandible on the NHMO skin (photo). Too fine to model at 15-60 m; at most a thin paler line along the tomium.
- **Bill angle at rest**: 33-57° below level on six floating birds, median about 45° (photo-measured). The head is carried with the bill well down.

### 5.2 Head

- "Gadwall have a fairly large, square head with a steep forehead" [AAB-ID]; "a relatively steep forehead rising above a narrow bill"; "The forehead may look more noticeably steep on the male Gadwall, which often keeps the head feathers more puffed out" [Kaufman 2020]. "A head crest, varying in color from gray to brown is usually present" (alternate male) [Oring 1968, p. 373]; "Head, with low crest" (male), "with slight crest" (female) [Kortright 1942, pp. 175-176].
- **Forehead angle** (photo-measured): the forehead profile over the first 10 mm above the culmen base rises **26°, 37° and 47°** above the line of the culmen extended back (`gadwal-f-1`, `-f-3`, `-f-2`; mean 37°). In the image the forehead is within 6-9° of vertical on all three while the bill points 34-57° down: the forehead rises almost straight up from the bill base. A Mallard's forehead slopes back from the bill instead (the Mallard document should give its angle; the test is in section 10).
- **Crown**: flat-topped, highest roughly above or just behind the eye, with an angular rear crown where it meets the nape: the "square" head (photos).
- **Eye**: centre **24-27 mm** from the culmen base (`gadwal-f-2` 24.3 mm, `gadwal-m-1` 26.9 mm), up and back from it. Visible eye about **6-8 mm** across on the male, 5-7 mm on the females (photo-measured). Iris dark brown [Oring 1968, p. 373].
- **Culmen / head** (head = feathered outline from the culmen base to the back of the head, along the bill): 0.55 (`gadwal-f-2`), 0.56 (`gadwal-m-harvest`), about 0.53 (`gadwal-pair-1` male). No live-bird calliper data exist for ducks in the USGS set; this is a feathered-outline value.

### 5.3 Neck and body on the water

- At rest the neck is drawn in, the head sitting over the front of the breast: the bill tip is only 25-45 mm ahead of the breast's most forward point (`gadwal-m-local` 25, `gadwal-m-1` 34, `gadwal-f-2` 45 mm; photo-measured). Alert birds stretch the neck up; feeding birds reach forward and down.
- The breast is full and round; its most forward point sits **just above the waterline**: 15-30 mm above the near-side waterline on six photos, 0-18 mm above the water plane once the camera's elevation is allowed for.
- The back line is almost level, rising slightly to the highest point over the tertials at about 35-70% of the body length from the breast, then running down into the long tertials and primaries. The male's long, pale tertials and scapulars make his back line higher and more peaked than the female's (photo).
- **Floating body length**, breast front to tail tip (photo-measured): **283** (`m-local`), **295** (`m-1`), **298** (`f-1`), **298** (`f-2`), **287** (`pair-1` male), 341 (`f-3`, neck stretched to drink), 221 (`m-harvest`, angled). Median of the first five **295 mm**.
- **Bill tip to tail tip at rest** (the model's `length`): **308** (`m-local`), **310** (`f-1`), **330** (`m-1`), **326** (`pair-1` male), **344** (`f-2`); 399 on `f-3` (head down) and 255 on the angled `m-harvest`. Median of the five **326 mm**, 64% of the published stretched length of 512 mm (computed).
- Walking on land the body is more compact: 268 mm breast to tail on `gadwal-m-standing` (photo-measured).

### 5.4 Tail

- 16 rectrices in 86% of 55 captives [Oring 1968, p. 373]; "tail-feathers 16" [Ridgway 1887, p. 94]. Ridgway's key separates the wigeons by "tail-feathers 14, the tail much graduated" [Ridgway 1887, p. 95]; none of the sources I read describes the Gadwall's tail shape beyond "Tail, brownish grey, narrowly edged with whitish" [Kortright 1942, p. 175]. On the floating photos the closed tail is pointed, the central feathers longest. Use `wedge` (**Estimate** from photos).
- **Carriage on the water**: 0-17° above level, median about 14° (`m-local` 17°, `m-1` 12°, `f-1` 14°, `f-3` about 15°, `f-2` 5° below level; photo-measured). "The Gadwall sits low and flat on the water, while the Baldpate rests buoyantly ... with tail held high" [Kortright 1942, p. 177].
- **Tail tip past the primary tips**: +2 mm (`m-1`), +14 mm (`f-3`), +21 mm (`f-2`), +22-29 mm (`m-local`) (photo-measured). The long tertials reach close to the primary tips on the male.

### 5.5 Folded wing on the water

- "When the bird is at rest, the wing is almost completely tucked in among the body plumage, buried under the scapular feathers from above and the breast feathers coming up from below" [Kaufman 2020]; "The flashy, white speculum is usually a poor identifying feature, seldom being visible when on the water, as the flight-feathers of the sitting bird are generally covered by the feathers of the sides" [Kortright 1942, p. 176].
- What shows (photos): the tertials (long, pointed, pale silver-grey on the adult male's alternate tertials, "Tertials of adult males are long, acutely pointed, silver-gray"; shorter, "silver-brown with cream colored tips" on the female [Carney 1992, p. 16]); the dark primary tips beyond them; and, on most birds, a **white bar** of the speculum between the tertials and the flank feathers.
- **The speculum on a floating bird** (photo-measured, four of six floating birds): a white bar **20-41 mm long and 4-10 mm high**, lying along the upper edge of the flank feathers just below the tertials, its front end **50-58%** and its rear end **59-65%** of the way from the breast to the tail tip, **25-40 mm** above the near-side waterline. Measured: `f-1` 38 × 6 mm at 50-63%; `f-3` about 28 × 4 mm at 51-59%; `f-2` about 20 × 10 mm (a triangle) at 58-65%; `m-harvest` 41 × 6 mm. Hidden on `m-1` and small on `gadwal-m-standing`. The iNaturalist tally (section 3.2) gives how often it shows.
- The side (flank) feathers cover the rest of the wing: on the photos their upper edge runs from the shoulder to the stern at about 55-65% of the height from the waterline to the top of the back (**Estimate** from photos).

### 5.6 Feet

- Webbed, three front toes joined by full webs; hind toe small, "hind toe without lobe" [Kortright 1942, p. 176]. Middle toe without claw 45-48 mm, longer than the 37-43 mm tarsus (section 2.1). On `gadwal-m-standing` the tarsus measures 39.6 mm and the middle toe to its tip about 44 mm (photo-measured).
- Colour: adult male "bright orange yellow, with blackish webs" [Kortright 1942, p. 175], "grayish yellow (occasionally straw-yellow or dusky)" [Oring 1968, p. 373]; female "dull yellow, webs, dusky" [Kortright 1942, p. 176], "straw-yellow to orange-brown; webs dusky" [Oring 1968, p. 373]; juveniles "grayish yellow" [Oring 1968, p. 373]. On the photos the male's legs and toes are bright orange with greyish webs.
- While floating the feet hang under the belly and are hidden; in clear water the orange feet show faintly just under the surface below the rear flank (`gadwal-m-1`, `gadwal-m-harvest`). On a tip-up one foot is often visible at the surface beside the upended body (`gadwal-tipup-1`).

## 6. Floating posture

### 6.1 Sources

"The Gadwall sits low and flat on the water, while the Baldpate rests buoyantly, seemingly more alert, with tail held high" [Kortright 1942, p. 177]; "Gadwall are dabbling ducks—they ride fairly high in the water" [AAB-LH]. The AAB line compares dabbling ducks with diving ducks; Kortright's compares the Gadwall with the wigeon. For scale: the Mallards in Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen's flume, 1081 g, "had a water-line length of 0·33 m" [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970, p. 771].

### 6.2 Heights above the waterline (photo-measured)

Method: heights are measured vertically from the **near-side waterline** (where the flank meets its reflection at mid-body), which is what a low observer sees as the waterline. Every photo was taken from somewhat above the water (the photographers stood on banks), which lowers the near-side waterline in the image by about (half-width) × sin(camera elevation) and so inflates these heights by roughly 5-20 mm; the corrected values follow the table. Heights are not changed by a bird turned about the vertical, so `gadwal-m-harvest` is usable here.

- **Where the waterline runs** (side view): from the lower front of the breast, just below its most forward point, along the lower flank to the stern, where the undertail coverts leave the water. Near-side waterline length 209-284 mm (photo-measured, median about 250 mm).

### 6.3 Body depth and draft

- **Body depth**, top of the back to the lowest point of the belly on a walking male: **115 mm** (`gadwal-m-standing`, scaled by the culmen and confirmed by its tarsus, 39.6 mm; photo-measured, one bird).
- **Draft from buoyancy** (computed): a floating bird displaces its own mass of water, 865 cm³ for 865 g. Taking the underwater hull as half an ellipsoid (volume coefficient π/6 = 0.52 of the enclosing box) with a waterline length of 0.23-0.26 m and a beam of 0.12-0.13 m (**Estimate**: no source gives a Gadwall's body width; the Mallard is wider), the draft is 865 / (0.52 × L × B) = **4.9-6.0 cm**.
- **Draft from the photos** (computed): body depth 115 mm minus the corrected freeboard of 47-74 mm gives **41-68 mm**.
- **Draft as a fraction of body depth: 0.45** (range 0.36-0.59). This is the block model's `draft` value unchanged. Both methods put the waterline a little below the middle of the body's depth.

### 6.4 Relaxed, alert, feeding

- **Relaxed or loafing**: neck drawn in, head over the breast, bill 35-57° down; back almost level; tail 0-15° up (photos above). Asleep, the head is turned back and the bill tucked into the scapulars (iNaturalist photos, section 3.2).
- **Alert**: the neck is stretched up and the head raised; the body line does not change (**Estimate** from photos; no source measures it).
- **Swimming**: as relaxed, the neck slightly forward; the head does not bob (none of the swimming birds in the video clips of section 7.4 pumps its head as a coot does).
- **Feeding**: surface dabbling with the bill at the water and the neck forward (`gadwal-f-3`: bill just lifted, head lowered, body level); head-dip with the head and neck under water and the rear body rising a little; tip-up with the body near vertical (section 7).

## 7. Swimming and feeding behavior

### 7.1 Cruising and turning

No study has measured a Gadwall's swimming speed. The Mallard, a close relative "which swims in exactly the same way" [Phillips 1923, p. 148], has:
- **Preferred speed 0.48 m/s**: "the observed ducks swam freely with a mean speed of 0·48 m/sec (range 0·45-0·51 m/sec) ... indistinguishable from the experimentally derived value of 0·50 m/sec for minimum cost of transport" (wakes of free-swimming Mallards on a pond) [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970, pp. 771-772].
- **Maximum sustained 0.70 m/s**, equal to the hull speed of a 0.33 m waterline: "The ducks were also unable to sustain swimming speeds in excess of 0·70 m/sec" [p. 766]; hull speed V = (gL/2π)^½ [p. 768]; "for short bursts, the ducks were able to swim in excess of their theoretical hull speed" [p. 771].
- **Below 0.35 m/s** they would not swim steadily in the flume: "they swam to the front of the chamber, drifted back to the rear, and then repeated the cycle" [p. 765].
- **Paddle rate** about constant: "Strokes/sec = 2·44 + ..." [p. 775, Fig. 7]; 2.65 ± 0.2 Hz at 0.3-0.6 m/s in a still-water tank, where "To move at a faster rate (as for escape), the ducks propelled themselves over the water using their wings in rapid rowing strokes" [Biewener & Corning 2001, p. 1748].
- For a Gadwall (computed, Prange's formula): the measured waterline of 0.21-0.28 m (median about 0.25 m, section 6.2) gives a hull speed of **0.57-0.67 m/s, about 0.62 m/s**; Prange's Mallard waterline (0.33 m) would give 0.72.
- Feeding Gadwalls move slowly: "foraging speed (metres per minute) of unpaired gadwalls was higher than that of paired birds"; "Foraging speed increased with declining food abundance" [McKnight 1998, abstract only; no numbers visible].
- Turning: no source measures it. On the video clips (section 7.4) swimming birds change heading smoothly while paddling, without stopping; I did not measure a rate. Keep the current `TURN` of 1.2 rad/s (69°/s) (**Estimate**).

### 7.3 Feeding modes and their frequencies

Definitions: *surface dabbling* (bill at or just under the surface, head up); *head-dip* (head and neck under, body level or tilted a little, tail not raised); *tip-up* (upending: body tilted steeply forward, tail up, head and neck reaching down).

- Szijj: "Haupternährungsform ist die Nahrungsaufnahme im Schwimmen mit Kopf unter Wasser ... Das Gründeln dauert etwa so lange wie bei der Krickente und reicht bis 42 cm tief" ("The main feeding method is feeding while swimming with the head under water ... Upending lasts about as long as in the Teal and reaches down to 42 cm") [Szijj 1965, p. 44]. The Teal's upends are "shorter than in other *Anas* species" [p. 43].
- In Alabama the Gadwalls fed on Eurasian watermilfoil that grew to the surface; surface-pecking fell and tip-ups rose as the vegetation near the surface was eaten [McKnight & Hepp 1998, pp. 675, 679]. So **the mix follows where the food is**: surface-pecking where plants reach the surface, head-dips where they are 10-30 cm down, tip-ups only for the deepest.
- The Gadwall rarely dives: "These birds never engage in true diving habits, except when wounded or before reaching maturity ... They feed by tipping up in the water exactly like a Mallard" [Phillips 1923, p. 148]; "tipping forward to feed on submerged vegetation without diving" [AAB-ID]; "Forages mainly while swimming by taking items from surface or by dabbling with head submerged, sometimes by up-ending, occasionally by diving" [Audubon]. Gadwalls were "not observed diving" in 147 h in Alabama [McKnight & Hepp 1998, p. 675].
- Reach: upends to **42 cm** [Szijj 1965, p. 44]; "about the maximum depth that Gadwalls could reach when tipping up": 30 cm [LeSchack & Hepp 1995, p. 431]. Mallards reach 48 cm [Szijj 1965, p. 46].
- **Against the Mallard** (the difference the game should show): the Mallard upends in 42% of feeding observations, the Gadwall in 13% or less.

**Video estimate: a Gadwall pair upending** ([SBBOT n.d.], "Bottom's Up": "Gadwall pair feeding in front of the hide at Sandwich Bay Bird Observatory's nature reserve Restharrow Scrape", Kent, England; CC BY; 1920 × 1080 at 30 fps, 76.3 s, one continuous take, the camera panning to follow). I extracted frames at 4 per second and noted for each bird whether it was upended (tail above the water, head and neck under), level, or between. Timings are ±0.25 s.
- **Upend duration** (first to last frame with the tail up): male 2.3-4.5 s, mean **3.5 s** (n = 9); female 1.5-4.3 s, mean **3.2 s** (n = 11); both **3.4 s** (1.5-4.5, n = 20). Three more upends were cut by the start and end of the clip, and the female made one half-upend (body at about 45°) of 1.75 s.
- **Between upends** the bird is level for **1.8-8.8 s, median 3.0 s** (n = 21; male median 3.3, female 2.5); the long gaps (5-9 s) include swimming a few body lengths. That is three times the Black Duck's 1.0 s pause [Brodsky & Weatherhead 1985, p. 73]: these Gadwalls surfaced, often made one or two short head-dips of 0.5-1 s with the bill and face in the water, and tipped up again.
- **Rate**: 8.7 (male) and 10.3 (female) upends a minute; each bird spent **47-53%** of the clip upended (computed).

The same is seen on `gadwal-tipup-1.jpg` and `-2.jpg`, whose photographer describes birds "tipping vertically" [Keith P 2022].
- **Feet**: the orange feet paddle visibly at the surface beside and behind the upended body in most upends (frames at 4.75-7.5 s, 12-14 s, 35-38 s, 64-66 s), which holds the bird vertical.
- The pair fed side by side, often upending together, 0.3-1.5 body lengths apart (frames).
- This is one pair in England in spring or winter (the date is not given) in shallow water; the Willamette birds will upend less when food is near the surface (section 7.3).

### 7.5 Food stealing (note only; do not design for it)

"Overall, 30% (n = 423) of Gadwalls observed were kleptoparasitic" on American Coots, October-February in Alabama, most in February when surface food was scarce [LeSchack & Hepp 1995, p. 429; McKnight & Hepp 1998, p. 675]. "They often follow American Coots, foraging on plants that have been brought up to the surface" [BirdWeb]; "They sometimes steal food from flocks of diving ducks or coots" [AAB-ID]. Szijj notes "Nahrungsparasitismus" (food parasitism) as relatively frequent [Szijj 1965, p. 44].

### 7.6 Flock, pairs, spacing

- Pairs form in autumn: "greater than 90% of females are paired by November, 4 to 5 months before breeding" [LeSchack et al. 2020, introduction]; "Gadwall pairs form during fall migration"; "virtually all females find mates by November" [AAB-LH]; "more than 80 percent of female Gadwalls already paired up by December" on the wintering grounds [Kaufman 2020]. So in late September some of the three birds may be a pair swimming together, the male close beside or just behind the female.
- Groups: "The Gadwall I have seen were always in very small groups, ranging from three or four to half a dozen" [Phillips 1923, p. 148]; "this species is rather unsociable in its habits" [p. 148]; "When migrating, it flies in small flocks of about a dozen birds" [Bent 1923, p. 83]. It associates with wigeon, pintail and coots [Bent 1923, p. 84; BirdWeb].
- Nearest-neighbour distances: no source. On the videos and photos (**Estimate**): a feeding pair stays **0.3-1.5 body lengths apart (about 0.1-0.45 m)**, often upending side by side [SBBOT n.d.; Haanpää n.d.-a]; the Kyoto pair swam about one body length apart (`gadwal-pair-1.jpg`); in a small group swimming and feeding close to shore, neighbours were 0.3-2 body lengths apart [Haanpää n.d.-b, "Gadwalls swimming"]. Birds that are not a pair space out more, 1-4 body lengths (0.3-1.2 m) (**Estimate**, same clips). In the Willamette photos, swimming pairs were about 2.5 and 3 body lengths apart (records 320600462 and 320568185) and a trio of two males and a female kept within 1-2 body lengths (246353350) (section 3.2).

**Where the Gadwall differs from the Mallard**, in short: fewer tip-ups (about 12% of feeding against 45%), shorter tip-ups (3.1 s against 4.2 s), more feeding with the bill at the surface and the head and neck under, a slightly slower and smaller hull, and a quieter, less gregarious flock of pairs and small groups. Both paddle the same way and neither pumps its head.

## 12. Open questions and confidence

**High confidence**
- Museum measurements: culmen 40-44.5 (male) and 37-42 (female), wing, tarsus and toe from Ridgway / Baird, Brewer & Ridgway and Phillips, with AVONET's six skins agreeing. Mass from Kortright's 223 birds and Lokemoen's 690.
- The Gadwall-to-Mallard ratios of section 2.5, because each pair of numbers comes from one author and method: a 10% smaller body with a 25% shorter, 20% narrower bill.
- The bill's planform: parallel-sided and round-tipped, width within ±1% from 13 mm behind the tip to the base, in two views of one skin that agree within 0.2 mm, and matching "the edges nearly parallel" [BBR 1884, p. 487].
- Moult timing from Oring (captive and wild birds of known age), Bent and Kortright: drakes moult out of eclipse from late August to November; juveniles start Alternate I at 65-80 days.
- Feeding-mode shares from Szijj (1965) and McKnight & Hepp (1998): the Gadwall upends far less than the Mallard and feeds mostly with the bill at the surface or the head and neck under water. The mix changes with where the food is.
- Kleptoparasitism of coots, pair formation in autumn.

**Medium confidence**
- **The late-September plumage mix** (93% of males in alternate; 47% males): 173 records and 249 sexable birds, but photographers favour close, bright males, near-full-alternate males with a few brown feathers pass for alternate at photo resolution, and 120 records were classed by a sub-agent.
- **Floating length and heights** (length 0.31-0.34 m; back 47-74 mm, crown 90-124 mm, tail tip 11-54 mm above the water; waterline over 75-85% of the body). Five to seven photos, each scaled by a sex-mean culmen (±5% per bird); all taken from above the water, corrected with an estimated camera elevation. The local October male, photographed from nearly water level, agrees.
- **Draft 0.45**: two independent estimates (depth minus freeboard, and buoyancy) agree at 0.36-0.59, but the body depth is from one walking male and the beam is an estimate.
- **Upend duration 3.1 s (1.1-4.5)**: 23 upends by four birds in two clips (one pair in England at an unknown season, one pair of unstated place and date); it fits Szijj's qualitative "about as long as in the Teal".
- The forehead angle (26-47°) and eye position: three photos each.
- The time budget for late September (about 45% feeding): Paulus's October figure from Louisiana, abstract only.

**Low confidence or not verified**
- **The harvested photo's body proportions**: `gadwal.jpg` gives a body 25% shorter than every other photo, so it is probably angled. Its heights agree with the others.
- **Nail size** (6-7 mm long, about 6.5 mm wide): the length from two side views at the limit of resolution, the width an estimate.
- **Body width** (0.12-0.13 m): no source; chosen to fit the buoyancy check.
- **Cruise speed 0.40 m/s**: no Gadwall measurement; scaled down from free-swimming Mallards (0.48 m/s) by the smaller hull.
- **Surface-dabbling and head-dip bout lengths, the foraging speed of 0.05 m/s and the spacing**: one or two short clips and a handful of photos.
- **Juvenile appearance**: one sharp late-September juvenile photo and the literature; ageing female-types from photos is uncertain (section 3.2).
- **Snippet-only claims**: none; the abstracts used are marked "abstract only".

**Sources I could not read** (not relied on for any number): Palmer 1976 (*Handbook of North American Birds*, vol. 2) and Pyle 2008 (lending only or not online); Cramp & Simmons 1977 (BWP vol. 1); the paywalled *Birds of the World* account's Appearance, Measurements and Behavior sections [LeSchack et al. 2020]; Paulus 1982, 1983 and 1984 beyond their abstracts; McKnight 1998 (*Can. J. Zool.*) beyond its abstract; Pöysä 1983 and 1987; Thomas 1982 beyond its abstract; Green 1998 (abstract only, and it has no Gadwall); Berthold 1961 and King 1962 (food parasitism and diving notes); Marshall et al. 2003 (*Birds of Oregon*) and eBird's Willamette bar charts (not reached; Oregon status rests on BirdWeb, the Willamette Valley Birding Trail checklist and iNaturalist).

**Worth doing if higher confidence is needed**
- Photograph floating Gadwalls from water level at Willow Lake's distances, beside an object of known size, to measure the floating length and freeboard without the culmen scale.
- Time Gadwall upends, head-dips and surface-dabbling bouts in the Willamette Valley in September, when the birds feed on late-summer vegetation.
- Read Paulus 1984 in full for the October budget by sex and pair status, and Pyle 2008 for measurements by age and sex.
- Measure the built model's waterline, draft and heights against section 9 before tuning the tests.
