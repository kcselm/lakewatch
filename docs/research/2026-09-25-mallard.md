# Mallard: measurements, late-September plumage, field marks, structure, floating posture, and dabbling

Date: 2026-09-25
Purpose: source data for the detailed Mallard (`mallar3`) model on the new swimming posture, its flat duck bill with a nail, its webbed feet, and the `dabbler` style on `swim`. The cast is four Mallards on `swim` in `near-water` (polygon [[-9,-10.5],[12,-10.5],[14,-21],[-11,-21]], 10-23 m from the vantage), `"plumage": "mixed"`, at Willow Lake, Willamette Valley, Oregon, on a morning of about 20-30 September. The player sees them by eye, through binoculars (7.5°) or the telescope (2°).

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt of a page I could not read; treat it as unverified. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo and scaled it by the published culmen (or, where stated, by a printed rule). "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated. "Video" marks numbers read off CC-licensed video by eye; treat them as estimates too.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements** (mm; section 2):
  - Live wild birds [Söderquist et al. 2014, Table 4]:
    - exposed culmen **55.1 ± 2.7 (M) and 50.8 ± 2.0 (F)**;
    - bill width at the nostrils **21.9 and 20.8**;
    - bill height at the nostrils **19.0 and 17.7**.
  - Mean of the sexes: culmen **53**, width **21.4**, height **18.3** (computed).
  - North American skins [Baird et al. 1884, p. 492]:
    - culmen 53-61 (M) and 51-60 (F);
    - tarsus 41-46 and 38-46;
    - middle toe 51-55 and 48-52;
    - wing 279-301 and 260-292.
  - Tail 87 (European skins) [Tobias et al. 2022].
  - Mass: adult males 1,261 g, immature males 1,174 g, adult females 1,084 g, immature females 1,034 g (Bellrose & Hawkins 1947, n = 402-730 per class) [Johnsgard 1975, p. 222].
- **Bill shape** (section 2.6):
  - **Top view**: nearly parallel-sided.
    - Narrowest a fifth of the way out from the base, at **0.86 of its maximum width**.
    - Widest 60-75% of the way out: **the slightly spatulate end**.
    - A slight flare at the feathered base (0.93).
    - Photo-measured on a CC BY museum skin lying beside a millimetre rule [NHMO 2024].
  - **Side view**: about 18.5 mm deep near the base, 12-15 mm at mid-bill and 7.5-8 mm at 80% of its length, then a small dark nail.
  - The culmen is straight to within about 1 mm, and the bill's width is **0.40-0.42 of the culmen**.
- **Late-September plumage** (section 3):
  - Adult drakes moult out of eclipse from late August to early November: 26 August-6 November in museum specimens of *Anas* [Pyle 2005, p. 214]. "Some birds regain their full plumage in October, but some not until November or even later" [Bent 1923, p. 41].
  - The curled uppertail coverts regrow straight in July and "commence, about August 15 ... to curl upwards"; "by September 25, they are usually quite complete" [Millais 1902, pp. 22-23].
  - **Willamette tally**: 120 of the 551 research-grade wild-type iNaturalist records from 11 September to 12 October, 459 birds classified.
    - Of 135 classifiable drakes, **65% are in breeding plumage, 24% in visible moult and 11% eclipse-like**.
    - The share in breeding plumage climbs from **27% in 11-18 September to 68% in 19-25 September, and to 86-87% after 25 September**.
    - Drakes are 57% of the classifiable wild-type birds.
    - Of the 17 breeding drakes whose stern could be judged, **12 had the curl**.
    - No bird could be aged as a juvenile.
- **Recommended variants and flock mix**:
  - **`male`**: breeding plumage, curl present.
  - **`maleMoult`**: a drake part-way out of eclipse. On the local photos the **body is nearly finished while the head lags**:
    - head speckled grey-brown, with green only on the crown and nape (0-60%);
    - no ring;
    - a yellow to olive-yellow bill;
    - no curl.
  - **`female`**: adult females and all juveniles.
  - **Flock of four: 1 `male`, 1 `maleMoult`, 2 `female`**. For a weighted spawner, the alternative is male 0.40, maleMoult 0.15, female 0.45 (section 3.3).
- **Female bill** (the Gadwall comparison; section 4.4):
  - Orange, with a **dark saddle along the culmen**:
    - it starts **8-20% of the way out from the base**;
    - it is **5-7 mm deep (about half the side) at 30-50%**;
    - it thins to 2.5-3.5 mm at 60-70%;
    - it is at most a hairline on the ridge from 80%.
  - The base, the lower side along the cutting edge and the distal fifth stay orange. The nail tip is dusky. Dark spots mark the basal third (photo-measured on two side-on females).
  - In the tally, **41% of readable female bills show this saddle**.
  - **37% are mostly dark**, with orange only at the base and edges. These are probably young birds, whose bills are "duller" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
  - 22% are mostly orange.
- **Speculum on the water**: "a poor identifying feature of a sitting duck as the feathers of the sides usually cover that part of the wings" [Kortright 1942, p. 151].
  - In the tally, **75% of floating birds show none of it, 14% a sliver and 11% a patch**.
  - On a side-on female the visible part is a **10 × 13 mm white fleck, 45 mm above the water**, at the rear lower edge of the folded wing (photo-measured).
- **Floating posture** (section 6; three strictly side-on photos):
  - back **77-83 mm** above the waterline;
  - crown **96-107 mm** at rest;
  - tail tip **29-40 mm**;
  - wingtips 42-64 mm;
  - the drake's curl top 57 mm;
  - waterline length 271-283 mm.
  - **Draft ≈ 5-6 cm** (computed from buoyancy): about **0.4 of the body depth** (≈ 13-14 cm).
- **Swimming** [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970, pp. 771-772, 775]:
  - Free-swimming Mallards chose **0.48 m/s** (0.45-0.51), the speed of minimum cost of transport.
  - The sustained maximum is 0.70 m/s.
  - The feet stroke at a steady **≈ 2.4 per second** at all speeds.
- **Feeding**:
  - On Lake Constance, **42% of feeding Mallards were upending and 44% had the head and part of the neck under water**; 7% fed at the surface [Szijj 1965, p. 50, Table 3].
  - Upends last **4.2 s on average and 5.8 s at most**, reaching 48 cm down [Szijj 1965, p. 46].
  - On CC video, tip-ups last 0.75-7.8 s (typically 2-5 s), with the body near vertical and the orange feet paddling above the surface (section 7).
- **Time budget** (diurnal scans, open water, winter, South Carolina, both sexes, n = 920): feeding 47%, swimming 31%, resting 7%, comfort (preening) 6.5%, alert 6% [Gray et al. 1996, p. 480, Table 1].
- **Tracing references** (scratchpad, not the repo):
  - `mallar3-male-side.jpg` (Stan Bousson/USFWS, public domain), `mallar3-female-side.jpg` and `mallar3-female-side-2.jpg` (Kathy Büscher, CC BY 2.0): the floating profile.
  - `mallar3-male-bill.jpg` (Alexis Lours, CC BY 4.0): the bill in side view.
  - `mallar3-bill-dorsal-nhmo.jpg` (NHMO, CC BY 4.0): the bill in top view.
  - Two local CC BY-NC photos of moulting drakes (section 8).

### 2.6 The bill in top view and side view (photo-measured)

**Top view.** No source gives the width along the bill. Baird et al. give only the genus shape: "the bill a little longer than the head or foot, rather broad, depressed, the edges parallel, the end rounded" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 489].

I measured the dorsal photograph of the Oslo skin **NHMO-BI-64916/1-P** (adult male, 15 February 1921, locality not given), which lies beside a QPcard 201 with a printed millimetre rule [NHMO 2024]:
- **Scale.** The rule's 10 and 110 mm ticks are 1,380 px apart: **13.8 px/mm** on the table plane. The bill lies a few centimetres nearer the camera, so absolute widths may read about 3-5% high (Estimate).
- **Method.** I thresholded the bill against the pale background and read its width every 10 px across the axis. The axis is tilted 7.5°, so the widths are multiplied by cos 7.5° (computed).
- **Positions are fractions, not millimetres.** The dorsal chord (tip to the feather edge on the midline) is 631 px = 45.7 mm, against a male culmen of about 55. The bill is pitched about 35° out of the table plane, which shortens its length but not its widths. So positions are given as **fractions of the chord, from the base (s = 0) to the tip (s = 1)**.

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

**Adult drakes.**
- **Bent:**
  - "The annual molts and plumages of the adult consist of a double molt of all the contour feathers, into the eclipse plumage in the summer and out of it again in the fall; the flight feathers are molted but once, while the drake is in the eclipse plumage, in August."
  - "I have seen drakes in full eclipse plumage as early as July 20, but usually it is not complete until August. It is worn for about a month, the earliest birds beginning to molt out of it in August. Some birds regain their full plumage in October, but some not until November or even later" [Bent 1923, pp. 40-41].
- **Pyle**, from museum specimens of 11 *Anas* species including the Mallard [Pyle 2005, pp. 210, 214]:
  - The wing moult of *Anas* "occurred from 6 July to 4 September".
  - The traditional "definitive prealternate" body moult "succeeded the wing molts, occurring from 26 August to 6 November in most *Anas* (N = 198)". It "was essentially complete in both sexes ... and included the acquisition of bright plumage in males".
  - The dates are the mean ± 2 SD of specimens in active moult, "to encompass an estimated 96% of the molting periods" [p. 210]. The window's midpoint is about 1 October (computed).
- **Millais**, from Scottish birds:
  - The change from eclipse into winter dress "goes on by regular steps from August 12 to September 30, when the whole process is completed"; "By October 10 the old drake has, to all intents and purposes, assumed his full winter plumage" [Millais 1902, pp. 22-23].
  - His plates of adult males are captioned "10. Colour change and moult commencing. September 10th" and "11. October 1st. 12. October 10th. Nearly complete. 13. October 20th. Complete winter dress" [Millais 1902, Plates II-IV].
- **Kortright**, for North American birds [Kortright 1942, p. 150]:
  - "Early in September, the male commences a continuous moult out of the female-like plumage of the eclipse, into the full winter plumage. By early October, the head shows a considerable amount of green, and new finely vermiculated feathers show among the scapulars; plain brown feathers appear on the back, and some black ones on the rump. The chest, breast, and sides begin to take on the general pattern of the full winter plumage, but in varying degree, the buffy-brown feathers of the eclipse plumage still predominate; the bill is olive green and the feet, dull reddish orange, with darker webs."
  - Later "the green of the head is completed, the buffy-brown feathers lingering longest on the cheeks; the white neck-ring becomes noticeable ... the bill becomes more yellowish ... Finally, the last of the buffy-brown feathers disappears from the chest, breast and sides. Some birds regain their full plumage in October, some not until November or even later".
- **Freshly finished drakes** differ from spring ones: in autumn the neck ring is "somewhat straggling and broad" and the chestnut chest feathers have white edging, both "toned down" by late winter [Millais 1902, p. 23].

**The drake curl.**
- **What curls:**
  - "Four central tail-feathers sharply upcurled and glossy black" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
  - "middle feathers of upper coverts curled upwards" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
  - "Two middle feathers black, slightly recurved; the two longer upper tail-coverts greatly recurved" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 492].
  - Millais, who followed the feathers through the moult, calls them "the new sets of long feathers covering the top of the tail" [Millais 1902, p. 22]: the central uppertail coverts.
- **Absent in eclipse**: "Curled central tail-feathers absent" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
- **Regrowth**:
  - The feathers "came in fresh and straight about July 6, commence, about August 15, whilst still in their brown colour, to curl upwards. After this date they grow rapidly and continue to curl, changing colour to jet black till, by September 25, they are usually quite complete" [Millais 1902, pp. 22-23].
  - His "single moult" plate draws them "taken—6, July 6th; 7, August 15th; 8, September 20th; 9, October 12th", showing how "They then turn up and over, at the same time changing colour until complete" [Millais 1902, plate caption between pp. 22 and 23; the plate number is illegible in the OCR].
- **Young males**: "the jet-black vent-feathers and black curly feathers above the tail are often amongst the last to come into the plumage" [Millais 1902, p. 26].

**Juveniles.**
- **Bent:** "In this juvenal plumage the sexes are practically indistinguishable, though the male is slightly larger and has a larger bill. In this plumage the young birds resemble the adult female to a certain extent, but they are darker and more brownish, especially on the chest and back ... From this time on the sexes differentiate rapidly ... by a continuous molt ... The result is that the young birds have assumed by December, or when about 6 months old, a plumage which is practically the same as that of the adults" [Bent 1923, p. 40].
- **Phillips:**
  - "Young Female in First (Juvenal) Plumage: Very similar to the adult female, but lower surface always has a 'stripey' or streaked appearance; upper surface may be difficult to distinguish from that of adult female. Bill and legs duller in color, and tail feathers blunt at the tips."
  - "Young Male in First Plumage: Similar to young female on lower surface, but constantly darker and somewhat more uniform above ... the bill is clear olive green, not mottled" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
- **Kortright:**
  - "By September, the plumage of both sexes resembles that of the adult female, but is darker and more brownish, especially on back and chest, feathers of chest lack central markings of chest-feathers of adult female; breast and belly streaked with brown. Young male shows vermiculated feathers on flanks. Young males are larger than young females, and have larger bills, lacking spotting of female bill" [Kortright 1942, p. 150].
  - The adult female's chest feathers "show distinct buffy and also brown U-shaped markings (lacking in juveniles of both sexes)" [p. 150].
- **Millais:**
  - Young males change "from about August 12 to November 12". "The bill and legs of the young male change gradually from pea-green to a bright orange, and by the end of November it is generally impossible to tell the young males from the old ones" [Millais 1902, pp. 25-26].
  - Immature females are told by "the dull colour of the bill and legs, and the narrow, smaller, and more closely ribbed mottling ... the breast markings of the immatures are always indecisive and 'stripey'" [p. 26].
  - His plate of young males' breasts shows "6, Sept. 30th, Early hatched bird" against "7. Sept, 30th. Late hatched bird", with "8. Oct. 10th. Showing head moulting last" [Millais 1902, Plate VIII].
- **Pyle:** the first prealternate body moult of *Anas* ran "from 2 September to 16 November in most (eight) species (N = 38)" and replaced on average 72% of body feathers in males [Pyle 2005, pp. 213-214].
- **Timing of young in the Northwest:** Oregon and Washington egg dates run from 17 March to 2 July [Bent 1923, p. 47]. Incubation averages 28 days, and fledging takes 55-59 days [Johnsgard 1975, p. 227]. A clutch completed on 2 July fledges in late September (computed).
- **The tail as an age mark:** juveniles keep notched tail tips until at least "the first week in September", and "many individuals ... do not complete the tail moult until late in the autumn" [Kortright 1942, p. 31]. This is invisible at game range.
- **At 10-23 m a juvenile of either sex reads as a female:** mottled brown with a dark eye-stripe. The differences need a close view:
  - a streaked rather than U-marked breast;
  - a darker back;
  - a duller or olive bill in young males;
  - flank vermiculation starting on early young males.

**Adult females.**
- Their wing moult comes later: "Females usually do not begin their wing molt until they have abandoned their well-grown broods, and thus the peak of their flightless period occurs more than a month after that of males" [Johnsgard 1975, p. 233].
- Their autumn body moult has "no great contrasts in colour to be overcome", so "the old feathers in August and September fall out, and the new ones come in directly" [Millais 1902, p. 26].
- A late-September female looks like a normal mottled female.

### 3.3 Recommendation

**Three variants.**
- **`male`: adult drake in breeding plumage, curl present.** 65% of all drakes in the window, and 70-85% around the game's date.
- **`maleMoult`: a drake part-way out of eclipse, as the local birds show it.**
  - Grey flanks with scattered brown feathers.
  - Chestnut breast, mottled and pale-fringed.
  - Black stern and white tail edge.
  - **Head speckled grey-brown with a dark eye-stripe, and green only on the crown and nape.**
  - **No ring, no curl; bill yellow to olive-yellow.**
  - It also stands for the eclipse-like birds (11%), which differ mainly in having less green on the head.
- **`female`: adult female.** It also stands for juveniles of both sexes, which a birder cannot tell from females at 10-23 m.
- **Not modelled**:
  - full eclipse drakes: rare by the game's date, and female-like apart from the bill;
  - identifiable juveniles;
  - domestic types.

**Flock of four: 1 `male`, 1 `maleMoult`, 2 `female`.**
- Two drakes and two female-types matches the local 54-57% drakes.
- One of the two drakes in moult slightly over-represents moult: around the game's date 15-30% of drakes are moulting, against 50% here. It is kept because it shows the player the late-September bird the brief asks about, and a birder at Willow Lake on 25 September would often see one.
- **For a weighted spawner**, use **male 0.40, maleMoult 0.15, female 0.45**. This is computed from the drakes of 19-30 September (69-86% breeding) and 54-57% drakes overall, and it gives at least one moulting drake in about half of all flocks of four (computed).
- **Today the spawner picks uniformly among variants**, a third each. That would make a third of the flock moulting drakes and a third breeding drakes, so the fixed mix or the weights should replace it.

## 4. Field marks of each recommended plumage

These are colour words from the sources; I have not invented hex values. "(photo)" marks my observation on the reference photos in section 8.

### 4.1 `male`: adult drake, breeding (alternate) plumage

- **Head and neck:**
  - "rich metallic green" [Phillips 1923, p. 4]; "rich iridescent green, with purplish gloss in certain lights ... crown, blackish green" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
  - In the field, "the dark, often apparently black, head color of the male is evident" [Johnsgard 1975, p. 222].
  - The green shifts to blue-violet on the crown and nape in some lights (photo).
- **Neck ring:**
  - "a well-marked white ring" [Phillips 1923, p. 4]; "narrow white collar, incomplete behind" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
  - **About 2-3 mm deep** on the side of the neck (photo-measured on `mallar3-male-bill.jpg` and `mallar3-male-side.jpg`).
  - It lies at the base of the green, where the head meets the breast. With the neck drawn in on the floating male, that is 20-26 mm above the water (photo-measured).
- **Breast:**
  - "rich chestnut" [Phillips 1923, p. 4]; "rich purple chestnut, almost meeting on lower hindneck, feathers with lighter edgings which tend to wear off towards spring" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
  - The chestnut covers the front from the ring down to the waterline. It ends in a clean edge against the grey flank, about level with the front of the folded wing (photo).
- **Flanks and belly:**
  - "sides, breast, belly and flanks, white, finely vermiculated with dusky, producing silvery-grey effect" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
  - "More than any other dabbling duck, male mallards are dark at both ends and light in the middle" [Johnsgard 1975, p. 222].
- **Back and scapulars:**
  - "Mantle and scapulars gray, more or less mixed with brown, and minutely freckled and barred with blackish. Upper back dark brown to blackish" [Phillips 1923, p. 4].
  - "scapulars, greyish white, outer ones chestnut brown, all finely vermiculated with dusky" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
  - On the water, the folded scapulars and tertials form a pale grey-brown panel along the top of the flank, with a darker chestnut-brown band along its lower edge (photo, `mallar3-male-side.jpg`).
- **Stern:**
  - "rump and upper tail-coverts black, with rich purple and green reflections. Under tail-coverts purplish black" [Phillips 1923, p. 4].
  - The tail is "ashy colored with the margins of the feathers white" [p. 5], "outer feathers mostly white" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
  - On the water: a black stern with a white band along the tail, and above it the black curl, two loops about 20 mm high (photo-measured).
- **Tertials:** "long and broad, grey sometimes tipped with whitish or buffy, chestnut brown on outer webs" [Kortright 1942, p. 149]. The dark primary tips lie over the stern (photo).
- **Bill:**
  - "brilliant lemon yellow to greenish yellow; nail black" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
  - "yellow, sometimes dusky on ridge, earlier in season olive or greenish yellow" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
  - "olive-yellow or ochraceous-olive (in life), the nail black" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 492].
- **Iris:** "dark brown" [Phillips 1923, p. 5]; "hazel" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 492].
- **Legs and feet:** "coral red, more brilliant when the bird is in perfect condition" [Phillips 1923, p. 5]; "bright reddish orange or yellowish, earlier in season, duller reddish orange" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].

### 4.2 `female`: adult female (also juveniles at game distance)

- **Body:**
  - "General color dark brown above, with feathers of mantle, scapulars and back edged with buff or white. The under surface brown, more or less streaked with black, but here there is great variation" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
  - "Entire body variegated and streaked with dusky brown and tawny, having yellowish-brown edges to the feathers ... breast and belly, lightest" [Kortright 1942, p. 150].
  - On the water: dark-centred feathers with broad buff edges, in bold chevrons and scallops along the flank and back (photo).
- **Head:**
  - "buff, streaked with brown, more darkly and heavily streaked on crown; cheeks, paler; chin and throat, buffy, without streaks; dark streak through eye" [Kortright 1942, p. 150].
  - "a definitely striped head, with a dark crown and eye-stripe, contrasting with pale cheeks and a light superciliary stripe" [Johnsgard 1975, p. 222].
  - The eye-stripe runs from the bill base through the eye to the rear of the head (photo).
- **Tail:**
  - "greyish brown, darker in middle, pale, almost whitish on margins" [Kortright 1942, p. 150]. In the field, "the whitish tail and the two white bars of the speculum are distinctive" [p. 151].
  - On the water, the pale tail edges show as a whitish wedge at the stern (photo).
- **Tertials:** plain grey-brown, long, lying over the rear flank. A white fleck shows at the lower edge of the folded wing, where the speculum's rear bar lies (section 4.5).
- **Legs and feet:** "pale orange red" [Kortright 1942, p. 150].
- **Iris:** "dark brown" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
- **Bill:** section 4.4.

### 4.3 `maleMoult`: drake part-way out of eclipse (late September)

**The eclipse plumage he is leaving:**
- "Similar in general appearance to adult female, but darker on the upper side, without the characteristic light edging of the feathers. Pileum darker and more iridescent. Usually some admixture of previous plumage, or beginnings of autumn plumage. Curled central tail-feathers absent. Lower surface with the chin and throat more spotted than in the female and the upper breast darker and ruddier" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
- "the bill is dull olive green without spotting" [Kortright 1942, p. 150].
- "Nonbreeding male is browner, like a female, but retains its black-and-white tail and often shows at least a trace of green on the head" [AAB-M gallery caption].

**How the sources describe the moult in:**
- First green on the head, then grey vermiculated feathers among the scapulars and black on the rump. In early October the chest and sides are still mostly buffy-brown [Kortright 1942, p. 150].
- The chestnut of the upper chest arrives as a tint that deepens through September, while "the spotted feathers all down the breast ... gradually lose their spots and change to the grey ones of the full winter dress" [Millais 1902, p. 22].

**What the local photos show** (section 3.2) is often the reverse order: **the body is nearly finished while the head lags.**
- Head: speckled grey-brown with a dark crown and eye-stripe; green only on the crown and nape (0-60%); no ring.
- Body: breast chestnut, mottled or pale-fringed; flanks grey with a scatter of brown feathers; stern black with a white tail edge; no curl.
- Bill: yellow to olive-yellow.
- Kortright's buff-bodied, green-headed stage is less common in the sample. Both orders occur; Millais says of young males that "no man can say which part of his clothing he will change first" [Millais 1902, p. 25].

**At 15 m**, it reads as a pale-bodied drake with a dull brown head and no ring. It is neither the clean dark-ended, light-middled breeding male nor the evenly mottled female.

**Soft parts:** in eclipse the bill fades "to a dull greenish-yellow", and the legs to "a dull greenish yellow with black webs, only gradually resuming their former brilliance when the full winter plumage is nearing completion" [Millais 1902, p. 23].

**Painting it** (Estimate, from the photos above): take the male's layer list and change it:
- Head: speckled grey-brown with a darker crown and eye-stripe; a green wash on the crown and nape only, the top 30-40% of the head.
- No ring.
- Breast: chestnut with brown scallops over it.
- Flank: grey, with brown scalloped feathers over about 20-30% of it.
- Bill yellow-olive, no curl, legs dull orange.

### 4.4 The female bill (the Gadwall comparison)

**Sources:**
- "Bill dull orange, irregularly splotched, especially about nostrils with blackish; nail black" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
- "orange to greenish olive, with brown or dusky on ridge, spotted with darker ... nail, dusky" [Kortright 1942, p. 150].
- "orange-and-black bill" [AAB-M gallery caption]; "Females and juveniles are mottled brown with orange-and-brown bills" [AAB-M].
- Johnsgard's field mark for the pair: females "may be recognized by the combination of their fairly large size and their orange yellow bill, which is distinctly heavier and more orange than that of a female gadwall" [Johnsgard 1975, p. 222].

**Where the dark sits.** Photo-measured on the two side-on females, `mallar3-female-side.jpg` and `mallar3-female-side-2.jpg`, each bill rotated to horizontal on a millimetre grid. Positions are along a 50.8 mm culmen, from the feathered base.

| Along the bill | 0-8% / 0-20% (0-4 / 0-10 mm) | 20% | 30% | 40-50% (20-25 mm) | 60% | 70% | 80% | 85-95% | 97-100% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dark on the upper mandible, depth below the culmen | none: orange base, a few dark spots | 1.5-2 mm (the saddle starts; the nostril sits in it) | 1.7-5.2 mm | **5.7-6.8 mm**, about half the side; ragged lower edge | 3.4-3.5 mm | 2.4-2.6 mm | ≈ 1 mm line on the ridge | none | dusky to black tip, 1-3 mm |
| Orange | whole depth | lower 90% | lower 70-90% | **lower half, to the cutting edge** | lower 70% | lower 75-80% | lower 90% | whole depth | - |

- Scattered dark spots 1-2 mm across sit on the orange 5-10 mm below the culmen, over the basal third (both photos).
- **So the saddle runs from 4-10 mm to about 40 mm from the base.** It is deepest 15-25 mm from the base, where it covers the upper half of the bill's side, and fades to a hairline by about 80%.

**Variation.**
- In the Willamette sample (section 3.2), 21 of 51 readable female bills showed this saddle.
- 19 were **mostly dark**: dark grey over most of the upper mandible, orange only at the base, along the cutting edge and sometimes at the tip. These fit the sources' duller-billed young females (e.g. 103852193, 402306727, 183758516).
- 11 looked mostly orange, several of them on low-resolution photos.
- "Irregularly splotched" [Phillips 1923, p. 5] and Kortright's "orange to greenish olive" both allow a range.
- **The model's `female` should carry the saddle**: it is the commonest pattern and the one the field guides teach.

**Against the Gadwall** (from the sibling Gadwall document, `2026-09-25-gadwall.md`):
- The female Gadwall's dark runs "unbroken from the feathers to the nail", about 3.5 mm deep along the ridge, and the orange is a neat panel on the lower side.
- **The Mallard's dark is a saddle that stops well short of the tip, leaving an orange base and an orange end.** The dark-billed young Mallards are the confusing ones.
- The Mallard's bill is also about a quarter longer and 20% wider: culmen 53 against 40-42.5 mm (computed).

### 4.5 The speculum

- "Speculum rich purple bordered anteriorly with a narrow black, and then a broad white bar, both on the coverts; bordered posteriorly by similar bars at tips of secondaries" [Phillips 1923, p. 5].
- "secondaries, with speculum of iridescent purplish blue, bordered in front and behind with black and white bars" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
- The same in both sexes ("Wings as in the above") [Baird et al. 1884, p. 490].
- **Size:** in North American males the violet is "1.50 or more" inches wide, at least 38 mm [Baird et al. 1884, p. 493].
- **On a floating bird:** "the white bars of the speculum are sometimes noticeable"; "The speculum is, however, a poor identifying feature of a sitting duck as the feathers of the sides usually cover that part of the wings" [Kortright 1942, p. 151].
- **The tally agrees.**
  - 110 floating birds could be assessed in 85 of the records: **83 (75%) showed none, 15 (14%) a sliver and 12 (11%) a patch**. 4 of the 12 patches were on birds with the wing loosened, raised or bathing.
  - The other 35 records show the same pattern.
  - Standing and walking birds usually show the speculum.
- **On the side-on female** `mallar3-female-side.jpg` the visible part is a **white fleck about 10 × 13 mm** (the rear white bar). It sits at the lower edge of the folded wing, **45 mm above the water and 44% of the body length forward of the tail tip** (photo-measured). On the floating male photos none of it shows.
- **For the model:** paint the full speculum on the folded wing, but let the flank feathers hide all of it except a 10-15 mm fleck of the rear white bar, and at most a sliver of blue, at the wing's rear lower edge. It shows fully in flight and when the bird preens or stretches.

## 5. Structure

- **Overall:**
  - "Mallards are large ducks with hefty bodies, rounded heads, and wide, flat bills. Like many 'dabbling ducks' the body is long and the tail rides high out of the water, giving a blunt shape. In flight their wings are broad and set back toward the rear" [AAB-M].
  - "A fairly large duck, noticeably larger than teal but much smaller than a Canada Goose" [AAB-M].
- **Head** (photo-measured unless noted):
  - Rounded, with the crown's highest point **about 15 mm behind the eye** (on `mallar3-male-side.jpg`).
  - **The forehead slopes.** Over the first 15-20 mm above the culmen base it rises only **13-19° more steeply than the culmen line extended backward**, then curves over the crown (the two male photos). For comparison, the Gadwall document measured 26-47° for that species, whose forehead rises almost straight up from the bill.
  - The rear of the head is full and rounds into the nape. Bill base to the rear of the head: 72 mm (horizontal, feathered outline).
  - Eye centre 76 mm from the bill tip, 22 mm behind and above the culmen base. The visible eye is about 7 mm across with its dark rim; the iris about 5.5 mm.
- **Bill** (section 2.6):
  - Long, flat and broad; "about as long as head" [Kortright 1942, p. 149].
  - Nearly parallel-sided, slightly widened toward the rounded end.
  - Straight culmen, small dark nail.
- **Neck:**
  - Thick.
  - At rest it is drawn down, so the head sits close over the breast. On the floating male the chin is about 25 mm above the water (photo-measured).
  - Extended upward when alert (section 6.3).
- **Body on the water** (photo):
  - Long and boat-shaped.
  - The back is nearly level: highest over the rear flank, falling slightly toward the stern and the neck.
  - The breast is a full curve from the neck base down to the waterline. Its lowest forward point is only 0-16 mm above the water (photo-measured).
  - The stern rises in a blunt end: black on the male, brown on the female.
- **Tail:**
  - About 87 mm long [Tobias et al. 2022].
  - On the water it is held **level to about 20° above level**: 18-19° on the male and the relaxed female, nearly level on the swimming female (photo-measured).
  - The tip is 29-40 mm above the water and **7-23 mm beyond the wingtips** (photo-measured).
  - Shape: **`rounded`** (Estimate). The white outer feathers make a rounded fan in flight photos; no source states the shape.
  - The drake's curled coverts rise from the rump in front of the tail as two black loops about **20 mm high**, their top **57 mm above the water** (photo-measured on `mallar3-male-side.jpg`).
- **Folded wing on the water:**
  - The flank feathers cover the lower part of the wing and all of the speculum except a small rear fleck (section 4.5).
  - What shows: the scapulars and tertials as a long panel along the top of the flank, and the dark primary tips over the stern.
  - The primary tips are **42-64 mm above the water**, ending 7-23 mm short of the tail tip (photo-measured).
  - The wrist is hidden under the feathers at the side of the breast.
- **Feet:**
  - Three front toes, fully webbed.
  - The hind toe is small, raised and "without lobe": the first key character separating the dabblers from the diving ducks [Kortright 1942, pp. 150-151].
  - The middle toe is 48-55 mm against a tarsus of 38-46 mm [Baird et al. 1884, p. 492]: about 1.2 times the tarsus (computed).
  - Colour: coral red to orange in the breeding male, duller in the female, dull greenish yellow with black webs in eclipse [Phillips 1923, p. 5; Millais 1902, p. 23].
  - While floating, the feet hang below the hull, out of sight: see the clear-water video [Jake's Place 2011], and one foot showing through the water under the rear flank on `mallar3-male-side.jpg`.
  - When the bird upends, they show above the surface, paddling (section 7).

### 6.2 Draft (computed)

The water hides the hull, and no source gives a Mallard's draft. From buoyancy:
- **Volume.** A floating bird displaces its own mass of water: **1.15 L** for the mean of the sexes (1.03-1.26 kg, section 2.4).
- **Hull shape.** Treat the underwater hull as half an ellipsoid with waterline length L, beam B and draft d: V = (π/6)·L·B·d, so d = V / (0.524·L·B).
- **Length.** L = 0.28 m (photo, section 2.7); Prange's birds had 0.33 m.
- **Beam.** The Oslo skins measure **137-140 mm** across the body in dorsal and ventral view, on the table-plane scale (photo-measured on NHMO images 14188761, 14188762, 14188767, 14188774). In life the width may be about 5% less (Estimate): **B ≈ 0.13-0.14 m**.
- **Result.** **d = 1.15 / (0.524 × 0.28 × 0.135) ≈ 5.8 cm; with L = 0.33 m, 4.9 cm.** For males and females together the range is about **4.5-6.5 cm**.
- A snippet says "Body density and buoyancy of ducks were calculated to be near 0.7 g ml−1" (after Lovvorn & Jones 1991) (Snippet; not used).

**Body depth**, back to keel: 80 mm above the water plus 50-58 mm below, **≈ 13-14 cm**.
- As a check, two standing drakes photographed three-quarter-on measure **125 and 131 mm** from the top of the back to the lowest point of the belly (photo-measured on `Male mallard duck 2` and `Stockente (68)`, scaled by the culmen).
- **So the draft is about 0.40-0.43 of the body depth.** The block model's `draft: 0.4` agrees.

**Where the waterline crosses the body in side view** (photo):
- low on the breast, where the forward curve comes down to within 0-16 mm of the water;
- along the lower flank, where the flank feathers meet the water in a nearly straight line;
- under the stern, where the black (male) or pale (female) undertail meets the water just ahead of the tail.

The belly is entirely under water.

### 6.3 Relaxed, alert and feeding

- **Relaxed / loafing:** the neck drawn in, the head resting low over the breast, the crown about 96-107 mm up (the table), the body level. Sleeping birds turn the head back and lay the bill in the scapulars (tally photos).
- **Alert:** the neck stretched up, the body level. The crown height was not measured on a side-on floating photo. **Estimate: 1.3-1.5 times the rest height, 130-150 mm**, from the visible length of the neck.
- **Swimming:** the body level, the neck slightly forward (`mallar3-female-side-2.jpg`: the bill only 11° below level). The tail tip stays low: 29 mm on the swimming female.
- **Head-dip** (Szijj's feeding "im Schwimmen mit Kopf unter Wasser", while swimming with the head under water):
  - The body stays level or tips forward slightly; the head and part of the neck go under [Szijj 1965, p. 40].
  - On video, the tail rises a little and more of the stern shows (Video, section 7).
- **Tip-up:** the body swings to vertical.
  - Szijj: "stellt sich durch Nach-vorn-Kippen zur Wasseroberfläche senkrecht, wobei die vordere Körperhälfte untertaucht und die hintere in die Luft ragt" (it stands vertically by tipping forward, the front half of the body under water and the rear half sticking up into the air) [Szijj 1965, p. 40].
  - On video, the body axis is 75-90° from level, and the tail, the undertail and the paddling feet stand above the water (Video).
  - Mallards upend to reach **48 cm** down [Szijj 1965, p. 46]. With a stretched length of 55-59 cm (section 2.4), only about 10-15 cm of the bird then stands above the water: the tail and the rear of the body (computed).
  - The feet, near the rear of the hull, paddle at or just above the surface (Video).

## 7. Swimming and feeding behavior

### 7.1 Swimming

- **Cruising speed** [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970, p. 771]:
  - "the observed ducks swam freely with a mean speed of 0·48 m/sec (range 0·45-0·51 m/sec)", "indistinguishable from the experimentally derived value of 0·50 m/sec for minimum cost of transport". The speeds were computed from the wavelength of the bow wave of free-swimming Mallards crossing "a large pond".
  - The maximum sustained speed in a flume was 0.70 m/s, close to the calculated hull speed of 0.71 m/s. "for short bursts, the ducks were able to swim in excess of their theoretical hull speed".
- **Paddling:**
  - "The duck ... was observed to paddle at a constant stroke rate at all swimming speeds" [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970, p. 772].
  - The regression in their Fig. 7 is "Strokes/sec = 2·44 + (1·14 × 10⁻ⁿ × swimming speed, m/sec)" with a negligible slope; the exponent is too small to read on the scan. The plotted points lie between about 2.1 and 2.9 strokes/s from 0.15 to 0.7 m/s [p. 775]. So **≈ 2.4 strokes per second**.
  - In vivo recordings give limb cycle frequencies of "swimming 2.65 versus terrestrial 2.61 Hz" [Biewener & Corning 2001, abstract].
  - "darters and mallards use alternate paddling both on the surface and underwater" [Csillag & Ribak 2025, Discussion, citing Townsend 1909 and Taylor-Burt 2020].
  - The feet are hidden while floating, so this matters only for the wake and ripple rhythm, and for the visible feet during a tip-up.
- **Turning:** no source. On video a drake swimming slowly turned about 180° in 3-4 s [Crowder 2017, 0.5-4.0 s]: **about 45-60°/s** at low speed (Video). Today's `TURN = 1.2 rad/s` (69°/s) is close.
- **Wake:** a V-wake and ripple rings follow a cruising bird (all swimming clips).

### 7.2 Feeding modes

**Szijj 1965** (Ermatinger Becken, Lake Constance) is the only study I found that gives the Mallard's mix of feeding modes. "Die einzelnen Individuen sind nach der Art ihrer Nahrungsaufnahme in täglichen Querschnitten ausgezählt": individuals were counted by feeding type in daily cross-sections, as percentages within each species [Szijj 1965, p. 50, Table 3].

| Feeding mode (Szijj's definition, pp. 40-41) | Mallard, % of feeding birds |
|---|---|
| 1. Diving | 1.5 |
| 2. Upending ("Gründeln": vertical, front half under, rear in the air; "Das Gleichgewicht wird durch Fuß- und Schwanzbewegungen gehalten", balance kept by foot and tail movements) | **42** |
| 3. Feeding while swimming with the head under ("nur der Kopf und ein Stück des Halses taucht unter, ohne bedeutendere Ortsbewegung. Oft ist damit kräftiges Treten verbunden": only the head and part of the neck under, without much movement, often with vigorous treading that stirs up the mud) | **44** |
| 4. From the surface ("taucht nur den Schnabel ins Wasser, öfters mit verschieden schneller Ortsbewegung gekoppelt": only the bill in the water, often while moving) | 7 |
| 5. On foot | 1.5 |
| 6. Chasing insects | 4 |

- **Upending time and reach.** "Stockenten können ziemlich lange gründeln; Durchschnittsdauer 4,2, Höchstdauer 5,8 Sekunden. Sie reicht dabei 48 cm tief" (Mallards can upend for quite long: mean 4.2 s, maximum 5.8 s, reaching 48 cm deep) [Szijj 1965, p. 46].
- **Modes grade into each other.** A duck "erst schwimmend unter der Wasseroberfläche sucht (3), dann sich schräg zur Wasseroberfläche stellt und allmählich ins Gründeln übergeht (2, Stockente)": it first searches while swimming with the head under, then tilts and gradually passes into upending. The Mallard is Szijj's example [p. 41].
- **Diving is rare.** "Die Tauchleistungen werden recht einheitlich angegeben: Tiefe 1-2 m, Tauchdauer maximal 10 Sekunden" (reported dives are fairly consistent: depth 1-2 m, duration at most 10 s) [Szijj 1965, p. 46]. "The mallard is not a diving duck and ordinarily does not go below the surface of the water" [Bent 1923, p. 44]. Not modelled.
- **Other sources:**
  - food "is picked up on or above the surface or obtained by partial immersion in shallow water" [Bent 1923, p. 41];
  - "tipping-up is the usual manner of foraging" [Johnsgard 1975, pp. 228-229];
  - "they feed in the water by tipping forward and grazing on underwater plants. They almost never dive" [AAB-M].
- **Lab bouts** (captive Mallards, wheat at 5 or 35 cm) [Guillemain et al. 2001, p. 348] (text extract):
  - "shorter feeding bouts (deep: 2.3 s ± 0.1 SE, N = 9; shallow: 22.2 s ± 1.8 SE, N = 8)";
  - deep feeders scanned 12.2 times a minute, shallow feeders 2.6.
  - So shallow feeding, with only the bill under and the eyes above water, goes on for tens of seconds; deep feeding comes in short bouts.

**Video** (by eye; frames at 2-6 per second):
- **[Little Known Factz 2024]**: a female and several drakes in a shallow pond, Massachusetts, 1 March 2024, 4K at 60 fps.
  - Female tip-ups: **4.2 s** (3.5-7.75 s), **3.25 s** (12.5-15.75 s), **5.0 s** (21.25-26.2 s), and at least 2.8 s (30.5 s to the cut).
  - Between them the female was level for **4.3-5.5 s**, including head-dips of 0.5-1.5 s and surface feeding with the bill at the water.
  - A drake stayed up **7.8 s** without a break (24.0-31.8 s, checked at 6 frames per second), his orange feet paddling above the surface for much of it. Another drake's tip-up lasted at least 7.5 s (0-8.5 s; not checked frame by frame).
  - Going down took 0.2-0.5 s; coming up 0.2-0.3 s.
  - In the second scene, four drakes fed together, tipping up and head-dipping within about one body length of each other.
- **[Gómez 2012]**: four drakes and a female in a park pond in November, 720p at 24 fps.
  - Tip-ups: 3.25-3.5 s, about 3 s, about 2 s, 1.75-2 s; one by the female ≈ 0.75 s.
  - The head was up for 0.75-2 s between tip-ups.
  - The body swings to vertical in about 0.25-0.5 s, the tail pointing straight up and the feet out of the water, paddling.
  - A second drake fed with the body level and the head and neck under at the same time (3.25-10 s).
- **Together**, the video tip-ups ran **0.75-7.8 s, typically 2-5 s**, which matches Szijj's 4.2 s mean. The 7.8 s drake exceeds his 5.8 s maximum.

**Surface dabbling:** no rate was measured.
- The bill is held at the surface, often while the bird swims slowly [Szijj 1965, pp. 40-41].
- On the tally photos, surface-dabbling birds swim with the bill tip in the water.
- **Estimate:** a slow swim at 0.05-0.15 m/s with the bill touching the surface. The bill's small rapid movements are not visible at game range.

### 7.4 Spacing

- No source gives nearest-neighbour distances on the water.
- **Video** (perspective ignored):
  - Feeding birds were **within 0-1 body length (0-0.35 m)** of each other. A pair touched as they head-dipped side by side, and four drakes tipped up within about a body length [Little Known Factz 2024, 3 s and 34-48 s].
  - A travelling pair swam in line about 10-15 body lengths apart [Crowder 2017, 9.5-14.5 s].
- **Tally photos:** Mallards were alone in 45% of records, in twos in 19%, and in threes to fives in 19% (section 3.2). The October photos often show a drake and a female swimming within a body length or two of each other.
- **Pairing:** "Most pairs form in the fall or early winter" [AAB-M gallery caption]; "Shortly after mallards have completed their prenuptial molt into their winter plumage, social courtship begins" [Johnsgard 1975, p. 229]. A group of four made of two loose pairs fits the season.

**On the painted atlas:**
- **`male`**:
  - the head's mean colour is dominated by green, and a white ring band is present;
  - the breast is chestnut (a red-brown hue) and much darker than the flank;
  - the flank is light grey with low saturation;
  - the stern is near-black;
  - "dark at both ends and light in the middle": the flank's luminance is at least twice the breast's and the stern's.
- **`female`**:
  - the bill is orange, with a dark region on its upper middle: at 30-50% of the bill's length the upper third of the side is darker than the lower third, but not at 0-5% or 85-95%;
  - the flank pattern has high contrast (dark centres, buff edges).
- **`maleMoult`**:
  - the head is green only in its upper part: a green crown band over a grey-brown face;
  - no white ring and no curl;
  - the flank is light grey with scattered brown, its mean luminance between the male's and the female's.

## 12. Open questions and confidence

**High confidence**
- The culmen, and the bill width and height at the nostrils [Söderquist et al. 2014]; the tarsus, toe and wing ranges [Baird et al. 1884]; the masses [Bellrose & Hawkins 1947 in Johnsgard 1975; Kortright 1942].
- A cruising speed of 0.48 m/s and a steady paddling rate of about 2.4 strokes per second [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970].
- An upending mean of 4.2 s (maximum 5.8 s), and a feeding mix of 42% upending to 44% head under water [Szijj 1965], though from one lake in the early 1960s.
- The adult drakes' moult window, late August to early November [Pyle 2005; Bent 1923; Kortright 1942; Millais 1902], and **the local switch from mostly moulting to mostly finished drakes between mid and late September** (120 records).
- The field-mark wording in section 4.

**Medium confidence**
- **Heights above the waterline** (back 77-83 mm, tail tip 29-40 mm): three side-on photos, each scaled by the sex-mean culmen (± 5%). The male's head measurements agree across two independently scaled photos.
- **The bill's top-view profile**: one museum skin, dried and pitched, whose ventral view agrees. The skin's widths may read 3-5% high (it is nearer the camera than the rule) or low (shrinkage). The shape of the profile is the result that matters.
- **The female saddle in mm**: two photos. The tally gives its frequency (41% of readable bills).
- **The flock mix**:
  - Photographer bias (drakes, tame park birds, loafing birds) and a few large flocks weigh on the percentages.
  - The period table counts birds, and 5-15 records per period carry most of it.
- **The `maleMoult` pattern** (the head lagging behind the body) rests on about 30 local birds. It is the reverse of Kortright's order; both orders occur.
- **Tail shape** `rounded`: no source.

**Low confidence or not verified**
- **Draft** (4.5-6.5 cm): computed from a half-ellipsoid hull and skin widths; no measurement.
- **Waterline length**: the photos give 0.27-0.28 m. Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen give 0.33 m for their captive drakes without saying how they measured it.
- **Middle toe with or without claw**: Baird et al. do not say. Ridgway's convention, used in the shorebird research, was without the claw.
- **Nail width**: an estimate.
- **Time budget**: winter, South Carolina. There are no September data from the Pacific Northwest.
- **Alert neck height**: an estimate.
- **Snippet-only claims**: the Nebraska and Missouri time budgets (Birds of the World, paywalled) and the 0.7 g/mL body density. None is used for a number.
- **Guillemain et al. 2001** was read through a text extract, not the page image.
- **Ageing and sexing of the photos**:
  - All 120 records were classified by helper agents on the written criteria of section 3.2: 35 by two first-round helpers, whose notes stopped part-way and are tagged in the appendix, and 85 by a second-round helper.
  - I checked their criteria and summaries, and viewed the photos behind the key calls: the moulting drakes in section 8 and three of the dark-billed females.
  - One record (303799240, 30 birds) is from Vancouver, Washington, just inside the box.

**Sources I could not read** (not relied on for any number)
- **Birds of the World**, Mallard account (Drilling, Titman & McKinney 2002): its Appearance, Behavior and Measurements sections are paywalled.
- **Palmer 1976** (*Handbook of North American Birds*, vol. 2) and **Pyle 2008**: not online.
- **Paulus 1988** (a review of time-activity budgets of nonbreeding Anatidae), and **Jorde et al. 1984**, whose Condor PDF is behind a bot check.
- **Lovvorn & Jones 1991** (body mass, volume and buoyancy of aquatic birds): only the abstract and secondary quotes.

**Worth doing if higher confidence is needed**
- Measure the built model's waterline heights against section 6.1, and set the draft from its hull volume and the 1.15 kg mass.
- Find a side-on photo of a floating alert Mallard, for the height of the head with the neck up.
- Photograph a fresh bill from above with a scale, to confirm the skin's width profile.
