# Pied-billed Grebe: measurements, late-September plumage, field marks, floating posture, and diving

Date: 2026-09-25
Purpose: source data for the detailed Pied-billed Grebe (`pibgre`) model and the `grebe` foraging style on `swim`. The cast is two grebes on `swim` in the `open-water` zone of Willow Lake (polygon [[-22,-15],[26,-15],[32,-52],[-28,-52]] m, 15-60 m from the vantage), seen through binoculars (7.5°) or the telescope (2°) on a Willamette Valley morning, about 20-30 September.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt of a paywalled page; I could not read the page, so treat it as unverified. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo and scaled it by the exposed culmen (or, where stated, by another part). "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements** (mean of the sexes; mm):
  - Exposed culmen **21** (M 22.7, F 19.3), wing **125** (M 130, F 120) (Cramp & Simmons 1977, n = 13 and 18) [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117]; mass **0.40 kg** (M 458 g, F 339 g) [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117].
  - Bill depth at the base **11.5** and width **5.5**: BBR 11.4 [BBR 1884, p. 441]; AVONET 10.6 × 5.4 at the nostril [Tobias et al. 2022]. "The length of the culmen less than twice the basal depth" [BBR 1884, p. 440].
  - Tarsus **36**, middle toe without claw **46** [BBR 1884, p. 441]; AVONET tarsus 37.1. The tail is a hidden downy tuft (AVONET 28-36 mm): "virtually no tail" [AAB-ID].
  - Ridgway's *Birds of North and Middle America* never reached the grebes, and Bent 1919 gives no body measurements.
  - On the water the bird traces at **0.20-0.235 m** from bill tip to stern, against the 0.30-0.38 m of skins (photo-measured).
- **Age and plumage, 21-30 September** (228 iNaturalist records from the Willamette box, 143 birds ageable):
  - **No breeding plumage.** About **63% plain basic**, **34% striped juveniles** (half of them faint), **3% adults with a ring remnant**. Striped juveniles fall from 44% in mid-September to 18% in early October. Bent: young "retain the black and white stripes on the head until late in October" [Bent 1919, p. 44].
  - **Model two variants, `basic` and `juvenile`**; a third, `transition` (a dull band at mid-bill), is optional. **Raise the cast to 3 birds: 2 `basic`, 1 `juvenile`.** With two birds, one of each.
- **Bill:** deep, compressed, the lower edge straight and the top arching down to a small hook 1-1.5 mm below the lower edge; depth falls to 0.94 / 0.81 / 0.61 / 0.36 of the base at 25 / 50 / 75 / 90% (photo-measured; `taper` ≈ 2.5). The breeding ring sits at 0.57 of the bill from the feathering and is 0.2 of the bill wide (photo-measured).
- **Feet:** lobed toes (lobes an **Estimate**, ≈ 0.3 of the toe wide), dark greenish-slate, paler lead-grey inside [BBR 1884, p. 441]; legs set at the stern, never visible on a floating bird; seen in a dive kick, a preening stretch, or a take-off run.
- **Floating** (photo-measured on six photos):
  - Back top **20-37 mm** above the water when sleek (use **28 mm**), about **50 mm** when dry and fluffed; stern tuft tip 4-19 mm; crown 80 mm, 106 mm with the neck raised.
  - Draft about **47 mm, 0.6-0.7 of a 75 mm body depth** (**Estimate** by buoyancy).
  - The white undertail "powder puff" lies on the water on a sleek bird and is cocked up as a 30 × 20 mm ball on a fluffed one.
  - **Sinking**: "gradually sink down backward, like a disappearing frog, without making a ripple" [Bent 1919, pp. 44-45]; "until only their heads are above water, like submarine periscopes" [BirdWeb]. Periscope: the back about **45 mm under** (73 mm below rest); "crocodile-style" with the eyes at the surface about 100 mm below rest (computed). The one sink on video went from floating to back-awash in **0.4 s** and under in 1.9 s.
- **Diving:**
  - Foraging dives **7.6 ± 3.1 s (1-15, n = 154)**, surfacing **3.7 ± 3.0 m** away (0-12); escape dives **9.3 ± 2.7 s (3-18, n = 52)**, **10 ± 4.4 m** (2-20) [Bleich 1975, p. 279]. Dive/pause ratio 2.3, so pauses of about **3.3 s** and 70% of a feeding bout under water (computed).
  - Starts, from video (6 starts): **4 head-first roll-unders** (gone in 0.4-0.65 s, stern tipping up, no spring), **1 splash dive** (the "crash dive": driven straight down with spray), **1 sink**; **no duck-style leap**.
  - Surface speed while foraging about **0.05-0.08 m/s** (video, **Estimate**); cruising 0.15 m/s recommended.
  - Time budget: **no study exists**. Recommend half the time in dive bouts (≈ 35% of all time under water), the rest loafing, preening and cruising (**Estimate**). Solitary: keep birds ≥ 10 m apart.
- **Tracing references** (scratchpad, not the repo; iNaturalist; all but one CC BY-NC and from the Willamette Valley):
  - `pibgre-basic-side.jpg` (Patricia, obs. 62163037, 0.150 mm/px) for the profile and bill;
  - `pibgre-juv-side.jpg` (Patricia, obs. 136620812, 26 September, 0.194 mm/px) for the juvenile and a second profile;
  - `pibgre-basic-relaxed.jpg`, `pibgre-basic-alert.jpg`, `pibgre-basic-fluffed.jpg` for postures and colour; `pibgre-juv-bold.jpg`, `pibgre-juv-ccby.jpg` (CC BY), `pibgre-transition.jpg`, `pibgre-low.jpg`, `pibgre-low-2.jpg`.
  - The harvested `public/media/photos/pibgre.jpg` is a breeding adult: right only for the ring.
- **Biggest open questions:** body depth and draft (no measurement), the toe lobes, the time budget and dive-start frequencies (video n = 6), and how long a hiding bird holds the periscope.

## 2. Measurements

**Ridgway never published the grebes.** *The Birds of North and Middle America* (Bull. U.S. Natl. Mus. 50) ended with Part XI (Friedmann 1950); the loons and grebes were never covered. Bent 1919 gives no body measurements for this species (only eggs). The primary tables I could read are Baird, Brewer & Ridgway's *Water Birds of North America* (1884), the sexed wing and culmen series from Cramp & Simmons 1977 that Johnsgard 1987 reprints, and the AVONET skins. Palmer 1962, Pyle 2008 and the Birds of the World account (Muller & Storer 1999, with its measurement appendix) could not be read.

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

- **Adults lose the ring and the black throat after breeding.** "The black throat of the adult and the black band on the bill are acquired just prior to the breeding season. Some adults show traces of the black throat in the fall or have it well developed, but partially concealed by the whitish tips of the feathers" [Bent 1919, p. 44]. Winter adults have the "throat whitish, sometimes with traces of black" and a bill "yellowish to dusky gray with a dull greenish tinge but no black band" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117]. "Definitive prebasic molt in adults is complete and begins in mid- to late breeding season" [BoW-A, Snippet].
- **Juveniles keep their stripes into October.** "Many young birds retain the black and white stripes on the head until late in October, though some have completely changed before that time into the brown plumage of the first winter, in which the bright russet color of the neck, breast, and flanks is conspicuous" [Bent 1919, pp. 43-44]. "Young birds retain a striped head pattern for some time, perhaps through their first fall" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117]. The first prebasic moult "is partial, includes head-, neck-, and body feathers, and is gradually completed through winter (approximately 7 months after hatching)", and "white on the side of head and neck is gradually reduced to small white spots or bars at 7 months" [BoW-A, Snippet].
- **The breeding season runs late, so September has young of all sizes.** "Pied-bill grebes begin breeding around April or May and continue through about October" [ADW]; "Where climate allows, the Pied-billed Grebe may have a long breeding season, from early spring to mid-autumn" [Audubon]; one or two broods [AAB-LH]. "Juveniles accompany adults for several weeks" [AAB-ID gallery caption].
- **Migrants arrive in September and October.** In western Washington "resident populations increase as local birds are joined by northern migratory birds, which begin arriving in western Washington in September and October" [BirdWeb]. "On the fall migration these grebes proceed slowly through September and October, lingering on the inland ponds and small streams in family parties, in pairs or even singly" [Bent 1919, p. 46].

### 3.2 iNaturalist, Willamette Valley box

Present all year, with September the busiest month. Records from 11 September to 12 October: **228** (2016-2026; 9, 3, 3, 1, 21, 18, 21, 35, 44, 58 and 15 by year). Life-stage annotations are sparse: 8 "juvenile" and 6 "adult".

**Ageing.** I aged the first photo of each record (the second where the first was unusable) into six classes:
- **BR** breeding: a clear black ring on a whitish bill, or a clear black throat.
- **TR** transition: a partial or faint dark band on the bill, or a greyish, mottled throat; an adult losing its breeding marks.
- **BA** basic: plain brown head, pale chin, horn-coloured to greyish bill without a ring. This includes adults in basic plumage and first-winter birds that have already lost their stripes; I did not try to separate them.
- **JS** juvenile, striped: white and dark stripes or blotches on the sides of the head and neck ("faint" where only pale remnants show on the cheek).
- **CH** a downy or half-grown chick.
- **NA** not ageable.

A sub-agent aged 76 of the 228 records on the same written criteria (marked "(sub-agent)" in the appendix). I aged the other 152 from 2x2 contact sheets.

- **No bird in full breeding dress.** Every ringed bill is a transitional adult; the black throat, where any is left, is a dusky smudge or a thin line at the chin.
- **Striped juveniles fall from about half the ageable birds in mid-September to a fifth in early October**, and more of them are "faint" as the month goes on, as Bent's "until late in October" predicts. Small young with red-orange bills and bold stripes are still being fed and still beg on 11-26 September (314456404, 314497213, 316921873 "almost continuously begging", 401802002 "occasionally begging audibly"); a downy chick was photographed on 13 September 2023 (183133214).
- **Basic birds dominate from about 20 September.** Several show a rufous or tawny foreneck and flanks (the "bright russet" of Bent's first winter). Some bills still carry a small dusky smudge at mid-bill that may be what is left of the ring; these were called BA.
- The transitional birds are all 12-22 September except three on 1-8 October; the last clear partial band is 8 October (138025243).
- iNaturalist favours close, striking birds, and a striped juvenile is more photogenic than a plain brown one, so the juvenile share is, if anything, an overestimate. Several Beaverton (Greenway Park) records show the same family on different days.

### 3.3 Recommendation

- **Model two variants: `basic` and `juvenile`.**
  - `basic`: plain brown, whitish chin, pale horn bill without a ring. About 60% of the ageable birds on 21-30 September.
  - `juvenile`: a first-autumn bird with a whitish face streaked and blotched dark-brown, a dark crown, a pinkish-horn bill with a dark culmen. About a third of the birds; half of them faint.
- **A third variant, `transition`** (a dull dark band at mid-bill and a grey smudge at the chin on a basic bird), is optional. It is 3% of birds in the game's window and 12% in mid-September. It would be the only way to show the "pied" bill the species is named for, so it is worth adding only if a third bird is added and the designers want the name-mark visible; otherwise skip it. **Do not model breeding plumage for late September.**
- **The mix:** with two birds, one `basic` and one `juvenile` shows both plumages but overstates juveniles (50% against about 35%). **Better: raise the cast to 3 birds, 2 `basic` and 1 `juvenile`**, which matches the 63:34 split. Three birds suit the zone: the species is "usually seen singly, in pairs, or in very small parties" [Bent 1919, p. 45], and the open-water polygon is about 55 × 37 m.
- **Mechanics:** today `plumage: "mixed"` picks a variant uniformly at random per bird (`src/birds/spawn.ts`), and the location schema accepts only `male`, `female` or `mixed`. A fixed 2:1 mix needs either two cast entries with a named variant each (the schema would have to accept variant names) or a weighted pick. The plan should choose; the research only fixes the ratio.
- **Size is the same for both.** Late-September juveniles are full-grown (the young are "nearly full-grown before the down entirely disappears" [Bent 1919, p. 43]); the striped young still begging in mid-September looked full-sized on the photos. Use the mean of the sexes for both (section 2.5).

## 4. Field marks of each recommended plumage

Colour words are the sources'; I have not invented hex values. "(photo)" marks my observation on the reference photos of section 8, with positions in mm scaled by the culmen (21 mm). Sexes are alike in plumage [Johnsgard 1987, p. 115; Audubon].

### 4.1 `basic` (adult non-breeding and plain first-winter)

The descriptions:
- "Winter plumage: Head and neck dull brownish, darker on the pileum and nape, and becoming white on the chin and throat (sometimes also on the malar region); lower parts silvery white, brownish laterally and posteriorly; upper parts as in the summer plumage. Bill horn-color, becoming blackish basally and on the culmen; lower mandible more lilaceous, with a dusky lateral stripe; iris of three distinct colors, disposed in concentric rings, the first (around the pupil) clear milk-white, the next dark olive-brown, the outer pale ochraceous-brown ... tarsi and toes greenish slate, the joints darker" [BBR 1884, p. 441, from a November Nevada bird].
- "WINTER PLUMAGE. Forehead and crown dark grayish brown; throat whitish, sometimes with traces of black; rest of head grayish buff with slightly darker line through eye. Neck, breast, and flanks grayish buff tinged reddish to rich reddish tawny. Otherwise as in breeding plumage, though white rear often more conspicuous. The bill is yellowish to dusky gray with a dull greenish tinge but no black band" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117]. The "breeding plumage" upperparts it refers to: "Upperparts dark brown, often with hoary look in fresh plumage" [p. 115].
- "These are brown birds, slightly darker above and more tawny-brown on the underparts ... While breeding, the bill is whitish with a black band ('pied'), but otherwise is yellow-brown" [AAB-ID]. "Nonbreeding birds have a brownish neck and lack the black bill stripe"; "Some nonbreeding birds have more cinnamon coloring on the neck" [AAB-ID gallery captions]. "Brownish overall, with rusty tinge in winter" [Audubon].

By region (photos: `pibgre-basic-side.jpg`, `pibgre-basic-relaxed.jpg`, `pibgre-basic-alert.jpg`, `pibgre-basic-fluffed.jpg`):
- **Crown and hindneck:** dark grey-brown to blackish-brown, the darkest part of the bird. The cap comes down to the top of the eye; the hindneck stays dark down to the back (photo).
- **Face:** greyish-buff to buff-brown below the eye, paler than the crown, with at most a slightly darker line through the eye [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117] (photo).
- **Chin and throat:** whitish, a clean patch from the base of the lower mandible back about **25 mm** and down about **10 mm** (photo, `pibgre-basic-side.jpg`), fading into buff on the upper foreneck.
- **Foreneck, sides of the neck and upper breast:** tawny to rufous-buff ("grayish buff tinged reddish to rich reddish tawny"), strongest on the lower 30-35 mm of the neck above the water (photo). On some birds it is dull grey-brown; on others bright cinnamon.
- **Back and scapulars:** dark grey-brown with paler grey-brown feather edges, a "hoary" look (photo). Uniform at 15 m.
- **Flanks at the waterline:** brown, mottled rufous-buff and dark brown, with pale buff or white showing where the feathers part (photo). "Lower parts silvery white, brownish laterally and posteriorly" [BBR 1884, p. 441]; the white belly is under water on a floating bird.
- **Stern: the white "powder puff".** White undertail coverts, "white rear often more conspicuous" in winter [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117]. On the photos a white tuft **20-30 mm long** at the stern, at the water on a sleek bird (`pibgre-basic-side.jpg`, 22 mm) and cocked up as a fluffy white ball **up to about 30 × 20 mm** on a fluffed or alarmed bird (`pibgre-basic-relaxed.jpg`, `pibgre-basic-fluffed.jpg`, and many appendix records). Allen's bird "rested rather high on the water, holding its tail, if we may speak of it as such, erect, and nervously flashing the light areas on the flanks" [Bent 1919, p. 43].
- **Bill:** pale yellowish-horn to greyish-horn, the culmen dusky grey-brown from the base to near the tip, the lower mandible paler and yellower (photo); a few birds keep a dusky smudge at mid-bill on the cutting edge, perhaps the last of the ring (section 3.2).
- **Eye:** dark brown iris [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117: "dark brown eyes"], with a pale inner ring round the pupil [BBR 1884, p. 441]; a thin whitish bare eyelid ring, about 0.5 mm, and a narrow whitish feathered crescent behind and above the eye (photo). "Whitish eye ring" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117].
- **Feet** (seen only when diving, stretching or taking off): "greenish slate, the joints darker" [BBR 1884, p. 441]; "dark lead color" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117].

### 4.2 `juvenile` (first autumn, striped)

The descriptions:
- "JUVENILES. Bill yellowish brown; no orbital ring. Sides of head and neck variably dark to grayish brown, blotched and streaked irregularly with white; throat white; rest of neck brownish buff. Upperparts blackish brown; breast and flanks rather dark brown mixed with buff; belly and under tail coverts white, though latter not as conspicuous as in adult" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117].
- "Young, first plumage: Similar to the winter dress, but side and under part of the head white, indefinitely striped with brown, the throat sometimes immaculate" [BBR 1884, p. 441]. "Young: Similar to winter adult, but sides of head more or less distinctly striped with brown" [Ridgway 1887, p. 6].
- "Young pied-billed grebes have strongly striped faces and dark brown eyes, as well as pale brownish or buffy bills" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 113]. "Juveniles have striped faces" [AAB-ID].
- Juvenile bill: "the upper mandible is dark brown or dark yellow-brown while the lower is pink-buff" [BoW-A, Snippet].

By region (photos: `pibgre-juv-side.jpg`, a faint-striped bird of 26 September; `pibgre-juv-bold.jpg` and `pibgre-juv-ccby.jpg`, bolder birds):
- **Crown:** blackish-brown, darker than in `basic`, down to the top of the eye (photo).
- **Face stripes:** on a whitish-buff to buff face, (1) a pale stripe from the gape back under the eye, about 22 × 2-3 mm; (2) a pale stripe from behind the eye back towards the nape, about 20 × 2-3 mm; between and around them dark brown streaks and blotches that continue down the sides of the neck (photo, `pibgre-juv-side.jpg`). On bold birds the pale stripes are white and the dark ones blackish, and the throat is white, bordered by a dark line down each side (`pibgre-juv-bold.jpg`; appendix records 314497213, 403449447).
- **Throat:** white.
- **Foreneck and breast:** buff to rufous-buff, mottled and streaked dark brown (photo), "breast and flanks rather dark brown mixed with buff".
- **Back:** blackish-brown, darker and plainer than `basic`.
- **Stern:** white, but a smaller puff than the adult's.
- **Bill:** pinkish-horn to yellowish-brown, the culmen dark; small young in mid-September still have red-orange or pinkish-red bills (appendix records 314616598, 401802002). No ring.
- **Eye:** dark brown, "no orbital ring" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117].

**At 15-60 m** the face pattern is what separates the two: the juvenile's head reads as a dark cap over a pale, streaky face; the basic bird's as a plain brown head with a pale chin. On a 1080-pixel-high screen one pixel covers (computed from the fields of view): through the telescope (2°) 0.5 mm of bird at 15 m, 1.0 mm at 30 m and 1.9 mm at 60 m; through binoculars (7.5°) 1.8 mm at 15 m and 7.3 mm at 60 m. So the 2-3 mm stripes read as stripes through the telescope out to about 30 m, and beyond that, and through binoculars, only as a pale face under a dark cap; paint them 3-4 mm wide and with strong contrast rather than finer.

### 4.3 The ring and throat, for a `transition` variant (optional)

- Breeding bill: "bill milk-white, crossed past the middle by a black band, the terminal portion more bluish" [BBR 1884, p. 441]; "bill whitish, crossed about the middle by a black band" [Ridgway 1887, p. 6]; "multicolored, with a broad black band in the middle, and otherwise mostly bluish white" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 115].
- **Ring position and width** (photo-measured on the harvested photo `pibgre-harvest.jpg`, a breeding adult): the band's centre is at **0.57 of the bill length from the feathering** (about 12 mm), its width about **0.2 of the bill length (4 mm)**, slanted forward-down so that it sits a little further forward on the lower mandible; the pale tip beyond it is about 7 mm.
- Transitional birds in the tally show the band dull grey-black and narrower, sometimes only on the cutting edge or as a spot, and the throat a grey smudge or a thin dark line at the chin (section 3.2; `pibgre-transition.jpg`).

## 5. Structure

### 5.1 Head and bill

- "They have compact bodies and slender necks, with relatively large, blocky heads and short, thick bills. They have virtually no tail" [AAB-ID]. "Its brown plumage and short, high, arched bill distinguish it from other common North American grebes" [BoW-I]. "The only member of the group having a short, chickenlike bill" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117]; "the short, thick, henlike bill" [Bent 1919, p. 45].
- **Bill shape:** "bill very stout, the length of the culmen less than twice the basal depth; bill much shorter than the head, the culmen much curved terminally" [BBR 1884, p. 440]. The bill is "laterally compressed and down curved at the tip, forming a slight hook" [SIMoN]. Depth is about **twice the width** (AVONET 10.6 × 5.4 at the nostril).
- **Depth along the bill** (photo-measured on the two strictly side-on bills, `pibgre-basic-side.jpg` and `pibgre-basic-alert.jpg`; depth measured square to the straight lower edge of the lower mandible, as a fraction of the depth at the feathering):

| Position from the feathering | 0 | 25% | 50% | 75% | 90% | tip |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Depth / base depth (`basic-side`) | 1.00 | 0.92 | 0.77 | 0.58 | 0.31 | 0 |
| Depth / base depth (`basic-alert`) | 1.00 | 0.97 | 0.86 | 0.65 | 0.42 | 0 |
| **Mean** | **1.00** | **0.94** | **0.81** | **0.61** | **0.36** | **0** |

  - Base depth / culmen chord: **0.61** and **0.51** on the two photos; BBR's 11.4 / 19.1 = 0.60.
  - The **lower edge is straight**; nearly all the taper is in the top of the bill, which arches down to the tip. So the bill's centreline falls by about half the base depth over its length, and the tip of the upper mandible hooks **about 1-1.5 mm below the line of the lower mandible** (photo, `pibgre-basic-relaxed.jpg`).
  - In `bill.ts` terms (`f = 1 − 0.85 σ^taper`), these fractions fit **`taper` ≈ 2.2-2.7**: a blunt, deep bill that narrows mostly in its last third (computed).
- **Width along the bill:** AVONET 5.4 mm at the nostril; on head-on photos (`60248678`, `97408181` in the appendix) the bill is a narrow wedge, about half as wide as deep at the base and knife-edged at the tip. **Estimate:** width 5.5 mm at the feathering, 5 mm at 25%, 4 mm at 50%, 3 mm at 75%, 1.5 mm at 90%.
- **Head:** a large, rounded-square head; steep forehead running straight into the high culmen base; the crown peaks above or just behind the eye; the nape is often spiky when wet (photo). On `pibgre-basic-side.jpg` the feathered head outline is about **63 mm** from the rear of the head to the bill feathering and **39 mm** from crown to chin; bill tip to rear of head is 80 mm, so culmen / head (feathered outline) is about **0.34** (photo-measured). No skull-based head length was found.
- **Eye:** visible dark eye about **6.5-7.5 mm** across (photo-measured on two photos), set high, its centre level with the top of the bill base and about **17 mm behind the feathering** of the culmen (`pibgre-basic-side.jpg`). A large eye for the head.

### 5.2 Neck

- "Slender necks" [AAB-ID]; "compact and short-necked" [Audubon].
- On the water the neck is usually carried **upright**, a smooth column rising from the front third of the body, with a slight S at the throat (photo, all side-on birds). The feathered neck is about **33 mm** thick front to back at mid-height, and the foreneck runs about **40 mm** from the water to the throat at rest (`pibgre-basic-side.jpg`), about 55 mm with the neck raised (`pibgre-basic-alert.jpg`) (photo-measured).
- **Crown height above the water**: 79-81 mm with the neck at rest (`pibgre-juv-side.jpg`, `pibgre-basic-side.jpg`), 97-99 mm on fluffed birds that ride higher, and **106 mm** with the neck raised (`pibgre-basic-alert.jpg`) (photo-measured). Relaxed and loafing birds sink the head into the shoulders (`pibgre-basic-fluffed.jpg`; appendix records 244540709, 320456343), and when swimming fast or hunting the neck is stretched forward and low (`pibgre-low-2.jpg`).

### 5.3 Body on the water

- Waterline length (front of the breast to the stern, at the water): **164-172 mm** on three photos; bill tip to the end of the white stern tuft **204-235 mm** (photo-measured, table in section 6).
- **Back line:** a low, even dome, highest at about 40% of the waterline length back from the breast, sloping gently to the stern; flat and low on a sleek, wet bird, rounded and higher on a dry, fluffed one (photo).
- **Breast:** rounded, rising steeply from the water into the neck; the neck meets the breast at the front 15-20% of the body.
- **Stern:** no visible tail. The body ends in the white undertail tuft, which lies on the water on a sleek bird and is cocked up 20-40° as a fluffy ball when the plumage is fluffed (photo; see 4.1).
- **Width:** on head-on photos the floating body is broad and rounded, roughly 2.5-3 times the width of the head (photo, `97408181`, `60248678`, not measured). **Estimate:** body half-width at the waterline about 40-45 mm.

### 5.4 The tail

- The rectrices are soft, downy and hidden in the undertail coverts: AVONET's skins give **28-36 mm** (mean 32.6) from the base [Tobias et al. 2022], but "they have virtually no tail" in the field [AAB-ID], and "their tail is small and usually hidden by feathers that grow low on the back, called uppertail coverts" [SIMoN]. What shows is the white undertail puff (4.1).

### 5.5 Folded wing on the water

- Grebe wings are small (wing chord 125 mm against a 400 g body; hand-wing index 34 [Tobias et al. 2022]) and fold into the flank feathers. On all the side-on photos **no wing outline shows**: the scapulars and back feathers cover the folded wing, and the flank feathers overlap it from below (photo). At most a dark wedge of primaries shows at the rear third of the back on a sleek bird, the same colour as the back.
- "In flight (which is extremely rare) the wings appear mostly brown, but the secondaries exhibit highly variable amounts of white on their tips and inner webs" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117]; "the inner webs of the secondaries tipped with white" [BBR 1884, p. 441].

### 5.6 Legs and feet

- "Feet at the buttocks": "The Latin genus name for grebe, 'podylimbus,' means 'feet at the buttocks' ... Lobed (not webbed) toes further assist with swimming" [AAB-O]. "Their claws are flattened like fingernails, and their toes are lobed rather than webbed" [BirdWeb family text].
- Tarsus **36 mm**, middle toe without claw **46 mm**; "tarsus shorter than the middle toe without claw" [BBR 1884, pp. 440-441]. The tarsus is laterally flattened (a blade in the water) in all grebes; I found no Pied-billed number for its depth. **Estimate:** 7 mm deep front to back, 3 mm thick.
- **Lobes:** I found no measurement for this species. **Estimate** for the model: each front toe carries a lobe on both sides, the pair together about **0.3 of the toe length** wide at the widest (about 12-14 mm on the middle toe), narrowing to the base of each joint (lobes scalloped at the joints), the outer toe longest in grebes, the inner shortest, a small lobed hind toe. Basis: the family description above and the grebe-foot descriptions in [Johansson & Norberg 2001, abstract only].
- **Colour:** "tarsi and toes greenish slate-black on the outer, and plumbeous on the inner side" (breeding) and "greenish slate, the joints darker" (winter) [BBR 1884, p. 441]; "the feet are dark lead color" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 117]. So dark slate-grey with a green tinge outside, paler lead-grey inside and on the lobes.
- **When visible:** only in the dive itself (a kick of the feet as the bird goes under), when the bird rises to flap its wings or stretches a foot out behind while preening (appendix record 183587316: "wings raised in a stretch with a lobed foot stretched out behind"), and when running over the water to take off [Bent 1919, p. 44]. Never on a floating bird.

- **The back rides 20-37 mm above the water when the plumage is sleeked, and about 50 mm when it is dry and fluffed.** That is the grebe's buoyancy control, visible on still photos: "They can sink deeply or stay just at or below the surface, exposing as much or as little of the body as they wish" [AAB-O].

The whole white belly is under water.

### 6.2 Draft

- No source gives a draft or body depth for this species, and no photo shows the belly. **Estimate by buoyancy** (computed): a floating bird displaces its own mass, 0.40 kg, so about **400 cm³** is under water. Modelling the body as an ellipsoid with the measured waterline length (0.17 m) and an estimated width of 0.085 m, the submerged part needs a **draft of about 45-50 mm** whatever the body depth.
- With the sleek back at 20-37 mm, the sleek body depth (back to keel, plumage included) is about **70-80 mm** and the **draft about 0.6-0.7 of the body depth** (computed). The fluffed bird's extra feather volume, above and below the water, lifts it by about 20 mm.
- For the model: **body depth ≈ 75 mm; waterline at 0.62 of the depth below the back (back 28 mm above water) at rest**, the middle of the three sleek birds (20, 28, 37 mm); fluffed loafing birds ride about 20 mm higher.

### 6.3 Relaxed, alert and sinking

- **Relaxed / loafing:** plumage fluffed, back 45-52 mm above water, stern puff cocked, neck drawn in or upright (photos above).
- **Normal swimming and foraging:** plumage sleek, back 20-37 mm, stern at the water, neck upright.
- **Alert, before a dive, or swimming fast:** lower; "it also has the power so to contract its displacement that it can swim along with only its head and neck above water" [Bent 1919, p. 44]; the neck held up (106 mm crown) or stretched forward and low (`pibgre-low-2.jpg`).
- **Sinking out of sight:** "or it can gradually sink down backward, like a disappearing frog, without making a ripple" [Bent 1919, pp. 44-45]; "When disturbed, they dive headfirst under water, or they sink slowly into the water until only their heads are above water, like submarine periscopes" [BirdWeb]; "When disturbed or suspicious, it may sink slowly until only head is above water" [Audubon]; "Can adjust their buoyancy to float with just the upper half of the head above the water" [AAB-ID gallery caption]; "When hiding, it may remain underwater with eyes and nostrils just above the surface" [BoW-I]; "These grebes often avoid danger by submerging, crocodile-style, with just the eyes and nostrils above the surface" [AAB-LH]. In hiding it may show "only its bill above water" [Bent 1919, p. 39].
  - **How far down:** from the back at +28 mm to the "periscope" with only the head and upper neck out, the back is about 40-50 mm under water (the neck column 40-55 mm long, section 5.2); "crocodile-style" puts the eye at the surface, so the body sinks a further 50-60 mm, **about 90-110 mm in all** from rest (computed from the heights in 6.1).
  - **Which way:** "down backward" [Bent 1919, p. 45]: the stern goes under first and the body tilts tail-down a few degrees while the head stays level.
  - **How fast:** the sources say only "gradually", "slowly", "inch by inch" [Audubon-S]. The one sink on video dropped from normal floating to back-awash in **0.4 s**, held there, and slid under 1.9 s after it began (section 7.3); a hiding bird that stays at the periscope depth settles more slowly, by the sources' words.

## 7. Swimming and diving behavior

### 7.1 What the sources say

**Foraging.** "Forages by diving from surface and swimming underwater, propelled mainly by feet" [Audubon]. "It takes most food by diving either in open water or among aquatic vegetation, but it picks some food off vegetation or the surface of the water, or even catches it in midair" [BoW-I]. "They usually dive for food, but occasionally pluck insects from foliage, the water's surface, or the air" [AAB-LH]. "They catch small fish and invertebrates by diving or simply slowly submerging" [AAB-ID]. Food: crayfish 27%, fish 24%, insects 46% by volume in 174 stomachs (Wetmore 1924, in [Johnsgard 1987, p. 118]).

**Dive durations and distances.** The only timed studies I could read:

| Study | Birds, place, season | Dive duration, s | Surface pause | Distance under water |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Bleich 1975, p. 279], forage dives | El Dorado Park, Los Angeles Co. and Fallbrook, San Diego Co., California; May-July 1973, Feb and May 1974; water 2-5 m, most dives in 2-3 m; 08:00-10:00 and 17:00-19:00 | **7.58 ± 3.07 (SD), range 1-15, n = 154** | dive/pause ratio **2.3** "calculated only from the mean values of forage dives and pauses" [p. 279], so a mean pause of **≈ 3.3 s** (computed) | **3.69 ± 3.02 m, 0-12 m** |
| [Bleich 1975, p. 279], escape dives | same; dives within 5 min of the observers' arrival or after the grebes noticed them | **9.31 ± 2.66, range 3-18, n = 52** | - | **10.00 ± 4.43 m, 2-20 m** |
| [Heintzelman & Newberry 1964, p. 291] | two birds, Brigantine NWR, New Jersey, 30 November 1963 | **9.37 ± 1.82 (6.2-12.4), n = 10**; **12.70 ± 2.77 (8.9-16.6), n = 4** | - | - |
| An English vagrant, in [Johnsgard 1987, p. 118] | one bird | 3-24 | - | at most "20 yards" (18 m) |

- Bleich: "Forage and escape diving times ... were significantly different (P<.001; t = 3.69)"; "Pied-billed Grebes submerged longer and moved farther during escape dives than during forage dives" [p. 279]. "When grebes were not observed to surface within 60 sec they probably surfaced in nearby vegetation and the observations were terminated" [p. 279]. "Escape dives are much less variable in both time and distance than are forage dives. This situation might be expected if birds diving to escape a predator were diving closer to their maximum times and distances" [p. 280].
- Johnsgard's reading: "Most foraging dives are in quite shallow water and are rather short in duration ... These observations suggest that little prolonged underwater chasing normally occurs" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 118].
- Mean underwater progress (computed from Bleich's means): forage 3.69 m in 7.58 s ≈ **0.5 m/s** net; escape 10.0 m in 9.3 s ≈ **1.1 m/s** net. The real swimming speed is higher, since forage paths are not straight.
- **Time under water while feeding** (computed from the dive/pause ratio 2.3): about **70%** of a dive bout is spent submerged.
- "These grebes frequently sunbathe between foraging dives; this may be an important means of heat conservation, especially for females" [BoW-F, Snippet].

**How dives start.**
- The **crash dive** (escape): "Pied-billed Grebes escape danger by 'crash-diving'—plunging with head and tail raised above the belly, making a splash" [AAB-LH]; "A crash-diving grebe pushes its body down with its wings thrust outward. Its tail and head disappears last, while the bird kicks water several feet into the air" [AAB-O]; "crash-diving, when it may kick water several feet into the air" [BoW-I]; "ordinarily, in a hurried dive, it plunges forward and disappears like a flash, swimming away for a long distance under water, to appear suddenly at some unexpected spot or perhaps to vanish and keep out of sight" [Bent 1919, p. 44].
- The **head-first dive** (ordinary): "They can also dive head first" [AAB-LH]; "When disturbed, they dive headfirst under water" [BirdWeb]; "It dives headfirst from a sitting position on the water" [SIMoN].
- The **sink** (stealth): "or simply sink quietly out of view, leaving no trace" [AAB-LH]; "or it can gradually sink down backward, like a disappearing frog, without making a ripple" [Bent 1919, pp. 44-45]; "stealthily sinking out of view" [BoW-I]. Mechanism: "Pied-billed Grebes can trap water in their feathers, giving them great control over their buoyancy" [AAB-O]; "It can squeeze out the air trapped in its feathers and internal air-sacs, and sink effortlessly" [Audubon-S]. Section 6.3 has the depths.
- No source gives how often each start is used. The split below is from video (7.3).

**Underwater.** "I have always supposed that the grebes do not use their wings under water, but Audubon ... says: 'They swam round the sides of the tub in the manner of the puffin, moving their wings in accordance with their feet'" [Bent 1919, p. 45]. Propulsion is mainly by the feet [Audubon]. Parents "dive with young clamped under their wings" [AAB-LH].

**On the surface.**
- Cruising speed: no source. See 7.3.
- Taking off: "When rising from the water it runs along the surface for a long distance, beating the water with its broad paddles" [Bent 1919, p. 44]; "need a long running-flapping start to take off from water" [AAB-LH]. "Rarely seen in flight" [Audubon]; it migrates at night [BoW-I]. The game's grebes should not fly.
- Wing-flapping: among displays, "a quick dive and resurfacing, over a distance of about 20 yards, terminated by vigorous wing flapping and associated splashing of water, and also a prolonged wing flapping of as long as 12 seconds while 'standing' almost vertically in the water" (Mather 1967, in [Johnsgard 1987, p. 119]). In autumn the appendix photos show single wing-flaps and stretches during preening (94897206, 183587316, 315159511).

**Spacing and company.**
- "Except during the breeding season this grebe does not associate much with other species; it is usually seen singly, in pairs, or in very small parties" [Bent 1919, p. 45]. "Less social than most species of grebes and are rarely found in flocks" [BirdWeb]; "Far less sociable than most grebes, almost never in flocks, sometimes found singly on small marshy ponds" [Audubon].
- But: "On Heron Lake, Minnesota, in early October, I have seen pied-billed grebes, in close-massed flocks, containing a hundred or more birds" (Chapman, in [Bent 1919, p. 45]); "In winter they occur on larger water bodies, occasionally in large groups" [AAB-ID]; migrants move "in flocks of various sizes, but such flocks are never very cohesive" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 118]. One Willamette record in the appendix (400094480, 12 September 2026) shows about 15 birds together.
- With other species: "On small bodies of water they mix somewhat with the other water birds, more from necessity than from choice. Threatened danger will almost always result in the separation of the grebes from the ducks with which they may be associated" (Jones, in [Bent 1919, p. 45]). In the appendix photos they swim among Mallards, Gadwall, wigeon, teal and coots; one is being chased by a coot (147749195).
- Aggression: "attacks are often made from under water, and many birds, especially grebes, react by fleeing when a Pied-billed Grebe dives toward them" [BoW-I]; outside their territories "the birds feed commonly without conflict" [Johnsgard 1987, p. 118].
- In the game's zone the birds should keep **tens of metres apart**, each working its own patch (**Estimate**, from the above and the appendix photos, where two birds of a pair or family are usually 1-10 m apart and unrelated birds further).

**Time budget in autumn.** I found **no time-budget study** for this species; the searches returned studies of other waterbirds only. What can be said from the sources: a feeding bird spends about 70% of a bout under water (Bleich's ratio); birds "frequently sunbathe between foraging dives" [BoW-F, Snippet]; and many appendix photos show birds loafing, preening or asleep on the water in the morning. The numbers in section 11 are **Estimates** built on the video (7.3).

- None of the six is a duck-style forward leap. The "crash dive" of the sources ("plunging with head and tail raised above the belly, making a splash" [AAB-LH]) matches the splash dive, with the stern raised before it goes; the sources tie it to alarm.
- Surfacing (the one seen): a pale bulge of water, then **head and body come up together within 0.1 s**, water sheds, and the neck is upright **0.4 s** after the first sign; then 3 s of looking round. No wing-flap after surfacing [Fossé 2016, 3:38.53-3:42].
- Distance under water: the 18 s dive surfaced **about 2.8 body lengths (0.6-0.9 m) ahead** along its heading, in the image plane only (a minimum) [Fossé 2016].

**The sink** (one event, checked at 5 fps by me) [Birds 2025, vT_p9bP40uo 0:27.4-0:30.2]:
- 0:27.4-0:28.0: floating normally, swimming, back flat and low, white stern tuft cocked.
- **0:28.0-0:28.4**: the body drops until the **back is awash**; only the neck, head and the cocked white tuft stay above water. **About 0.4 s.**
- 0:28.4-0:29.2: held low, neck upright, head level (**about 1.4 s** in all to 0:29.8).
- 0:29.2-0:29.4: the stern tuft rises briefly, then sinks.
- 0:29.8-0:29.87: the head goes **straight down**, without tipping forward; gone. No splash; a swirl at 0:29.9-0:30.3.
- So from floating to gone in **1.9 s**, with a clear two-step look: "drop to periscope, pause, slide under". The sources' "gradually", "slowly" and "inch by inch" describe the hiding version, held longer; no clip shows that.

**On the surface** [Fossé 2016]:
- **Face-in-water peering**: about 10 dips in 18 min, the face and bill fully under with the body at the surface, **0.8-1.2 s** each (also 0.8 s in another clip).
- **Swimming** while foraging or moving along the grass edge: **0.2-0.3 body lengths per second, about 0.05-0.08 m/s** (fixed camera, n = 3 intervals, ± 20%). Faster swimming with a V-wake shows in three other clips but could not be timed (camera tracking).
- **Maintenance**: long preening bouts, including belly-preening rolled onto one side with the white belly up (4-10 s); three **bathing bouts** of 5-6 s (head-dunks with the body so low that only the back shows, then wing-thrashing and rearing up to show the belly); rearing wing-flaps of 1-3 s; one slow wing-spread stretch of 3.5 s.
- **Rough time budget of that bird**: under water about 2-3%; surface foraging, swimming and alert about 20%; resting, preening and bathing about 78% (± 10 points). It was a lone summer vagrant loafing at a vegetated edge, so this is the loafing end of the range, not a feeding bird's budget.
- **Feet**: seen only when a preening bird held a foot out of the water (lobed toes, dark blackish-slate) [Vagner 2020, 0:16.6-0:17.3 and 0:30.2-0:31.8], and on a juvenile walking upright on mud with the legs set far back [Chrissy & Andy 2011, 0:53.5-0:58]. Never during a dive.
- **The white stern puff** was raised in 4 clips, including throughout the sink, and flat or not showing in 3.

These are few birds, mostly in summer and out of range; treat them as shapes and orders of magnitude, not measurements.

## 12. Open questions and confidence

**High confidence**
- Sexed wing and culmen (Cramp & Simmons via Johnsgard, n = 13 and 18), BBR's culmen, bill depth, tarsus and toe, and AVONET's bill depth and width; they agree with each other and with the photos' proportions.
- The plumage descriptions in section 4, from BBR, Ridgway, Bent and Johnsgard, and the photos.
- **Late-September age mix**: no breeding plumage; about 60% plain basic, a third striped juveniles (half faint), a few adults with a ring remnant (228 records, 143 birds ageable). Bent's "until late in October" agrees.
- Foraging dive duration and underwater distance, and the escape-dive contrast [Bleich 1975, n = 154 and 52], with Heintzelman & Newberry's two birds in agreement.
- The three ways of going under (crash dive, head-first dive, sink), and that the bird controls how high it floats: many sources.

**Medium confidence**
- **Floating heights** (back 20-37 mm sleek, about 50 mm fluffed; crown 79-106 mm; stern 4-19 mm): six photos, scaled by a 21 mm culmen whose individual variation is about ± 10%, and waterlines read by eye with the camera a little above the water.
- **Bill depth profile** (two strictly side-on photos) and the hook (one photo).
- **Waterline length** 164-172 mm and total length 204-235 mm.

**Low confidence or not verified**
- **Draft and body depth** (47 mm; 75 mm): a buoyancy estimate that assumes an ellipsoidal hull 85 mm wide. No source measures them.
- **Bill width along the bill, toe lobes, tarsus blade, bare tibia**: estimates.
- **How deep and how fast a sinking bird goes**: depths computed from heights and the neck, speed from video only (section 7.3).
- **Time budget and dive-start frequencies**: no study; video estimates only (sections 7.3, 11).
- **Juvenile bill length** in late September: unknown; the model uses the adult mean. If first-autumn bills are shorter, the juvenile trace's scale (0.194 mm/px) is about 8% too large.
- **Snippet-only claims**: the Birds of the World moult, bare-part, dimorphism and sunbathing sentences [BoW-A, BoW-F].
- **Ageing**: 76 of 228 records by a sub-agent (its criteria checked, its photos not all re-viewed); observer bias toward close, striking birds; several records show the same family at Greenway Park on different days.

**Sources I could not read** (not relied on for any number): Palmer 1962 and Wetmore 1965 (lending copies only), Pyle 2008, Cramp & Simmons 1977 directly, the Birds of the World chapters beyond the introduction (Plumages and Molts, Measurements, Behavior, Food), Johansson & Norberg 2001 (the grebe foot).

**Worth doing if higher confidence is needed**
- Read the Birds of the World measurement appendix and Palmer 1962 for tarsus, toe and mass by sex, and a skull-based head length.
- Find a photo or video of a Pied-billed Grebe out of the water, side-on, to measure body depth and the lobed foot.
- Time dive bouts and sinks on a Willamette bird in September.
