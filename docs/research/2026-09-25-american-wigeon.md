# American Wigeon: measurements, late-September plumage, field marks, structure, floating posture, and feeding on open water

Date: 2026-09-25
Purpose: source data for the detailed American Wigeon (`amewig`) model: the swimming posture, a bill with its own width profile and nail, webbed feet, and the `dabbler` foraging style on `swim`. The cast is five wigeons on `swim` in the `open-water` zone of Willow Lake (Willamette Valley, Oregon), about 15-60 m from the vantage, on a late-September morning, `"plumage": "mixed"`.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt of a page I could not read; treat it as unverified. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo and scaled it by the published culmen (or, where stated, by another part). "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated.

The species is *Mareca americana* (formerly *Anas americana*); the older sources call it the Baldpate or American Widgeon.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements, mean of the sexes** (mm; the older tables give ranges, so the means are midpoints unless stated):
  - Exposed culmen **36** (males 33-40, females 31-39.4) [BBR 1884, p. 521; GBS 1918, p. 107]. The bill is short: "much shorter than head" [Kortright 1953, p. 183].
  - Bill **width 16.0** and **depth 12.3** at the nostril (males 16.6 × 13.2, females 15.2 × 11.0; n=5 skins) [Tobias et al. 2022].
  - Wing chord **252** (males ≈ 260, females ≈ 244), tarsus **38**, middle toe **≈ 44** (males 41.9-47.0), tail **92** (males 102, females 81) [BBR 1884; GBS 1918; Phillips 1923, pp. 189-190; Tobias et al. 2022].
  - Mass **737 g** (males 765 g, n=112; females 709 g, n=73) [Kortright 1953, p. 383]. Stretched length 519 (M) and 470 (F) mm [same].
- **Bill shape.** *Mareca*: "Bill small, shorter than the head, rather narrow, the edges parallel to near the end, where they gradually converge to a rounded tip; culmen gently concave" [BBR 1884, p. 516]. On a male skull in top view the bill is **parallel-sided (15.5-16.0 mm) from about 12 mm behind the tip to the base**, and reaches 90% of its width 9 mm behind the tip (photo-measured). The black tip (the nail) is **8-10 mm long** along the culmen (photo-measured, three birds). Depth falls from about 13-14 mm at the base to about 9-10 mm at mid-bill (photo-measured).
- **Late September is the middle of the drakes' moult.** Adult males start the prealternate moult in mid-August and have "largely completed" it by October [Wishart 1985, pp. 174-175]; the older accounts put the change "in September and October", finishing as late as November-December [Bent 1923, p. 93; Kortright 1953, pp. 183-184]. Young males are a month or more behind.
- **iNaturalist, Willamette Valley, 21-30 September: 3 of 44 assessable birds (7%) show the white crown and green eye patch**; 6 more are eclipse or part-moulted males; **35 (80%) look female-type**. None of 22 birds from 11-20 September is a finished drake; 16% of 120 are in 1-12 October. Wigeons arrive in early September and build through October (iNaturalist records: 76 in September, 270 in October).
- **Recommended variants (three) and flock mix: 3 `female` (female-type), 1 `eclipse` (eclipse or part-moulted male), 1 `male` (alternate drake).** The spawner's uniform pick among variants would give too many drakes; weight it about 0.6 / 0.2 / 0.2.
- **Floating posture** (photo-measured on four side-on birds, scaled by the culmen):
  - Back **51-64 mm** above the water (mean ≈ 58); the stern's highest point is the upturned wingtip, **53-87 mm**; tail tip **46-52 mm**, the tail raised **≈ 15-20°** when cruising and **≈ 33°** when feeding. The breast front sits at the waterline.
  - Tail tip **≈ 30 mm past the wingtip** on two birds, which fits "frequently the tip of the tail extends noticeably past the tip of the longest primary" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 158].
  - **Draft ≈ 0.45 of body depth** (range 0.40-0.50; computed from the photo heights, a body depth of about 110 mm, and buoyancy at 0.74 kg). The wigeon "rests buoyantly ... with tail held high", the Gadwall "low and flat" [Kortright 1953, p. 177].
  - Traced bill tip to tail tip at rest: **0.32-0.35 m**.
- **Feeding on open water** is mostly surface picking and head-dipping, not tipping up:
  - Where algae reached the surface, five wigeons made **10.5 head-dips per bird per minute** (262 in 25 bird-minutes). Where the vegetation was on the bottom (62 cm deep) they fed only by robbing coots and pecking near them, **1.4 robberies per bird per minute**. No tip-ups are listed [Knapton & Knudsen 1978, pp. 403-404; computed].
  - Wigeons robbed coots in 5 of 51 mixed fall flocks (9.8%) [Eddleman et al. 1985, p. 469].
  - Tip-ups are "occasional" [Bent 1923, pp. 93, 95].
  - Winter time budget: feeding 46-72% by habitat, locomotion 11-18%, resting 7-27%, comfort 5-12%, alert ≤ 4% [Turnbull & Baldassarre 1987, p. 461].
  - Cruising speed ≈ 0.4-0.5 m/s (Estimate; freely swimming Mallards pick 0.5 m/s [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970]).
- **White forewing on the water:** usually hidden under the flank and scapular feathers, but it often shows on males as a **white bar along the top of the flank, about 100 mm long and 10-15 mm deep** (photo-measured) [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 158].
- **Tracing references** (scratchpad `ref\amewig\`): `amewig-male.jpg` (Forest Grove, 9 Oct 2020, CC BY-NC) for the drake profile, `amewig-female.jpg` (Multnomah Co., 12 Sep 2021, CC BY-NC) for the female, `amewig-eclipse-feeding.jpg` (Forest Grove, 5 Oct 2022, CC BY-NC) for the eclipse male and the feeding pose, and `amewig-eclipse-forewing.jpg` (Multnomah Co., 3 Oct 2021, CC BY-NC) for the forewing on the water. All are local birds within two weeks of the game's date. The harvested photo is head-on and cannot be traced.
- **Eurasian Wigeon:** 7 records in the box between 2 and 11 October and none in September (iNaturalist); a note only, not modelled.

- The bill is **parallel-sided** from about 12 mm behind the tip back to the frontal hinge (about 40 mm), and the tip is a rounded half-disc. This is BBR's "edges parallel to near the end, where they gradually converge to a rounded tip".
- The maximum (16.0 mm) equals AVONET's width at the nostril (16.0), so the horny sheath adds little in width.
- Over the exposed culmen (36 mm), measured from the tip: the width is 38% of the maximum at 4% of the bill, 55% at 8%, 77% at 16%, 90% at 24%, 96% at 32%, and 97-100% from 40% to the base. The widest point is **mid-bill to base, 40-100% of the way back from the tip**; the bill does not flare at the tip as a Shoveler's does.
- The bony tip of the skull may lie up to about 1 mm behind the nail's tip (Estimate), so the rounded end is slightly blunter in life.

- **The lower mandible is black in the drake** ("tip, extreme base and lower mandible, black" [GBS 1918, p. 106; Kortright 1953, p. 183]) and does not segment with the pale upper mandible, so these depths are for the upper mandible only. Add about 2 mm for the lower mandible visible below it (Estimate from the photos).

**Nail and black tip.** On three birds the black at the tip runs **8-10 mm** back along the culmen: 7.9 mm on the female, 10 mm on each drake (photo-measured; `amewig-female.jpg`, `amewig-eclipse-forewing.jpg`, `amewig-male.jpg`). That is 22-27% of the culmen. The black covers the nail and a narrow margin around it.
- **Nail width ≈ 7-9 mm** (Estimate). The bony tip is 8.8 mm wide 2.9 mm behind the tip and 12.3 mm wide 5.8 mm behind it, and the nail covers the front of this rounded end.
- The nail hooks down very slightly over the lower mandible's tip (photos). I could not measure the hook.

**Black ring at the base.** "Bill bright slate-blue, black at the tip, and with a black line along base of culmen" [Phillips 1923, p. 189]; "bill is blue-gray with a narrow black border at the base" [BirdWeb-W]; "A black outline around the base of the bill is a diagnostic feature of American Wigeon" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 163]. On the photos the line is **about 1-2 mm wide** (photo-measured, `amewig-male.jpg`: 5-8 px at 0.20 mm/px).

### 2.5 Head size

- Skull: total length **88 mm**, cranium length 51, width 25, height 22 (male) [Skullsite]. Exposed culmen / skull length = 36 / 88 = 0.41 (computed).
- On the three side-on reference photos, culmen / (bill tip to the back of the feathered head) is **0.355-0.36** (180/503, 198/548, 163/459 px; photo-measured). The feathers at the nape and the male's "slight crest" [Kortright 1953, p. 183] add about 10 mm behind the skull.
- So the head behind the bill base (total head minus culmen) is about **52 mm to the bone** and **62-64 mm to the feathered outline** (computed). Culmen / head is about **0.6-0.7**, a bill a little more than half the head: "much shorter than head" [Kortright 1953, p. 183].

### 2.6 Feet

- "feet small, the tarsus about as long as the bill" [BBR 1884, p. 516]; tarsus 38 against culmen 36 (computed ratio 1.06).
- Middle toe 41.9-47.0 mm in males [BBR 1884, p. 521] (claw treatment not stated; Estimate 42 mm without the claw). The hind toe is "without lobe" [Kortright 1953, p. 184], so the foot type is the plain **webbed** dabbler foot, not the lobed diver's.
- Colour: "legs and feet light bluish" [BBR 1884, p. 521]; "feet, greyish blue, with dusky webs" [Kortright 1953, p. 183]; "Legs and feet yellowish to bluish brown, webs dusky" [Phillips 1923, p. 189]; "legs and feet greenish slate, claws and joints dusky" [GBS 1918, p. 107]. On the harvested photo and `cand_438057192` the legs are grey with a faint olive or blue cast and the webs darker (photo).

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say about moult

**Adult males.**
- "With both old and young birds the molt into the eclipse plumage begins in June and the molt out of this into the adult winter dress is not completed until October or November ... The seasonal molts of the adult consist of the prolonged double molt of the body plumage, into the eclipse in June and July and out of the eclipse in September and October, and the single molt of the flight feathers in August. Old males in the eclipse plumage closely resemble females, except for the wings, which are always distinctive" [Bent 1923, p. 93].
- The only moult study I could read (191 wigeons collected 1976-1978 in British Columbia, Saskatchewan and Manitoba, plus 116 live-trapped and 72 museum specimens) [Wishart 1985, p. 172]:
  - "The first flightless male was collected on 11 July, and the majority of flightless birds were collected 15-31 July. One flightless male was collected on 20 August" [p. 174].
  - "The prealternate moult began on the head and body in mid-August after the wing feathers were almost fully grown. Moult began first on the head, breast, rump, scapulars and flank" [p. 175].
  - "By October, AHY males had largely completed the prealternate moult (Table 2). However, as late as January some still retained a few basic feathers on the back, flanks and rump" [p. 174]; "Adult males normally completed most of the prealternate moult by October" [p. 172].
- "The moult into the eclipse plumage begins in June and lasts through July, being complete in August ... In early autumn the male commences a continuous moult out of the female-like plumage of the eclipse into the full winter plumage ... In the early stages of the moult the adult male can be recognized by the appearance of some of the new, green feathers of winter plumage on the head, and a sprinkling of purplish-pink ones on the chest, and by the first new, vermiculated, pinkish-brown feathers on back and scapulars. During October, the pinkish-brown, vermiculated plumage of the upper body is gradually completed, and the dark chest-feathers of the eclipse plumage give place to the plain purplish-pink of the full winter dress; the vermiculated feathers appear on the sides, and by early December most birds have completed the moult. Considerable variation may be expected in the sequence of the plumage changes" [Kortright 1953, pp. 183-184].
- The eclipse male: "There is a general resemblance to the female, but the coloring of the head, sides of the breast and flanks is much richer in tone, inclining to chestnut instead of to dull brownish. The mantle and scapulars also are somewhat richer in color and may contain a few vermiculated black-and-white feathers. The wing has the large white elbow-patch as in the winter plumage, so that the adult male can always be told at a glance at any time during the summer" [Phillips 1923, p. 190].
- "Nonbreeding males have a reddish brown body with a brownish gray head and a dark smudge around the eye. The prominent white patch in the wing and green secondaries may not always be visible" (gallery photo: California, 23 September 2012) [AAB-ID].

**First-year birds.**
- "When about 4 or 5 weeks old, in August, the young baldpate assumes its first complete plumage ... In this first mottled plumage the sexes are much alike, but in the male the gray feathers of the back begin to appear in September and the progress toward maturity proceeds rapidly ... so that by December or January the most forward birds have acquired a plumage which closely resembles that of the old bird, except on the wings" [Bent 1923, p. 93].
- "Young Male in First Plumage: Closely resembles young female, but by September the median wing-coverts usually begin to show more white, mixed with the gray feathers ... In the month of October obscurely vermiculated feathers begin to appear upon the mantle or scapulars, and a few iridescent green feathers may be present above or behind the eye" [Phillips 1923, p. 190].
- "in young males the vermiculated side-feathers appear in September and at once distinguish the sex" [Kortright 1953, p. 184].
- "The timing of the prealternate I moult varied among individuals. Several acquired alternate I plumage by October, except for a few basic I feathers conspicuous on the flanks, sides and crissum. Others did not reach that stage until six months later ... Males ... achieved alternate I plumage first on the head and neck, followed by the breast and flanks, and then the rump. Moult on the back, scapulars, and tail finished last" [Wishart 1985, p. 174].
- In modern terms the formative plumage "occurs soon after fledging in late August or September and is quickly followed by first-alternate plumage in October-November. The F1 plumage creates little change in appearance in either sex and ordinarily goes undetected. While in first-alternate plumage, young males begin to acquire the appearance of an adult. Females remain largely the same as in their juvenal and F1 plumages" [Cox & Barry 2005, pp. 157-158].

**Reading.** In late September adult drakes are in the middle of their prealternate moult: it starts in mid-August and is "largely completed" by October in the Canadian sample, while the older accounts put the bulk of the change in September and October and the finish in November or December. Young drakes are a month or more behind. So a late-September flock should hold few finished drakes, several eclipse or patchy males that look female-like on the water, and females and juveniles.

### 3.2 Arrival in the Willamette Valley

- Oregon: "common to abundant migrant and wintering bird statewide"; "rare in summer in western Oregon"; "Medium sized flocks are common in fall and winter" [ODFW-W].
- "When wigeon first arrive in the Pacific Northwest in September and October, the birds often congregate on bays and estuaries along the coast ... Major wintering areas in the West include Washington's Puget Sound, Oregon's Willamette Valley, and the Central Valley of California" [Peters 2011].
- Washington: fall migration "lasts from late July through December", peaking "from mid-October to early December" in western Washington [BirdWeb-W].
- "On the fall migration the baldpate starts rather early in September, well in advance of the heavy frosts" [Bent 1923, p. 96]. Bowles, for Washington about 1900: "Eight or ten years ago they used to appear in enormous flocks during the first week in October" [Bent 1923, pp. 96-97]. California: "It begins to arrive in numbers in October" [GBS 1918, p. 108].
- The game's own frequency data (eBird checklists at Ridgefield NWR, Sauvie Island and Smith and Bybee, sampled on the 1st and 15th of each month) has the species on **0.33** of September sample dates and **1.0** of October dates (`data/birds.json`, `monthly`; method in `scripts/lib/frequency.ts`).

**Reading.** Wigeons are nearly absent in summer, arrive in early September, and build to winter numbers through October. On a late-September morning a Willamette lake holds wigeons in small to medium flocks, not yet the winter hundreds; five birds is realistic. The photographed flocks in the tally run from 2 to about 18 birds.

### 3.4 What the typical late-September drake looks like at 30 m

The typical drake on 25 September is an **eclipse or early part-moulted male**: a grey, finely speckled head with a darker smudge around and behind the eye (sometimes a few green or white feathers), a rich chestnut breast and flanks, dark scalloped back, black-and-white tertials, the white forewing showing as a white bar along the top of the flank on some birds, and a whitish patch at the rear flank before a dark undertail. A few are already vinaceous-pink on the body with plain heads (the Pb type). Fewer than one in ten birds shows the white crown and green patch.

At 30 m (computed from `src/player/optics.ts`, taking the field of view as vertical over a 1080-pixel-tall canvas): through the binoculars (7.5°) the field is 3.9 m tall, so a 0.33 m wigeon is about **90 px long** and the forewing bar (≈ 100 × 12 mm) about 27 × 3 px; the crown patch is a few pixels. Through the telescope (2°) the field is 1.05 m tall, the bird is about **340 px long**, the forewing bar about 100 × 12 px, and the black ring at the bill base 1-2 px. So in binoculars the flock reads by shape, the rufous flanks and the male's white rear patch; the telescope resolves the head pattern and bill.

### 3.5 Recommendation

**Model three plumages**, the most the brief allows:

1. **`female`** (female-type): grey speckled head with a dark eye smudge, rufous-orange flanks, dark brown scalloped back with rusty fringes, white belly. It stands for adult females, juveniles of both sexes, and eclipse males whose wing is hidden: **80%** of what a birder sees on a late-September Willamette lake.
2. **`eclipse`** (eclipse or part-moulted male): female-like but with richer chestnut breast and flanks, a greyer, more contrasting head, the **white forewing bar along the top of the flank**, and a white patch at the rear flank before a dark undertail. This is the typical late-September drake.
3. **`male`** (alternate drake): white to cream crown, green eye patch, vinaceous-pink breast and flanks, white rear-flank patch, black undertail. Only 7% in late September, but it is the bird a player learns the species from.

**Flock mix for five birds: 3 `female`, 1 `eclipse`, 1 `male`.** The tally gives an expected 0.35 alternate drakes in five in late September and 0.8 in early October (computed from 7% and 16%), 0.5-1 visibly marked eclipse or part-moulted males, and 3-4 female-types. One drake in five is a little generous for 25 September and right for early October; it keeps the species recognisable at the telescope. If the spawner keeps picking uniformly among variants, the flock gets 1.7 alternate drakes in five, twice too many; it needs weights of about **0.6 / 0.2 / 0.2** or a fixed composition.

### 4.1 `male` (adult male, alternate)

Sources:
- "Forehead and middle of crown (longitudinally) white, generally immaculate; ground-color of head and neck white, sometimes more or less soiled with grayish or brown, and thickly speckled with black; a broad space of metallic blackish green on the side of the occiput, running forward to the eye, and sometimes down the nape, where the two spaces are confluent. Jugulum plain pinkish vinaceous; sides and flanks the same, delicately undulated with black; lower tail-coverts velvety black; rest of lower parts pure white. Back and scapulars grayish white, more or less tinged with the color of the sides, and similarly undulated with black. Wing-coverts immaculate pure white, the anterior portion of the lesser-covert region cinereous, and the last row tipped with velvety black; speculum soft metallic green anteriorly, velvety black posteriorly; tertials velvety black, sharply edged with white, the lower one with its lower edge entirely pure white; primaries plain dark cinereous. Rump cinereous ...; upper tail-coverts velvety black ...; tail hoary cinereous. Bill light grayish blue, the end black; iris brown; legs and feet light bluish" [BBR 1884, pp. 520-521]. "The green patch on the side of the occiput is usually poorly defined, and broken up by lighter spotting" [p. 521].
- "Head, with slight crest; forehead and crown, pure white, sometimes faintly tinged with buffy; patch of glossy green from eye extending around hind head; rest of head and neck, creamy to buffy, speckled with black; chin, dusky ... bill, narrow and much shorter than head, greyish blue, tip, extreme base and lower mandible, black; eye, yellowish brown to dark brown ... white patch on flanks at each side of tail; feet, greyish blue, with dusky webs. Tail, middle feathers, pointed" [Kortright 1953, p. 183].
- "This green patch is often merely a series of green spots" [Phillips 1923, p. 189]. "In some lights the green head stripe can look bronzy" [AAB-ID].
- On the water: "The shiny white crown of the male, dark headpatch and pale grey neck give these parts a somewhat pie-bald appearance; the large white patch on each flank near base of tail is noticeable on the males at considerable distance" [Kortright 1953, p. 185].

By region (photo, `amewig-male.jpg`, a drake on 9 October at Forest Grove):
- **Crown:** white to cream, a broad stripe from the forehead (down to the culmen base) back over the crown to about 45 mm behind the bill base. Above and behind the eye it narrows to about 10 mm, with the green patch below it.
- **Green patch:** iridescent green, black at its edges, from the eye back to the nape: about 50 mm long and 20-25 mm tall, widening at the nape, where it runs down the back of the neck as a dark band. The eye sits in a black surround at the patch's front.
- **Face, cheek and neck:** cream-buff thickly speckled black; the throat and foreneck greyer.
- **Breast and flanks:** plain vinaceous-pink on the breast; the flanks the same with fine black vermiculation, clearest in the upper flank below the scapulars.
- **Back and scapulars:** pinkish-grey, finely vermiculated.
- **Tertials:** long, velvety black with sharp white edges, making black-and-white stripes over the rear flank, the lowest edged entirely white.
- **White rear-flank patch:** about 13 mm wide and 20 mm tall at the rear of the flank, just above the waterline, in front of the **velvety black undertail coverts** (photo-measured).
- **Forewing:** white; hidden on this bird (section 4.4).
- **Tail:** dark grey, pointed, the central feathers longest, projecting about 30 mm beyond the wingtip.
- **Bill:** pale blue-grey ("bright slate-blue" [Phillips 1923, p. 189]), black nail 8-10 mm, a 1-2 mm black ring at the base, black lower mandible.

### 4.2 `female` (female-type: adult female, juvenile, eclipse male with hidden wing)

Sources:
- "Above, dusky grayish brown, with transverse, rather distant, bars of dull white or light ochraceous. Wing-coverts dark dull cinereous, broadly tipped and bordered with white; speculum dull black. Head and neck streaked with blackish upon a dull whitish ground, the former color prevailing on the nape and behind the eye. Jugulum pale grayish vinaceous, the feathers darker beneath the surface; sides and flanks deeper vinaceous; lower tail-coverts transversely spotted with brown; rest of lower parts pure white" [BBR 1884, p. 521].
- "Head, whitish to creamy, streaked with dusky, darker behind eye and on back of head; neck, spotted or barred with dusky; bill and eye, same as in male. Body. Back, rump and scapulars, greyish brown, feathers edged with yellowish brown and greyish, scapulars, edged and barred with yellowish brown, longest one tipped with white; chest, yellowish to reddish brown, suffused with greyish brown; sides, yellowish to reddish brown, tipped with paler; breast and belly, white ... Tail, pointed, greyish brown, edged narrowly with whitish ... under coverts, white, barred with brownish ... tertials, dusky brown, slightly glossed with bronzy, outer webs, dusky brown to blackish, and margined with white" [Kortright 1953, p. 184].
- Juvenile: "By August both sexes are similar to adult female except feathers of scapulars are less margined with yellowish brown" [Kortright 1953, p. 184]; "Like adult female, but mantle more uniform and lacking light bars for the most part ... Green of speculum absent or poorly developed. Tips of tail-feathers blunt. No elongation of central feathers" [Phillips 1923, p. 190].
- "Generally, female Americans have a cold gray or pale cream head color"; the flecking is "heaviest around the eye and forming a distinct eye-patch"; "The cold-toned head of American Wigeon contrasts with a dark brown breast and the pinkish or orange washed flanks. The bright flanks usually catch the eye as the most colorful part of the bird. In adult female Americans, the breast is often coarsely marked with dark brown spotting, while immatures are usually much less distinctly marked. The scapulars and mantle feathers have broad rusty fringes" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 161]. "A dusky line running down the culmen identifies an immature in either species" [p. 160].
- "Females and nonbreeding males are warm brown with a brownish gray head and a dark smudge around the eye. Both sexes have a pale gray bill with a black tip" [AAB-ID].

By region (photo, `amewig-female.jpg`, 12 September, Multnomah County):
- **Head and neck:** grey, finely and densely speckled dark (a salt-and-pepper texture of about 1 mm flecks), slightly paler on the forehead and lores; a **darker smudge around and behind the eye**, about 15-20 mm across.
- **Breast:** warm brown with dark scalloping where it meets the water.
- **Flanks:** **rufous-orange**, plain or with faint pale tips, from the breast to the rear flank; the brightest part of the bird.
- **Back and scapulars:** dark brown centres with broad **rusty-buff fringes**, making a scalloped pattern of feathers about 20-25 mm long.
- **Tertials:** dark brown to blackish with **thin white edges**, lying as long stripes over the rear flank.
- **Rear:** undertail white barred brown (usually at or below the water); tail grey-brown, pointed.
- **Bill:** as the male's, the black ring at the base "present on most adult females and on the majority of immatures" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 163].

### 4.3 `eclipse` (eclipse or part-moulted male)

Sources: Phillips's eclipse male (section 3.1: head, breast sides and flanks "much richer in tone, inclining to chestnut", "a few vermiculated black-and-white feathers", "the large white elbow-patch"); Kortright's early-autumn male ("some of the new, green feathers of winter plumage on the head, and a sprinkling of purplish-pink ones on the chest"); AAB's nonbreeding male ("reddish brown body with a brownish gray head and a dark smudge around the eye").

By region (photo, `amewig-eclipse-feeding.jpg`, 5 October, Forest Grove, and `amewig-eclipse-forewing.jpg`, 3 October, Multnomah County):
- **Head:** grey-brown, finely speckled, with a darker patch from the eye back over the ear, sometimes a green sheen behind the eye and a few white feathers on the forehead.
- **Breast and flanks:** rich **chestnut**, darker and redder than the female's, with a few vinaceous, vermiculated feathers mixed in.
- **Back:** dark brown, scalloped, with some grey vermiculated feathers.
- **Tertials:** **black with sharp white edges** (bolder than the female's).
- **Forewing:** the white bar along the top of the flank (section 4.4).
- **Rear:** a whitish patch at the rear flank before a dark undertail, smaller and duller than the alternate male's.

### 4.4 The white forewing on a floating bird

- Adult males show a white forewing (the "elbow-patch") in every plumage [Phillips 1923, p. 190]. First-year males have "clouded white" coverts with dusky centres [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 158]; adult females have a white bar on the greater coverts [p. 160], which is hidden at rest.
- On the water the coverts "are often hidden beneath the scapulars and flank feathers when the bird is not in flight ... These coverts can also be visible on swimming or standing birds, on which an oval-shaped patch can be seen peeking through the space between the scapulars and flank feathers" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 158]. "The prominent white patch in the wing ... may not always be visible" [AAB-ID].
- On close side-on photos of floating males in the tally the forewing shows on most (for example 62160899, 66124292, 97146479, 138047832, 242933477) as a **white bar along the top edge of the flank feathers, below the scapulars and in front of the tertials**. On `amewig-eclipse-forewing.jpg` it is **about 100-110 mm long and 10-15 mm deep at its widest**, tapering at both ends, running from about 45% to 75% of the way back from the breast front to the tail tip (photo-measured). On `amewig-male.jpg` it is hidden.
- Recommendation: paint it on the folded wing's upper edge for `male` and `eclipse`, as a band the flank's top edge half-covers, so it shows as a thin bar most of the time.

### 4.5 Telling a female wigeon from a female Gadwall or Mallard

- **Bill.** Wigeon: short, blue-grey, black tip, and "A black outline around the base of the bill is a diagnostic feature of American Wigeon" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 163]. The Gadwall's bill is orange-sided with a dark ridge and the Mallard's long and orange-and-black (the design's marks; see the Gadwall and Mallard research for their sources).
- **Head.** Round, puffy, cold grey with a dark eye smudge [Cox & Barry 2005, pp. 158, 161], against the brown heads with a dark eye-line of the Gadwall and Mallard.
- **Body.** "the duck might easily be mistaken for the female gadwall, though it has a white unspotted breast and shows more white in the greater wing coverts ... It can readily be distinguished from the mallard duck by its smaller size and by the absence of the conspicuous white borders of the blue speculum" [Bent 1923, pp. 94-95]. The wigeon's rufous-orange flanks are plain; the Gadwall's and Mallard's are mottled. "The lighter color of the lower surface and the white axillars and dark speculum serve to distinguish the female Baldpate from the female Gadwall, our only other duck of comparable size and coloration" [GBS 1918, p. 108].
- **Posture.** "The Gadwall sits low and flat on the water, while the Baldpate rests buoyantly, seemingly more alert, with tail held high; the Baldpate shows a conspicuous white patch on flanks, lacking in the Gadwall" [Kortright 1953, p. 177]; Gadwalls "are longer of body than Baldpates and rest less buoyantly on the water" [p. 185].
- **Tail.** Pointed [Kortright 1953, p. 184], against the Mallard's rounded, white-edged tail.

### 4.6 Speculum, eye and feet

- **Speculum** (hidden on the floating bird, shown in flight or wing-flap): male "soft metallic green anteriorly, velvety black posteriorly" [BBR 1884, p. 521]; female "dull black, occasionally with a small patch of metallic green" [GBS 1918, p. 107]; first-year females "very little glossy black or green" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 160].
- **Underwing:** "lining, pale ashy grey, without mottling; axillars, white" [Kortright 1953, p. 183]. The harvested photo shows it (section 8).
- **Eye:** iris "dark brown" [Phillips 1923, p. 189], "yellowish brown to dark brown" [Kortright 1953, p. 183], "hazel" [GBS 1918, p. 106]; brown on `amewig-eclipse-forewing.jpg` (photo). Visible eye **7.9-8.8 mm** across (photo-measured, two birds). "The eyelid is very pale in American" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 163].
- **Feet:** section 2.6. Hidden while floating.

## 5. Structure

### 5.1 Profile and proportions

- **Head:** "American Wigeon typically had a higher forehead that reaches a slight peak just before or above the eye. After the peak, the head tapers off smoothly to a fairly rounded nape. This gives American a somewhat puffy, rounded appearance to the head" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 158]. "Head, with slight crest" (male) [Kortright 1953, p. 183]. On the photos the forehead rises steeply from the bill base, the crown peaks above or just in front of the eye, and the male's nape feathers make a slight bulge behind the crown (photo).
- **Neck:** held short at rest: "They tend to sit on the water with their heads pulled down, giving them a no-necked look" [AAB-ID]. Raised when alert (the crown 120-125 mm above the water against 95 mm with the neck drawn in; section 6).
- **Bill:** section 2.4. Short, parallel-sided, rounded tip, concave culmen, black nail. Points **42-59° below level** on the four reference birds, feeding or not (photo-measured).
- **Body on the water:** long and low, the back line nearly level from the neck base to the tertials, the breast full and rounded in front, the stern rising to the upturned wingtips and the raised pointed tail. "They sit buoyantly on the water, chest low and tail well elevated; bend of wing is carried low, and tip of wing is pointed upwards" [Kortright 1953, p. 185].

So the scales are right within about 10%, and if anything slightly small.

### 5.2 Folded wing and tail on the water

- **Wing:** most of the folded wing is hidden under the scapulars above and the flank feathers ("side pocket") below; what shows is the tertials and the primary tips, which rise behind the back to the stern's highest point, 53-87 mm above the water (section 6). The white forewing shows as a bar on some males (section 4.4).
- **Tertials:** long and "lanceolate" in the adult male [BBR 1884, p. 516], velvety black edged white; they lie over the rear flank as a fan of black-and-white stripes pointing up and back (photo).
- **Wingtip against tail:** "frequently the tip of the tail extends noticeably past the tip of the longest primary. Often the tip of the longest primary falls somewhere between the middle of the tail and the longest uppertail covert" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 158]. Photo-measured: tail tip **30 mm** (drake) and **29 mm** (female) past the wingtip, horizontally.
- **Tail:** "Tail-feathers acuminate, the middle pair projecting considerably beyond the rest" [BBR 1884, p. 516]; "Tail, middle feathers, pointed" [Kortright 1953, p. 183]. So `wedge`. Juveniles have "Tips of tail-feathers blunt. No elongation of central feathers" [Phillips 1923, p. 190], but at 30 m that difference is invisible; use `wedge` for all three variants. Carried above the water at about 15-20° when cruising and about 33° when feeding (section 6).

### 5.3 Body cross-section

- **Width:** about **125-130 mm** across the breast, feathers included, on the harvested head-on photo (photo-measured, scaled by the bill's width at its base taken as 16 mm; low confidence).
- **Depth:** about **105-120 mm** from the back to the keel (the lowest belly), from the museum mount's depth / length ratio (0.56 on `mus_15zz`, Commons, a standing drake) applied to the traced body lengths, and 120 mm from the neck base to the belly on the harvested photo (photo-measured; low confidence). I use **110 mm**.
- **Feet:** section 2.6; the web spans the three front toes; hind toe small and unlobed.

- **Draft.** No photo shows the hull under water, so the draft is computed:
  - Body depth ≈ 110 mm (section 5.3); back above water ≈ 58 mm, so draft ≈ **52 mm, 0.47 of the depth** (computed).
  - Buoyancy check: a 0.74 kg bird displaces 740 cm³ of fresh water. An ellipsoidal hull 200 mm long, 125 mm wide and 110 mm deep, cut 52 mm above its keel, has 661 cm³ under water (computed; the neck base and tail add the rest). So a draft of 45-50% is consistent with the bird's mass.

The pitch is about 5-10° bow-down (Estimate from the stern rise of about 20 mm over about 200 mm of body).

## 7. Swimming and feeding behavior

### 7.1 What the sources say

- **Feeding methods.** "The baldpate feeds on or near the surface by dabbling in the mud or tipping up in shallow water" [Bent 1923, p. 93]. "Dips bill in water to pick up aquatic plants and insects from the surface or tips up to pluck submerged vegetation" [AAB-ID]. "on the water they sit lightly and pivot frequently as they peck for their food" [Kortright 1953, p. 186]; "they pivot frequently as they feed and appear ever on the alert" [p. 185]. They "nibble vegetation from the water's surface or tip up to reach underwater vegetation" [AAB-LH]. "American Wigeons eat a higher proportion of plant matter than any other dabbling duck thanks to their short gooselike bill" [AAB-OV].
- **Head-dips and robbery, timed** [Knapton & Knudsen 1978, pp. 403-404], Oak Hammock Marsh, Manitoba, October 1976-1977. Three feeding methods were defined: "(a) A robbery occurred when a wigeon snatched food directly from the bill of a coot ... (b) Feeding pecks near a coot occurred immediately after a coot surfaced ... (c) An independent feeding was a motion in which a wigeon dipped its head below the surface."

| Area | Water, vegetation | Observation | Robberies | Pecks near coots | Head-dips |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (1976) | 40 cm, filamentous algae at the surface | 11 min, 77 wigeons and 27 coots | 0 | 0 | 191 |
| B (1976) | 42 cm, vegetation on the bottom | 20 min, 20 wigeons and 27 coots | 72 | 43 | 0 |
| C (1977) | 48.0 cm mean, vegetation at the surface | 5 wigeons × 5 min each | 0 | 0 | **262** |
| D (1977) | 61.7 cm mean, vegetation on the bottom | 6 wigeons × 5 min each | **41** | **29** | 0 |

  - Computed per bird: area C **10.5 head-dips per minute** (262 in 25 bird-minutes), one every 5.7 s; area D **1.4 robberies and 1.0 near-coot pecks per minute** (in 30 bird-minutes), robberies "59% of the total number of feedings".
  - "In a typical robbery a wigeon swam to where a coot had dived and lunged at the coot as soon as it surfaced"; "Wigeons moved apparently randomly among the coots, taking advantage of a feeding or robbing opportunity when it arose" [p. 404].
  - No tip-ups are listed in 40-62 cm of water.
- **Coots in fall flocks.** In 184 mixed flocks during fall migration in Oklahoma, wigeons fed cooperatively with coots in 8 of 51 flocks (15.7%) and robbed them in 5 (9.8%); 36 (70.6%) did not interact [Eddleman et al. 1985, p. 469, Table 4; my reading of the OCR columns, which add to 51]. "Wigeons and Gadwalls were the only species observed robbing food from coots" [p. 466]. Townsend's five baldpates "helped themselves to the spoils, tipping up occasionally to catch some sinking weed. They seemed even to be able to perceive the coot coming up through the water, for they would begin to swim toward the spot just before the coot emerged" (in [Bent 1923, pp. 95-96]).
- **Swimming.** "Compared to other dabbling ducks such as Gadwalls, Mallards, and Green-winged Teals, American Wigeons spend more time swimming and cover more ground between foraging periods" [AAB-LH]. Mallards trained in a flume swam at 0.35-0.70 m/s, with the minimum cost of transport at 0.50 m/s, and "Ducks swimming freely on a pond were observed to swim at the speed calculated in experimental trials to give minimum cost of transport" [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970, abstract; paper not read in full].
- **Daily pattern.** "Where not disturbed it is liable to feed at any time during the day, though it is always more active in the early morning or toward night. But, as it seldom enjoys much security, it more often spends the day skulking in the reeds, dozing on some sunny bank or playing about on open water at a safe distance from land" [Bent 1923, p. 93]. "Like the Black Duck it has become, to a large extent, a night feeder and spends the hours of daylight in the safety that is lent by distance from shore" [Kortright 1953, p. 187].
- **Time budget** (wintering, Eufaula NWR, Alabama, November-February 1983-1985; 751 wigeons in 10-minute focal samples scored every 15 s): feeding "dominated activity in all months (45-71%)" but "was variable throughout the day (32-95%)"; locomotion "varied (0-50%)"; alert, courtship and agonistic together under 5% [Turnbull & Baldassarre 1987, pp. 457, 459]. By habitat [p. 461, Table 1]:

| Activity (%) | Lower impoundment, deep open water (23 days) | River sandbars (28) | Upper impoundment, shallower (14) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feeding | 63.2 | 46.1 | 72.0 |
| Locomotion | 17.9 | 12.6 | 10.7 |
| Resting | 10.2 | 26.9 | 6.7 |
| Comfort | 6.0 | 12.4 | 5.1 |
| Alert | 1.5 | 1.3 | 4.0 |
| Courtship | 0.5 | 0.3 | 0.6 |
| Agonistic | 0.9 | 0.4 | 0.9 |

  The same study's Mallards fed only 15.5-36.1% and rested 27.8-48.8% of the time; the wigeons "probably spent more time feeding than did Mallards in order to obtain and process the lower quality natural foods" [p. 461].
- **Flocks:** "Medium sized flocks are common in fall and winter" [ODFW-W]; they "migrate mostly during the day, forming small flocks during spring migration and larger flocks (often more than 100 birds) during fall migration" [AAB-LH]. **No source I could read gives nearest-neighbour distances.**

### 7.2 Photos and video (Estimate)

- **Tally photos.** Of the 100 records with assessable birds, a few show feeding on the water: surface feeding with the bill at the water (242002491, 243454823, 244543494, 320580269, 137691785), grazing on land (62758720, 183762612, 245481688), none tipping up. Loafing and asleep birds are common (245481639-642, 187046640). Neighbours in small groups on the water are mostly **1-3 body lengths apart, 0.3-1.0 m** (by eye on 340496465, 343449919, 246429602, 319584594, 61629146); loafing pairs sit within one body length.
- **Video.** YouTube refused downloads during this session (rate limit and bot check), so the only clip used is [Danese 2024], a drake on open water in December, 57 s at 60 fps (CC BY-SA 4.0), examined at 2 fps.
  - 0.5-1.5 s and 49.0 s: the bird swims with its **bill at the surface**, head low and neck forward, for 0.5-1 s at a time.
  - 12-16 s: preening with the head turned back over the scapulars.
  - 22-43 s and 46-56 s: swimming with the head up, turning in wide arcs.
  - **No tip-up** in 57 s.
  - The camera pans with the bird, so no speed could be measured.

### 7.3 What the swim behavior should do on open water (Estimate)

- **Surface picking and head-dips are the wigeon's open-water feeding.** A feeding bird swims slowly (about 0.1-0.2 m/s; Estimate) with the bill at the surface, pivots often, and dips the head under about **every 6 s** (10.5 per minute; [Knapton & Knudsen 1978], computed). A dip lasts about 1-2 s (Estimate; the rate leaves 3-5 s between dips for swallowing and swimming).
- **Tip-ups are occasional.** No timed study counts them; Knapton & Knudsen list none in 40-62 cm, and Bent and Townsend say "occasionally". Recommend **about 1 tip-up per 10 feeding bouts**, lasting 2-4 s (Estimate). By comparison the design's Mallard dabbler tips up far more often.
- **Following coots** is real and characteristic (up to 59% of feedings where the food is on the bottom), but it needs coots nearby. Willow Lake has a coot cast; it can be a later option (section 11).
- **Time budget for a late-September morning:** feeding about 50%, swimming about 15-20%, loafing about 25%, preening about 10%, alert about 2% (Estimate, from the winter budgets above, shading toward loafing in daylight as Bent and Kortright describe).

## 11. Swim-style settings

The wigeon fits the `dabbler` style's state machine; it needs **different settings, not its own style**. Its feeding is head-dips and surface picking with rare tip-ups, where the Mallard's weight is on tip-ups. The one behaviour the `dabbler` style lacks, swimming to a surfacing coot and lunging at it, is characteristic but optional.

| Setting | Value | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Cruise speed (travelling between feeding spots) | **0.4 m/s** (0.3-0.5) | **Estimate**: freely swimming Mallards choose 0.5 m/s [Prange & Schmidt-Nielsen 1970]; a smaller duck slightly slower. Today's `SPEED` 0.45 is fine |
| Feeding swim speed | **0.1-0.2 m/s**, with frequent pivots (turns of 30-90° every few seconds) | **Estimate**; "pivot frequently as they peck" [Kortright 1953, p. 186] |

## 12. Open questions and confidence

**High confidence**
- Bill width 16.0 and depth 12.3 at the nostril (five skins), and the parallel-sided bill with a rounded tip (the genus description and the skull agree).
- Mass (Kortright, n=112 and 73), wing and tarsus ranges from four independent tables.
- The moult timing: adult drakes moulting out of eclipse from mid-August to October and later; young males later still (four sources agree).
- The late-September plumage mix: 7% finished drakes and 80% female-types among 44 assessable birds in 21-30 September, with the October increase as the sources predict.
- The feeding methods on water: head-dips and surface picking dominate; coots are robbed where the food is deep; tip-ups are occasional.

**Medium confidence**
- **Culmen 36 mm**: no published mean I could read; chosen from four sets of ranges. BBR's males (33-38) run 2 mm shorter than GBS's (34.7-40).
- **Floating heights**: four photos, each scaled by a single bird's culmen (±7%), with the waterline read from the bow and stern contacts to cancel the camera's elevation.
- **Tail past the wingtip** (29-30 mm): two photos, and Cox & Barry call the feature variable.
- **Draft 0.45**: computed from the photo heights and an estimated body depth, checked against buoyancy; no photo shows the hull under water.
- **Bill depth profile and nail length**: one to three photos each.

**Low confidence or not verified**
- **Body width (125-130 mm) and depth (110 mm)**: from a head-on photo scaled by the bill base and a museum mount's proportions.
- **Nail width** (7-9 mm): estimated from the skull's tip outline.
- **Middle toe**: males only, claw treatment not stated.
- **Tip-up rate and duration, head-dip duration, bout lengths, spacing, time budget for late September**: estimates. The only timed study [Knapton & Knudsen 1978] is short (55 bird-minutes) and in October in Manitoba; the time budget [Turnbull & Baldassarre 1987] is from Alabama in winter.
- **The Eddleman et al. Table 4 counts** were read from a scrambled OCR; the column sums (51 flocks) support my reading.
- **The tally**: one observer (me), first photos only; several photos are small. The F class mixes females, juveniles and hidden eclipse males.
- **Snippet-only claims:** none. Wishart 1979 (structural size) was seen only as a search abstract and is not used for any number.

**Sources I could not read** (not relied on for any number)
- **Palmer 1976** (*Handbook of North American Birds*, vol. 2) and **Pyle 2008**: lending-only on the Internet Archive.
- **Johnsgard 1978/2010** (*Ducks, Geese, and Swans of the World*): the UNL repository blocked downloads.
- **Birds of the World** (Mini et al. 2020): paywalled.
- **Wishart 1983a** (PhD thesis on the wigeon's behavioural ecology, with time budgets) and **Munro 1949** (*Studies of waterfowl in British Columbia: Baldpate*): not found online.

"Eurasian Wigeon has occurred throughout North America, being most numerous on the Pacific Coast from Canada to California. More than one hundred Eurasian Wigeons can sometimes be found mixed in with very large flocks of American Wigeons in British Columbia and northwestern Washington" [Cox & Barry 2005, p. 157]. "Hybrid American-Eurasian Wigeons are regularly found during winter in Washington" [BirdWeb-W]. The drake has a "bright, cinnamon-red head, a creamy crown" and a grey back [Kortright 1953, p. 185]. So on 25 September a Eurasian Wigeon at Willow Lake would be unlikely; it arrives with the October flocks.
