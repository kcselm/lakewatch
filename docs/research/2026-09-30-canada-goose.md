# Canada Goose: measurements, late-September populations and age, field marks, floating and standing postures and neck shapes, grazing, vigilance, family movements between water and grass, and (for later rounds) flight

Date: 2026-09-30
Purpose: source data for the detailed Canada Goose (`cangoo`) model, round 1 of three goose rounds. In this round the goose floats on the lake like the existing swimmers (relaxed, alert, head-dipping and tipping up) and grazes on a new grassy flat 25-45 m from the vantage; one family walks out of the water onto the grass and later back in. The scene is a Willamette Valley lake on a morning of about 20-30 September. At 35 m the telescope (2°) shows about 1.1 mm per pixel on a 1080-pixel-tall view, the binoculars (7.5°) about 4.2 mm, the eye (60°) about 37 mm (computed). Flight, arrivals and departures are for later rounds; section 7.8 collects what I found for them.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine or full-text-search excerpt of a book I could not open; treat it as unverified. "Abstract only" means I read the abstract, not the paper. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means measured in pixels on a cited photo and scaled by the exposed culmen (56.4 mm, section 2.6, unless stated). "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements** (mean of the sexes, the Western Canada Goose *B. c. moffitti*; mm): exposed culmen **56**, wing chord **489**, tail **151**, tarsus **92**, middle toe without claw **82** [Aldrich 1946, p. 97: 11 adult males and 6 adult females]. Bill depth at the base 20-30 and width 19-30 [BBR 1884, p. 457]; AVONET's six large skins give depth **17.7** and width **21.8** at the nostrils (computed). Mass about **4.0 kg** (Estimate inside the sourced 3.6-5.0 kg; section 2.4). Stretched length 0.89-0.95 m; **the model's traced length is about 0.64 m** (photo-measured, floating).
- **Who is on the lake.** On 20-30 September the valley's Canada Geese are almost all the **resident, pale-breasted Western Canada Goose (*moffitti*)**. The dark Dusky (*occidentalis*) first reaches the refuges in the last days of September, but its mean arrival is 31 October to 14 November [PFC 2015, p. 13]. **Model *moffitti*: large, silvery-pale breast, no white neck ring.**
- **One version: `adult`.** Hatch-year birds are 4-6 months old, full grown, and differ only in duller, softer plumage, a faintly speckled cheek and notched tail tips [Bent 1925, p. 212; BBR 1884, p. 456; Hanson 1962]. In 120 sampled local photos none could be aged as a juvenile (section 3.2). At 25-45 m a gosling of the year reads as an adult.
- **Group: about 8** (6-12): one **family of 5** (the pair and 3 young of the year) that walks out to graze, and a **loose unit of 3** (a pair and a yearling, or failed breeders) that stays near. Families average 4.7 birds in resident geese [Combs 1989, p. 365]; Western goslings survive to fledging at 0.49 [Eberhardt 1987]; clutches are "usually 5 or 6" [Bent 1925, p. 210]. In the local photos geese were in groups of 6-12 in 26 of 116 records (section 3.2).
- **Floating** (photo-measured, three side-on birds): back **0.13 m** above the water, crown **0.24 m** at rest, tail tip about **0.11 m**, waterline length **0.50 m**, draft about **0.06 m**, so the water meets the body about **0.30 of the body depth up from the keel**.
- **Neck shapes** (crown above the top of the back): floating at rest **0.10-0.17 m**; standing relaxed **0.21-0.23 m** (0.43-0.46 of the 0.49-0.50 m standing height); alert on the water **0.23-0.43 m**; grazing: the bill on the grass about **0.17 m ahead of the toes**, the neck leaving the body nearly level and bending down 55-65° near the head.
- **Grazing:** bite rate about **0.8-1.0 per second** for a 4 kg goose on short grass (computed from captive Canada Geese [Heuermann et al. 2011, Table 2]). Head-down bouts **8.4 s (gander) and 10.7 s (goose)**, head-up bouts **8.0 s and 5.6 s** [Sedinger & Raveling 1990, p. 174]. In local grazing groups about **63% of birds were head-down** (section 3.2).
- **Vigilance:** in autumn a **parent gander is alert 30-35% of the day, his mate 14%**, paired birds without young 5-20% [Caithamer et al. 1996, Table 1]; goslings 1.6-2.9% [Fowler & Ely 1997, Table 1]. **There is no coordinated sentinel turn-taking:** "We detected no systematic coordination of alert periods by pairs" [Sedinger & Raveling 1990, p. 176]. The old "sentinel relieved by its companions" [Bent 1925, p. 213] is anecdote. **Model each bird's own graze/alert cycle by role: the gander most, the goose less, goslings rarely, edge birds more.**
- **Order:** "any particular family member might be in the lead position at any one time", but the family follows the gander's change of heading, not the young's [Raveling 1969a, p. 676].
- **Feeding on water:** geese head-dip with the long neck and tip up "with their tails and feet in the air" [Kortright 1942, p. 89; Bent 1925, p. 213]; no timed upend or reach depth exists for the species (estimates in section 11). In the local photos 8 birds were head-dipping and none upending.
- **Cackling Goose:** 46 research-grade records in the box from 16-30 September against 208 from 16-31 October; on eBird it is on 2-14% of county checklists in the last week of September and 7-35% in late October (section 3.4). **A later Cackling round is warranted only for an October or winter scene**, not for this late-September morning.

## 3. Late-September populations, plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

**The resident Western Canada Goose.**
- "Western Canada geese are the only Canada goose subspecies that nest in Oregon and Washington"; "These geese were not originally found in western Oregon and Washington, but spread from east of the Cascades and were introduced by private aviculturists and wildlife managers" [Warren 2006, p. 63].
- "Common permanent resident throughout most of the state, augmented in winter by birds from British Columbia and Alberta ... escapees from captive breeding flocks and enthusiasm for transplanting geese have resulted in broad distribution across the state" [Marshall et al. 2003] (Snippet). "Locally common permanent resident. Common transient and winter visitant" [Gilligan et al. 1994, p. 42] (Snippet).
- Special early hunting seasons in northwest Oregon may run 1-15 September "based on previous evaluations that indicted [sic] the absence of migrant Canada geese during this period" [PFC 1998, p. 26].
- "Resident geese ... in the Willamette Valley have been marked with white neck collars" [PNGM 2015, p. 5]; white or black plastic collars mark westerns [Warren 2006, p. 9]. A collar is optional detail, not a plumage mark.

**When the migrants come.**
- **Dusky:** "begin migrating from post-molt staging areas in Alaska to wintering areas in late September (range, 22 Sept.-5 Dec.) ... Mean arrival dates in the Pacific Northwest were 31 October, 5 November, and 14 November for Copper River Delta, Middleton Island, and Prince William Sound birds, respectively (range, 9 Oct.-25 Dec.; all locations)" [PFC 2015, p. 13], from satellite telemetry. Earlier counts: "duskys began arriving at Baskett Slough National Wildlife Refuge (NWR) in Oregon on 25 September 1971-73, with the first duskys at William L. Finley NWR on 1 October 1973 ... Naughton (1993) reported the first duskys at William L. Finley NWR on 12 October in both 1985 and 1986" [Bromley & Rothe 2003, p. 31]. "In fall, the earliest migrant Dusky Canada Geese arrive in late Sep, but the first large influx of migrants, including all subspecies, usually comes in early Nov" [Marshall et al. 2003] (Snippet). Finley's sanctuary season "starts as the Dusky canada geese arrive, on October 1st" [USFWS Finley].
- **Lesser and Taverner's:** about 3,000-5,000 Lessers and 50,000 Taverner's winter in the Willamette Valley and lower Columbia [Marshall et al. 2003] (Snippet); Taverner's (now a Cackling Goose) arrive with the Cackling Geese [Mlodinow et al. 2008, p. 351].
- The whole winter goose population of northwest Oregon and southwest Washington has grown "to over 400,000. Prior to the 1970s, the winter goose population averaged fewer than 25,000 geese, and most of these were the dusky Canada goose subspecies" [PNGM 2015, p. 4].

**Breast colour by subspecies.** Westerns: "light gray in younger birds to almost white in adult birds" [PNGM 2015, p. 8]; "the silvery-breasted Western Canada Goose and the dark-breasted but slightly smaller Dusky" [Marshall et al. 2003] (Snippet). Dusky: "rich chocolate brown ... very dark gray" [PNGM 2015, p. 6]. Neck ring in westerns: "White neck ring not common" [Warren 2006, p. 57]; BBR found the white collar of the pale large geese "very indistinct or obsolete on account of the light color of the jugulum", but "usually very distinct" in *occidentalis* [BBR 1884, p. 457].

**Age.**
- "Fully grown when about 6 weeks old ... during the first summer and fall at least a decided difference. The plumage of young birds looks softer and the colors are duller and more blended. The head and neck are duller, browner black; the cheeks are more brownish white ... the light edgings above are not so distinct; and the sides of the chest and flanks are indistinctly mottled, rather than clearly barred. During the fall and winter these differences disappear ... by spring the young birds are practically indistinguishable from adults" [Bent 1925, p. 212].
- "Young: Similar to the adult, but the colors duller, the markings less sharply defined; black of the neck passing gradually below into the grayish of the jugulum; white cheek patches usually finely speckled with dusky; light-colored tips to the contour-feathers broader" [BBR 1884, p. 456].
- In hand: "The frayed or notched tip in the tail feathers of immature waterfowl"; "The primaries of the immature are pointed; those of the adult are obtuse or rounded"; narrow breast feathers with "the prominence of the shaft ... which gives the breast a streaked appearance" [Hanson 1962]. "Tail feathers may be notched, breast feathers relatively narrow, and outer primaries more pointed in HY than AHY birds" [WDFW 2011, Table 3, p. 16]. Immatures of the Cackling subspecies "average paler than adults" [Mlodinow et al. 2008, p. 356].
- **How old they are.** "Nesting begins in Mar in most areas of Oregon ... the ~28-day incubation period ... Broods present Mar-Aug; most young flying by Jul ... [parents] regain flight at same time the goslings are first able to fly at about 8 wk of age" [Marshall et al. 2003] (Snippet); Western goslings in California flew at 49-56 days (Moffitt 1931, in Bellrose 1976) (Snippet). A March-April hatch is **5-6.5 months old** on 20-30 September; a May hatch 4-4.5 months (computed).
- Juvenile Duskies measure a little smaller in wing and tail: juvenile males wing 462.0, tail 129.7, against adults' 479.0 and 143.5 [Bromley & Rothe 2003, Table 1]; their culmens are the same.

### 3.3 Subspecies: what the model represents

**The model is a *moffitti*, the resident Western Canada Goose:** the largest of the valley's geese (culmen 56 mm), the palest breast ("light gray ... to almost white"), the white cheek patch usually unbroken under the throat, no neck ring. A Dusky on 20-30 September is possible but would be one of the first few (mean arrival 31 October or later [PFC 2015, p. 13]); Lessers and Taverner's arrive with the Cackling Geese from late September. None needs a version this round.

- The first Cackling record each autumn in the box fell on 5-26 September in 2018-2026, leaving out lone summer birds in August (iNat, appendix).
- **Recommendation:** **no Cackling model this round.** On a late-September morning a few small Cackling flocks are possible (a flyover of 10-30 at most); in late October they outnumber Canada Geese at the refuges. A Cackling round is warranted if the game adds an October-November or winter scene; it would need its own small, stubby-billed (culmen 26-35 mm [Aldrich 1946]), short-necked, dark-breasted (*minima*) model.

### 3.5 Recommendation

- **One version, `adult`, painted as a *moffitti*.** It also stands for the hatch-year birds: full grown, "duller and more blended" with a faintly speckled cheek (section 3.1), differences that do not read at 25-45 m (section 4.2).
- **Same size for young and adults.** If a builder wants goslings told apart, scale them at most 3% smaller and dull the head's black and the cheek's white slightly (Estimate, from the Dusky juveniles' 3.5% shorter wing [Bromley & Rothe 2003, Table 1]).

### 4.2 What reads at 25-45 m

- **By eye** (about 26-48 mm per pixel at 25-45 m; computed): a dark bird 13-25 px long; the black neck and the pale breast read; the cheek does not.
- **Binoculars** (3.0-5.5 mm per pixel): the bird is 115-210 px long. The **white chinstrap** (about 18 mm wide, 3-6 px) and the **white undertail** (60-70 mm deep on the floating bird, 12-20 px) read; the black-to-pale line at the neck base reads.
- **Telescope** (0.8-1.5 mm per pixel): 430-800 px. The pale feather edges of the back (a few mm) read as rows of crescents; a juvenile's notched tail tips (a few mm, under the wings) and its faint cheek speckling might just resolve on a close, sharp goose; the duller head would read only beside an adult. Not worth a version.

## 5. Structure

- **Bill:** wedge-shaped, deep at the base and tapering to a rounded nail; the culmen a little concave in profile (photo). Depth at the base 20-30 mm, width 19-30 [BBR 1884]; broader than deep at the nostrils (21.8 against 17.7, AVONET six large skins).
- **Head:** small for the bird; the eye centre about 1.5 culmens (85 mm) from the bill tip; the visible dark eye 10-12 mm across (0.17-0.21 culmens) (photo-measured), so **eye radius about 0.005 m**.
- **Neck:** long and thick, 34-66 mm across at mid-length depending on stretch and fluff (photo-measured); it bends smoothly (no heron-like kink): a gentle S when relaxed on land, nearly straight and vertical when alert, a strong S when walking (`cangoo-L8.jpg`).
- **Body:** long and deep, boat-shaped on the water; on land the "legs are placed nearer the middle of the body ... and the birds are better able to walk on land" than ducks [Kortright 1942, pp. 4-5].
- **Folded wing:** the primary tips lie over the stern, about level with the tail tip or just short of it (photo).
- **Tail:** black, short and rounded (photo; `tail.shape` `rounded` an Estimate); BBR count "18 to 20" feathers in the large geese [BBR 1884, p. 456]. On the water held well up: the tail tip 0.10-0.12 m above the water on the two highest-riding photos.
- **Feet:** webbed, black; three front toes fully webbed, a small raised hind toe [BBR 1884; photo]. "Their feet turn inward when they're walking" [WDFW Link].

## 7. Behaviour

### 7.1 Daily rhythm and time budget

- **Morning:** at Crab Orchard (Illinois, about 40,000 Interior Canada Geese, radio-tracked), "In October and November under clear or partly cloudy skies, the first geese to fly did so within 10 minutes after dawn civil twilight"; overcast "delayed the time of first flight by approximately 10-15 minutes"; "The geese spent an average of just over 2 hours in the fields" (morning field period 134 ± 8 min in 15-31 October) [Raveling et al. 1972, pp. 279-285]. "Geese spent the night on the water, but very often loafed on the bank during the day" [p. 292]. In Oregon, "The first occurs about sunrise and is usually a mass departure to feeding areas. They filter back to roost areas throughout mid-morning" [Marshall et al. 2003] (Snippet). These are field-feeding flocks that fly out; a resident family on a lake with grass beside it walks instead (7.6).
- **Time budget** (coastal New Jersey, October-February, 10-minute scans, 977 scans; % of birds) [Zoghby 2013, Table 1, p. 19]:

| Period | Feed | Rest | Comfort | Swim | Alert | Fly | Walk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Morning (crepuscular) | 29.8 | 28.0 | 8.6 | 26.6 | 2.2 | 4.6 | 0.2 |
| Day | 36.4 | 21.2 | 5.7 | 23.1 | 3.4 | 5.1 | 5.1 |

  A senior thesis; low weight, but the only autumn-winter Canada Goose budget by day part I could read. "Geese did not spend a majority of their feeding period actually feeding; much time was also spent in alertness, loafing" [Raveling et al. 1972, p. 291]. Western brood-rearing females on the Columbia fed about 45% of daylight, broods 54% [Eberhardt 1987, abstract].

### 7.2 Feeding on the water

- "after the manner of the surface-feeding ducks, reaching down to the bottom with their long necks and even tipping up with their feet in the air" [Bent 1925, p. 213]; "they swim gracefully about, plunging their heads and necks under the water to feed" (Knight 1908, in [Bent 1925, p. 216]); "tipping up with their tails and feet in the air and reaching down to the bottom of the shallows with their long necks" [Kortright 1942, p. 89]; they "feed on submerged aquatic vegetation by reaching under the water with their long necks" [WDFW Link].
- **No timed upend, reach depth, or split of feeding between water and land** was found for the species. In the local photos 8 birds were head-dipping (records 59, 60, 78, 79, 88, 89) and none tipping up; one tip-up photo (`U6`, Colorado) shows the body 75-80° from level.

### 7.3 Grazing

- **Bite rate**, captive Canada Geese of three size classes on ryegrass turf, filmed at 25 frames a second: "bite rate (bites s⁻¹) = 2.377 − 0.278 · ln(biomass, g m⁻²) − 0.074 · M (kg)" [Heuermann et al. 2011, Table 2, p. 664; the minus signs read from the PDF's glyphs, consistent with the text: "Bite rate decreased ... with grass biomass"]. For **M = 4.0 kg on 50-100 g m⁻²** (a short lawn): **0.80-0.99 bites a second** (computed). Cropping a bite takes about 0.07-0.13 s and swallowing about 0.6 s (Table 2, computed at 50-100 g m⁻²); "geese do not chew" [p. 661].
- For comparison: Cackling goslings pecked 57,900-62,800 times a day over 10.6-13.4 h of feeding [Sedinger & Raveling 1988] (abstract only), about 78-91 a minute (computed); wintering White-fronted and Bean Geese pecked 78-117 a minute [Mooij 1992, pp. 131-132]. Peck rate falls as the sward grows taller [Lang & Black 2001] (abstract only; Barnacle Geese); greylags "pecked most quickly in short vegetation (< 4 cm)" [Woog 2025, p. 9].
- **Head-down and head-up bouts:** "Foraging time consisted of cycles of alert postures followed by periods of grazing"; grazing bouts "x̄ = 10.67 sec vs. 8.42 sec" (females vs males), alert bouts 5.58 s and 8.04 s [Sedinger & Raveling 1990, p. 174] (Cackling Geese with broods, Alaska, summer). Males grazed 50.9-60.6% of foraging time, females 66.9-74.6% [Table 2, p. 177].
- **Walking while grazing:** no step rate or speed found; see section 11.
- **The local photos:** 63% of birds head-down in grazing groups (section 3.2).

### 7.4 Vigilance

- **Definition:** "Geese with their head high and neck outstretched were considered alert (Raveling 1970)" [Caithamer et al. 1996, p. 107].
- **By role, autumn** (Interior Canada Geese, Illinois and Wisconsin; % of daylight; feed / rest / alert) [Caithamer et al. 1996, Table 1, p. 109]:

| Social class | Union County | Horicon |
|---|---|---|
| Parent male (gander) | 25 / 21 / **35** | 22 / 25 / **30** |
| Paired male, no young | 33 / 27 / **20** | 32 / 35 / **12** |
| Parent female | 34 / 30 / **14** | 32 / 33 / **14** |
| Paired female, no young | 44 / 32 / **5** | 40 / 36 / **6** |

  "Parents spent about twice as much time alert compared with pairs" [p. 108]. Alert time rose with brood size: males 21, 27 and 36% with 1, 2 and 3 or more young; females 9, 12 and 14% [Table 3, p. 110]. On lakes, alert time did not differ between social classes [p. 110].
- **Goslings:** 1.6-2.9% alert, feeding 64-81% of the time; "Adult males spent the most time alert ... goslings were rarely alert"; "Amount of time spent alert increased with brood size" [Fowler & Ely 1997, Table 1, p. 408; p. 409] (Cackling Geese, summer). Pink-footed Geese: scanning is "mainly by the male"; "to compensate ... he pecked at a faster rate than his mate" [Lazarus & Inglis 1978] (abstract only).
- **Group size and edge:** "For individuals in non-breeding groups the time spent extreme head up declined as group size increased" [Lazarus & Inglis 1978] (abstract only). Brent Geese at the flock edge spend much more time head-up than those in the centre, and the edge effect largely explains why vigilance falls as flocks grow [Inglis & Lazarus 1981] (abstract only). Black & Owen 1989 and Black et al. 1992 (Barnacle Geese; parents and edge costs) could not be read.
- **No sentinel turn-taking.** "We detected no systematic coordination of alert periods by pairs; 17 of 33 pairs ... overlapped in alert behavior less than expected by chance, while the remainder overlapped more than expected" [Sedinger & Raveling 1990, p. 176]. The older picture, "The feeding flocks are guarded by one or more sentinels, which are ever on the alert until they are relieved by some of their companions and allowed to take their turns at feeding" [Bent 1925, p. 213], and "one of the birds, probably an old gander, kept constant watch" over a family of seven feeding "for over an hour" [Kortright 1942, p. 90], describe what an observer sees (usually one head up) rather than an organised rota. **So the game should give each bird its own graze/alert cycle by role, uncoordinated; a single head up most of the time then falls out of the numbers.**
- Urban Chicago geese spent "on average 31.0% of their time in alert behavior" [Askren et al. 2022] (Snippet). Winter scans on the water found only 2-3% alert [Zoghby 2013].

### 7.5 Family, group, order and spacing

- **Families stay together:** "Families remained intact all winter and reassembled if separation occurred" [Raveling 1969b] (abstract only); "Unless broken up by shooting, family groups ... are often maintained throughout the autumn and winter" [Hanson & Smith 1950, p. 155]. Resident geese in Alabama and Georgia: successful pairs "remained with their young-of-the-year throughout the nonbreeding season"; failed breeders formed "pseudofamilies"; **families averaged 4.7 birds and pseudofamilies 6.1**; marked birds were apart from their unit in only 2.0% of observations [Combs 1989, pp. 362, 365]. Young "remain with their family group for about one year" [WDFW Link].
- **Brood size:** clutches "from 4 to 10 eggs, usually 5 or 6"; incubation "28 to 30 days" [Bent 1925, p. 210]. Western Canada Geese on the Columbia River: 15 of 27 females (55.6%) fledged at least one gosling; gosling survival through rearing 0.491 [Eberhardt 1987, abstract]. Production "may average only 2.48 to 2.84 goslings per pair" [Hanson & Smith 1950]. Dusky goslings survive to 50 days at 20-40% [PFC 2015]. **A late-September family: the pair and 2-4 young (3 adopted).** I found no Pacific Northwest count of fall family size.
- **Late summer:** "By late summer all of the family can fly, and they move to areas where there is abundant food, joining with other geese to form large flocks" [WDFW Link]. Westerns "Generally fly in smaller flocks, usually numbering less than 40" [PNGM 2015, p. 8]. Non-breeders (yearlings, many two-year-olds, failed breeders) moult-migrate north in summer [Marshall et al. 2003] (Snippet); by September some have returned.
- **Rank:** fights are won "large family > smaller family > pair > single"; "Intrafamily aggression was rare" [Raveling 1970] (abstract only).
- **Order:** "As in walking or swimming movements during winter, any particular family member might be in the lead position at any one time ... When the gander turned and maintained a new heading, the rest of the family followed, but if an immature or even the adult female initiated a new direction ... the gander did not necessarily follow" [Raveling 1969a, p. 676]. With downy young in summer "the gander usually leads the procession, the goslings following, and the goose acting as rear guard"; chased, a family made "a long single file, one parent leading, the other bringing up the rear" (Taverner, in [Bent 1925, pp. 210-211]).
- **Families in a crowd:** families "feed first and rest in the center of the aggregation" (Jenkins 1944, in [Hanson & Smith 1950, pp. 127-128]); "families sat closer to other geese when the vigilance level of the parents was low" [Lazarus & Inglis 1978] (abstract only).
- **Spacing:** **no open source gives nearest-neighbour distances in metres** for Canada Geese on land or water; Raveling 1970 and Black et al. 1992 are paywalled. Section 11 gives estimates.

### 7.6 Leaving and entering the water

- "Geese often gain access to grazing areas by simply walking out onshore from the adjacent body of water on which they have landed" [WDFW Link]. "fairways and open water often serve as landing and take-off zones from which the birds walk or swim to the feed areas"; "Metro geese have been observed leading broods through 70 m of dense cattail and woods and more than 200 m of grass to graze"; "locations with good visibility ... are selected for grazing"; "abrupt shorelines (>0.5 m with >60° slope) thwart goose movement" [Cooper 1998, pp. 20-21]. "I have often observed geese at Horseshoe Lake Refuge walk more than a quarter of a mile when they could have covered this distance by flying" (A. S. Hawkins, in [Kortright 1942, p. 89]).
- "Geese prefer to rest or feed on grassy areas next to water"; in Connecticut "all urban sites with goose problems were characterized by a lawn next to a body of water" [Smith et al. 1999, pp. 7-8, 12]; "Canada geese prefer to feed, roost, and loaf near water where they can escape if threatened" [Gosser et al. 1997]. Western broods preferred "terrestrial habitats within 5 m of the shoreline" [Eberhardt 1987, abstract].
- Threatened on land near water, geese "immediately betake themselves in silence to the latter, swim to the middle of the pond" (Audubon, in [Bent 1925, p. 217]).
- **Not found:** how often a group switches between water and land, and a first-hand description of a goose stepping up a bank (shaking or preening after). The grassy flat must meet the water with a gentle bank (under 0.5 m high and under 60°) for the walk-out to be believable [Cooper 1998].

### 7.7 Gait and swimming

- **Walking** (Barnacle Geese, mean 1.79 kg, on a treadmill at 0.25-1.25 m/s, "their highest sustainable speed", no gait change): duty factor 0.77 − 0.19U; stride length 0.44 U^0.33 m; stride frequency 0.48 + 1.79U Hz [Nudds & Codd 2012, Fig. 2; Nudds et al. 2010] (exponent formatting reconstructed; at 1 m/s 0.44 m × 2.27 Hz ≈ 1.0 m/s, consistent). At **0.25 m/s** this gives a stride of 0.28 m (a **step of 0.14 m**) at 0.93 strides a second (**1.9 steps a second**); at **0.5 m/s** a stride of 0.35 m (step 0.175 m) at 1.37 Hz (2.7 steps a second) (computed). A *moffitti* has more than twice the mass and a longer leg, so its steps are likely somewhat longer at the same speed (Estimate).
- **Photo:** a walking goose with both feet down, 0.13 m between the tarsus bases and 0.16 m toe to toe (`E5`, photo-measured, low confidence on scale).
- **Waddle:** Anatidae walk with "the typical waddling motion" [Provini et al. 2012] (abstract only); geese less than ducks, their legs "nearer the middle of the body" [Kortright 1942, pp. 4-5]. On a rear three-quarter photo the body leans about **6° toward the planted leg** (`L7`, photo-measured, low confidence). No measured roll amplitude was found.
- **Swimming:** "They swim gracefully on the water after the manner of swans and can make rapid progress if necessary" [Bent 1925, p. 215]. **No swimming speed or paddle rate found.** Section 11 scales the Mallard's.

### 7.8 For later rounds: flight, arrivals and departures

Not for this round; collected so the next rounds start from sources.
- **Wingbeat:** "237 beats/min, or 4 wingbeats/sec (SD 0.2)", with no phase relationship between birds [Gould & Heppner 1974, p. 503] (migrating flocks, New York, October). The current block spec's `wingbeatHz: 2.6` is low. Only "48% of 73 birds" flew at a frequency within 0.1 beat/s of the bird ahead [Hainsworth 1989] (abstract only).
- **Speed:** equivalent airspeed 16.7 m/s from one radar track (mass 3.63 kg, span 1.69 m) [Alerstam et al. 2007, Protocol S1].
- **Formation:** vee angles 27.5-44.0°, **mean 34.2° ± 6.4°**; birds **4.1 m apart on average (SD 0.79; 2.5-12.8 m)**; of 104 flocks, column or echelon 39.4%, cluster 17.3%, vee 16.4%, J 15.4%, compound vee 5.8%; mean flock 27.2 birds; "Flocks rarely maintained a rigid structure" [Gould & Heppner 1974, pp. 501-503]. On radar, angles of different vees ranged 38-124°, a single vee's angle changed by 5-43° between sweeps, larger vees had smaller angles, and vees were "more often ... a 'J'" with a rounded apex; altitude estimated "40 and 80 m" [Williams et al. 1976, p. 556]. The best saving is at a wingtip overlap of 0.16 m [Badgerow 1988, p. 750]; the median spacing of 55 geese gave a 36% induced-power saving, low at the apex and at large depths [Hainsworth 1987] (abstract only).
- **Takeoff:** preflight head-tossing, at low intensity "slow vertical movements of the bill and head", higher "bill ... pointing up and the head ... shaken from side to side", with "low gutteral vocalizations"; "Geese often unfolded their wings and walked or ran a few feet" [Raveling 1969a, p. 672]. Head-tosses before flight: singles 4.5, pairs 33.7, families of 3-5 79-171, taking 5-24 min from the first toss [Table 1, p. 673]; ganders initiated 16 of 50 times; "Often the first goose of a family to take off was the individual that initiated head-tossing"; "At the moment of takeoff the low grunting vocalizations change to a loud honking" [pp. 674-676]. "they run along for a few steps before rising" [Bent 1925, p. 214].
- **Landing:** "the whole flock set their wings and drift gradually down a long incline until close to the surface, then scaling or flying along they drop into the water with a splash" [Bent 1925, p. 215]. **Whiffling:** no primary Canada Goose source found; inverted flight "causes the flight feathers to twist, creating gaps", used "during landing" [Sigrest & Inman 2022] (abstract only; an engineering study).
- **Morning departures:** first flights within 10 minutes after dawn civil twilight in October-November, the heavy flight 10-30 minutes later and lasting 20-40 minutes [Raveling et al. 1972, pp. 279-284].

## 12. Open questions and confidence

**High confidence**
- The *moffitti* culmen, wing, tail, tarsus and toe [Aldrich 1946], and that the valley's late-September Canada Geese are residents with Duskies arriving mostly from late October [PFC 2015; Bromley & Rothe 2003].
- Vigilance by role (gander > goose > pairs > goslings) [Caithamer et al. 1996; Fowler & Ely 1997], the bout means [Sedinger & Raveling 1990], and the absence of coordinated sentinels [Sedinger & Raveling 1990, p. 176].
- Families staying together through autumn [Combs 1989; Hanson & Smith 1950; Raveling 1969b].
- The Cackling Goose's late-September scarcity and October peak (iNat, eBird, [PFC 2016; Mlodinow et al. 2008]).

**Medium confidence**
- **Floating heights:** three side-on photos, mostly non-Northwest birds; one (`c1`) rides much lower. Bounds allow ±15%.
- **Mass 4.0 kg:** inside the sourced range but not a *moffitti* sample mean (Hanson 1965, Palmer 1976 and Bellrose 1976 have the tables; not read).
- **Bite rate:** a captive study's regression evaluated at an assumed sward; the minus signs were read from glyphs.
- **Vigilance bouts** come from Cackling Geese with small goslings in Alaska in summer; autumn Western families may differ.

**Low confidence or estimates**
- **Draft:** no beam measurement (no top view or skin with a scale found); two methods agree on 55-76 mm.
- **Alert neck height:** two head-turned photos scaled by waterline length (0.23-0.43 m).
- **Grazing pose:** one photo with a doubtful scale; ratios to the back height used.
- **Walking:** gait equations from the Barnacle Goose, half the mass; no measured waddle amplitude; no walking speed while grazing.
- **Upending:** no timed goose upend, no reach depth; durations and reach scaled from the Mallard.
- **Swimming speed and paddle rate:** computed from the Mallard by hull length.
- **Spacing:** no measured nearest-neighbour distances on land or water.
- **Water-land switching:** how often and how long; the 20-60 min grazing period is an estimate.
- **Hatch-year proportion** in late-September groups: no local count; the iNat photos could age none.

**Sources I could not read** (none relied on for a number unless stated): Mowbray et al., *Birds of the World* (paywalled); Palmer 1976; Bellrose 1976 (snippets only); Delacour 1954 vol. 1; Pyle 2008; Hanson 1965 (snippet only); Black & Owen 1989; Black et al. 1992; Lazarus 1978; Raveling 1970 and 1969b (abstracts); Lazarus & Inglis 1978 and Inglis & Lazarus 1981 (abstracts); Hainsworth 1987 and 1989 (abstracts); Conover & Kania 1991; Owen 1972; Marshall et al. 2003 and Gilligan et al. 1994 (full-text-search snippets).

**Worth doing if higher confidence is needed**
- A side-on photo of a floating *moffitti* in the valley with a sharp culmen, and one of an alert floating goose side-on.
- A top view (a flying bird from above, or a skin beside a rule) for the beam and the bill's width profile.
- Time tip-ups and head-dips on a few CC video clips of Canada Geese.
