# Northern Shoveler: measurements, the spatulate bill, late-September plumage, floating posture, and skim-feeding

Date: 2026-09-25
Purpose: source data for the detailed Northern Shoveler (`norsho`) model, its swimming posture, its bill width profile, and the `shoveler` foraging style on `swim`. The cast is three shovelers on `swim` in the `open-water` zone (polygon [[-22,-15],[26,-15],[32,-52],[-28,-52]], about 15-60 m from the vantage), `"plumage": "mixed"`, on Willow Lake, Willamette Valley, Oregon, on a late-September morning.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt of a paywalled page; I could not read the page, so treat it as unverified. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo and scaled it by the published culmen (or, where stated, by another length). "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements, mean of the sexes** (mm; section 2.1): wing chord **230**, tail **78**, exposed culmen **64** (F 62, M 65), tarsus **36**, middle toe **43** (claw not stated), mass **0.61 kg** [Phillips 1925, p. 12; Johnsgard 2010, pp. 289-290; Baird et al. 1884, p. 527; Tobias et al. 2022; Kortright 1953, p. 384]. Males are only 3-6% larger in wing and culmen; one size serves both variants.
- **The bill** (section 2.4):
  - Width **16.5 mm at the feather line**, **19.5 at the nostril** (AVONET 18.6), widening steadily to **29.5 mm at 0.75 of the culmen** (range 0.65-0.80; 28-32 mm), then closing in a broad, rounded tip that still has 70% of the widest width 4.5 mm behind its end. Widest / base = **1.79** ("about twice" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 525]).
  - The only absolute outline is a ruler-scaled skull (29.6 mm widest); the shape comes from four outlines (two fresh-bird photos, the skull, Kortright's drawing).
  - Depth **21 mm at the base, 19.3 at the nostril (AVONET 18.7), about 15 through the middle**, 7 at 0.9 and 5 at 0.95: deeper than wide at the base, 2.5 times as wide as deep across the spoon. Culmen straight (dips 0.7 mm mid-way); `curve` 0. Nail 6.5 × 5.5 mm, hooked about 1 mm down.
  - Lamellae (about 110 [AAB-O]) are invisible at game range and largely hidden behind the hanging margins [Johnsgard 2010, p. 290]: do not model them.
  - Bill colour: eclipse male blackish to dark slate with an orange-brown cast; female olive-grey culmen spotted black, orange sides and lower mandible [Kortright 1953, pp. 215-216].
- **Late-September plumage** (section 3): **every sexable male in the Willamette photos from 11-30 September is in eclipse (25 of 25)**; the first transitional males appear on 6 October. Males are about half the sexable birds (57% in September, 49% in early October). Eclipse drakes have a **yellow iris**, a dark bill, a grey to sooty head and **rufous, scalloped flanks** [Kortright 1953, p. 215; Phillips 1925, p. 12], and (photo) a white patch on the upper rear flank. Females and juveniles have a **dark brown iris** [Leukering 2012; photos] and an orange-sided bill.
- **Recommended variants: two**, `male` = **eclipse male** and `female` = **female-type** (stands for juveniles too). **Flock mix: 50/50**; for three birds, two of one and one of the other. The green-headed drake is not a late-September bird.
- **Floating posture** (section 6, three photos): waterline length 0.23 m; back top **45 mm** above the water (38-57), neck-base mantle 28, crown **85** (81-94), tail tip 30 (26-47). The back line **rises 8° toward the tail** (the "front-heavy" look). **Draft ≈ 45% of the body depth** (buoyancy 0.42, photos 0.53; body depth about 82-95 mm, so 37-43 mm under water). Rest-pose length bill tip to tail tip **0.32 m** (0.29-0.35).
- **Feeding** (section 7): skimming with the head half submerged and the bill swept side to side [Bent 1923, p. 140; AAB-ID]; head kept down for long spells [Millais 1902, p. 60]; tip-ups rare and brief [Phillips 1925, p. 20]; pairs and groups of 3-4 circle nose to tail "for a minute or two" [Millais 1902, p. 60; Kortright 1953, pp. 218-219]. Daytime budget of wintering drakes: feeding 24%, swimming 28%, resting 22%, preening 17% [Webb & Brotherson 1988, p. 283]. On CC video (Estimates, section 7.3): a **travelling skim at 0.32 m/s** with the head down for up to a minute, a **pottering skim at 0.03 m/s** with 1.75 s dips; the bill 25-30° to the surface; **no detectable side-to-side sweep**; pairs spin at **3.4 s per revolution** (3.0-4.75), direction constant within a bout, the two birds' centres 0.25 m apart, bouts of 4-8 s (up to a minute or two); **no tip-ups** in about 70 bird-minutes; cruising 0.45 m/s with the bill 27-35° below level; feeding birds 0.2-0.35 m apart, one skimming in the other's wake. Settings in section 11.
- **Tracing references** (section 8): `norsho-female-side-2.jpg` (Aldrich, CC BY-NC, Portland, 17 Sept 2024, 0.298 mm/px) for the profile and bill; `norsho-eclipse-side.jpg` (Foss, CC BY-NC, Lane County, 16 Sept 2026, 0.238 mm/px) for the eclipse male; `norsho-bill-skull.jpg` (Rodríguez-Casanova, CC BY-NC, ruler 12.82 px/mm) and `norsho-bill-top.jpg` (Lord Mountbatten/Lamiot, CC BY 3.0) for the width profile. The harvested `norsho.jpg` is an alternate drake in flight: not a tracing reference.

### 2.2 Head length and the bill against the head

No source I could read gives a total-head length for this species.
- "Bill longer than the head" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 525]; "much longer than head" [Kortright 1953, p. 215]; "distinctly longer than the head" [Johnsgard 2010, p. 290].
- Photo-measured culmen / feathered head (culmen base to the rearmost point of the head outline): **1.0** on `norsho-female-side-2.jpg` (relaxed, nape feathers fluffed), **1.24** on `norsho-female-side.jpg` (alert), **1.56** on `norsho-eclipse-side.jpg`. As with the shorebirds, the feathered outline is loose and posture-dependent.
- Model head (skull-level occiput to culmen base): **45 mm** (Estimate). Basis: the skull is "not very different from that of many other ducks and resembles the Teal's" (Shufeldt 1888, in [Phillips 1925, p. 19]); a 45 mm head gives culmen / head ≈ 1.4, inside the photo range and consistent with "distinctly longer than the head".

### 2.3 The bill: shape statements

- Genus *Spatula*: "Bill longer than the head, much expanded, or almost spatulate, terminally, where about twice as wide as at the compressed base; maxillary lamellae very thin, lengthened, almost completely exposed posteriorly, where resembling the teeth of a fine comb" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 525].
- For *S. clypeata* (against *S. platalea*): "Culmen nearly straight, slightly depressed in the middle; feathering at base of maxilla, on each side extending forward as far as that on the forehead" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 525].
- Ridgway's key to the Anatinae: "Hind-toe without a distinct membraneous lobe ... Bill spatulate (very broad near end and narrow at base) ... Spatula" [Ridgway 1887, p. 85].
- "bill ... black, lower mandible paler, spoon-shaped, much longer than head; width near tip about twice as wide as at base, average length 2¾", width at base 5/8" widening to about 1¼" near tip; lamellae conspicuously developed, long and bristle-like" [Kortright 1953, p. 215]; the same figures on p. 218 with a top and side drawing (Fig. 43).
- In the hand: "The strongly spatulate bill, which has soft lateral margins near the tip that hang over the sides and obscure the long lamellae, is unique to the shoveler among North American species of waterfowl" [Johnsgard 2010, p. 290].
- "Its width near the tip, about twice as wide as at the base, has several rows of comblike lamellae" [Webb & Brotherson 1988, p. 283].
- Lamellae: "about 110 fine projections (called lamellae) along the edges that act like a colander" [AAB-O]. Shovelers' maxillary and mandibular interlamellar distances are the same (Kooloos et al. 1989, in [Gurd 2007, p. 337]). Lamellar density "21.48 ± 2.41" (units as quoted, "lamellae/cm") [Nudds & Bowlby 1984] (Snippet).
- Downy young: "the young shoveller's bill is decidedly longer and more spatulate than that of the young mallard, and it grows amazingly fast, so that when two weeks old there is no difficulty in identifying the species" [Bent 1923, p. 138]; at two months "the bill has not nearly reached its full growth" [Millais 1902, p. 69].

### 2.4 The bill width and depth profile (photo-measured)

**Sources of the outline.** No source gives widths along the bill except the base and the tip. I measured four outlines:
- **Top view, fresh bird:** `norsho-bill-top.jpg` (Lamiot's cut-out of Lord Mountbatten's photo, CC BY 3.0) and its original `norsho-bill-top-2.jpg` (CC BY-SA 3.0): a dead bird from the Moscow Region, bill seen from above. The two are the same specimen. **Both are foreshortened along the bill** (the Mallard's bill beside it comes out at width/length about 0.6, against about 0.36 from AVONET's Mallard width 20.8 mm and bill 58.5 mm; computed), so I used only **relative** widths from them, not their length.
- **Top view, dried skull with a ruler:** `norsho-bill-skull.jpg` (Araceli Rodríguez-Casanova, iNaturalist 164545447, CC BY-NC): a skull with its dried bill sheath lying dorsal side up beside a centimetre rule. The rule gives **12.82 px/mm** (52 mm of ticks, computed); this is the only outline with an absolute scale. The dorsal sheath has flaked off the base, so the base width is not usable.
- **Drawing:** Kortright's Fig. 43, top and side views [Kortright 1953, p. 218]; digitized from the page scan.
- Method: the outline's upper edge was found per column from the luminance step against the plain background (fresh bird), or read off a tick-bordered enlargement (skull, drawing). The fresh bird is symmetric about the line through its two nostrils (y ≈ 601 px), so I doubled the upper half-width. Positions are fractions `s` of the culmen from its base (the forehead feathering on the midline, `s = 0`) to the tip (`s = 1`).

It then thins quickly over the last 30%.
- Cross-section: at the base the bill is **deeper than wide** (21 × 16.5 mm, "the compressed base"); at the widest point it is **about 2.5 times as wide as deep** (29.5 × 11.5 mm) (computed from the two profiles).

**Culmen line and nail.**
- "Culmen nearly straight, slightly depressed in the middle" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 525]. On `norsho-female-side-2.jpg` the culmen at mid-length lies about 0.7 mm below the base-to-tip chord (photo-measured): a straight bill with the faintest dip. **`bill.curve` ≈ 0** (0 to +0.01).
- The **nail** is a rounded, slightly hooked cap at the tip: **6-7 mm long, 5-6 mm wide** (photo-measured: 6.1 × 5.1 mm on the ruler-scaled skull; 0.09 of the culmen long and 0.20 of the widest width on the fresh bird). In side view it hooks down about 1 mm below the lower outline (photo, both females). It is pinkish-horn on the fresh bird and dark on the skull; on live females it is dark, like the culmen (photo).
- **Bill carriage at rest:** the culmen chord points 29-33° below level on relaxed and swimming birds (`norsho-female-side-2.jpg` 29.4°, `norsho-eclipse-side.jpg` 33°) and 43° on an alert bird with the head raised (`norsho-female-side.jpg`) (photo-measured). "The male sits low on the water with bill pointing in a downward slope" [Kortright 1953, p. 217].

**Lamellae.**
- "about 110 fine projections (called lamellae) along the edges" [AAB-O]; "rows of greatly enlarged bristles (lamellae)" [Phillips 1925, p. 19]; "maxillary lamellae very thin, lengthened, almost completely exposed posteriorly, where resembling the teeth of a fine comb" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 525]; the mandibular and maxillary interlamellar distances are the same (Kooloos et al. 1989, in [Gurd 2007, p. 337]).
- On `norsho-female-side-2.jpg` (0.3 mm/px) the comb shows as a fine pale fringe along the rear half of the gape, about 25 mm long (photo). With about 110 lamellae along a 60 mm edge the spacing is about 0.5 mm (computed; Estimate: "about 110" is not said to be per side).
- **Not visible at game range.** Through the telescope (2°) at 15 m one pixel of a 1920-px view is about 0.27 mm, at 30 m 0.55 mm; the comb is also mostly hidden behind the hanging margins [Johnsgard 2010, p. 290] (computed). Model no lamellae; at most a thin darker line along the rear of the tomium.

**What the bill looks like at game distance** (computed, 1920-px view): through the telescope at 30 m the 64 mm bill is about 115 px long and the spoon about 55 px wide; through binoculars (7.5°) at 30 m, about 30 px long and 15 px wide. The spoon's outline is readable through the telescope in any view but end-on.

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say about moult timing

- **Adult male eclipse and the slow return.**
  - "The midsummer eclipse plumage of the male is quite complete, and closely resembles the female plumage, except for the wings, which, of course, are molted only once in August, and for the breast and belly, which remains largely brown. The molt into the eclipse begins about the 1st of July and the change is very rapid. The molt out of the eclipse in the fall is more protracted; it sometimes does not begin until the middle of October and is not complete until December or later. Adult males can always be recognized by the wings" [Bent 1923, p. 140].
  - "The moult out of the eclipse plumage, in which the male closely resembles the female, is very protracted, and in early autumn most males are still found in this eclipse plumage. The wing is as in winter plumage. The autumn moult usually commences about the middle of October, when the bill still retains the yellowish-brown colour of the eclipse (black in winter). In early November the head begins to show some of the green feathering of winter plumage; some white feathers appear on scapulars and chest, and some chestnut feathers on sides, breast, and belly. Further progress towards full winter plumage is slow, and varies greatly in different birds; some birds regain full winter dress in December but the majority not until January" [Kortright 1953, pp. 215-216].
  - Eclipse: "In this plumage the male closely resembles the female, except for the breast, which remains largely chestnut, but is paler than in the winter male ... the wings do not change the male pattern. The bill during eclipse becomes yellowish brown" [Kortright 1953, p. 215].
  - "This plumage is assumed rather late in the summer and carried into the winter as with the Blue-winged Teal. The general appearance is very much like that of the female. The occiput is darker than in the adult female and the mantle is more uniform, without the broad light edgings to the feathers. Although as a general rule the old males do not attain winter plumage until later than most ducks, one does see some in nearly perfect feather by the first week in December" [Phillips 1925, p. 12].
  - "The autumn change of the adult male into winter dress is similar to that of other surface-feeding ducks, but the process, as in the case of the Garganey, is often protracted, and the bird not in complete plumage until December" [Millais 1902, p. 69]. His Plate figure 9 is an "Adult male. Full eclipse. September 1st."
  - "Males do not acquire their striking nuptial plumage until rather late in the winter, so that during fall most shovelers are femalelike in appearance, with the enlarged bill and bluish upper wing coverts being the primary field marks" [Johnsgard 2010, p. 290].
  - Specimens: the adults' autumn body moult (which brings the males' bright plumage) ran "7 October-4 February in *A. discors*, *A. cyanoptera*, and *A. clypeata* (N = 60; later than in other *Anas*)", against "26 August to 6 November in most *Anas*" [Pyle 2005, p. 214]. "Rectrices, and ornamental tertials and scapulars in *A. discors*, *A. cyanoptera*, and *A. clypeata*, were the last feathers to develop during this molt, as late as January or later on the winter grounds" [Pyle 2005, p. 215].
  - The "supplemental" plumage: Pyle discusses the extra autumn molt of Anatinae in general terms (the putative first prebasic "could be considered a presupplemental molt" [Pyle 2005, p. 218]); I found no primary text in reach that dates a shoveler-specific supplemental plumage. What birders call the transitional male (dark head with a pale facial crescent, white coming into the breast) is Kortright's "early November" stage; the photos below show it from 6 October. AAB caption: "When molting into breeding plumage, males can still have some coarse brown markings on the chest and flanks" [AAB-ID gallery].
- **Juveniles.**
  - "In this first plumage the sexes are alike, but the male is slightly larger" [Bent 1923, p. 139].
  - "By the middle of September we see the molt beginning, and from this date till the following February there is no surface-feeding duck whose plumage change progresses so slowly. In its ordinary course there is little difference between September and January" (Millais 1902, in [Bent 1923, p. 139]; [Millais 1902, p. 67]).
  - "JUVENILE. During first autumn and until January sexes are alike and resemble adult female except upperparts are darker and more uniform; head and neck are darker; underparts of young male are richer; young males are somewhat brighter than young females and their breasts are usually tinged with chestnut ... Bills of both sexes are pinkish horn" [Kortright 1953, p. 216].
  - "Young female in first plumage: Closely resembles adult female but can be distinguished by the more uniform appearance of mantle and scapular region (lack of light edgings to the feathers). The lower surface is darker and more streaked and spotted with gray ... Young male in first plumage: Similar to the female at corresponding age except for the bluer wing-coverts and brighter green on the speculum. Sometimes a few feathers bearing broad subterminal blackish bands appear on the breast in September ... During October young males with ruddy coloring and irregular cross-barring on the abdomen begin to appear" [Phillips 1925, p. 12].
  - Juveniles' first autumn body moult (first prealternate): "16 October to 18 January, in *A. discors*, *A. cyanoptera*, and *A. clypeata* (N = 26)", against "2 September to 16 November in most (eight) species" [Pyle 2005, p. 212].
- **Iris.**
  - Adult male: "iris bright yellow" [Ridgway 1887, p. 96; Baird et al. 1884, p. 527]; "Iris golden to reddish orange" [Phillips 1925, p. 12]; "eyes bright yellow" [Millais 1902, p. 67]; eclipse males keep it (AAB: nonbreeding males are "still easily identifiable by bill shape, yellow eye, and striking wing pattern" [AAB-ID gallery]).
  - Female: the older sources say yellow ("iris and feet as in the male" [Ridgway 1887, p. 96]; "Iris yellow" [Phillips 1925, p. 12]; "eye, yellow to straw colour" [Kortright 1953, p. 216]). A modern bander-birder writes: "any Northern Shoveler with clear and bright yellow eyes is an adult male, at least in the period July through January"; "Eye color is variable in females, ranging from dark brown or dark brownish-olive in juveniles to nearly amber in a small percentage of adults"; "some young males have adult eye color as early as February in their first year of life" [Leukering 2012]. The Willamette photos agree with Leukering: every bird with an orange-sided bill has a dark brown iris (appendix). **Paint the female iris dark brown.**
  - Bill as a sexing mark: "any Northern Shoveler with a bill that is extensively black is a male, regardless of season"; immature males' bills "transition from the dull 'slaty-orange' ... olive-tinged orange, or orange-tinged olive ... to the black typical of older males" [Leukering 2012].
- **Arrival.** "The shoveller is one of the earliest migrants in the fall; the first autumnal frosts, late in August or early in September, are enough to start it drifting along with the blue-winged teal; the migration is well under way by the middle of September, and a month later it is practically over" [Bent 1923, p. 141] (for the breeding range). In Oregon, iNaturalist research-grade records in the Willamette box rise from 19 in August to 85 in September and 160 in October (below).

The bill runs from blackish to dark grey with a brownish or orange-brown cast ("yellowish brown", [Kortright 1953, p. 215]; 242791332, 402522476). All show the yellow iris.

### 3.3 Recommendation

- **Model two variants, not three**:
  - `male`: the **eclipse male**. In late September this is the only male plumage in the valley (25 of 25). It is what makes the game honest: the drake a player expects (green head, white breast, chestnut flanks) is not there yet.
  - `female`: the **female-type**, which also stands for juveniles of both sexes.
  - A third, the **transitional male** (white feathers coming into the breast, a blackish head with a green gloss, sometimes a pale facial crescent), appears from 6 October. It is not needed for 20-30 September; add it only if the game's date range moves into October.
  - The full alternate drake is the catalog photo (`public/media/photos/norsho.jpg`) but not a late-September bird. The guide text should say so (section 4.4).
- **Flock mix** for three birds: males are 49-57% of sexable birds. With uniform picking between two variants the expected mix is right. If the spawner allows weights, use **male 0.5, female 0.5**; if it can force a mix, use **two of one and one of the other** (either way), so a player always sees both.

## 4. Field marks of the recommended plumages

These are colour words from the sources and my observations on the photos ("(photo)"); I have not invented hex values.

### 4.1 Eclipse male (late September)

- **Head and neck:** plain grey-brown to dark sooty grey, finely speckled, darkest on the crown; no green in late September (photo, all 25 September males). "The occiput is darker than in the adult female" [Phillips 1925, p. 12]. Some birds show a faintly paler face at the bill base (photo).
- **Iris:** clear yellow to orange-yellow; the most visible mark at 30-50 m (photo). "Iris golden to reddish orange" [Phillips 1925, p. 12].
- **Bill:** blackish or dark slate, often with a brownish or dull orange-brown cast toward the edges; "The bill during eclipse becomes yellowish brown" [Kortright 1953, p. 215]. Darker overall than any female's (photo).
- **Breast:** buff to rufous-brown with bold dark U-shaped scallops; "the breast and belly, which remains largely brown" [Bent 1923, p. 140]; "remains largely chestnut, but is paler than in the winter male" [Kortright 1953, p. 215].
- **Flanks:** rufous-chestnut, strongest on the side of the body below the folded wing, with dark scallops and bars (photo). This rufous flank is the eclipse male's second field mark after the eye.
- **Back and scapulars:** dark brown, "the mantle is more uniform, without the broad light edgings" [Phillips 1925, p. 12]; the long scapulars dark with pale shaft streaks (photo).
- **Folded wing at rest:** the powder-blue forewing usually hidden under the flank feathers; the green speculum and the blue sometimes show as a narrow strip above the flank (photo, 135639977, 247166647). Wing pattern unchanged in eclipse [Bent 1923, p. 140; Kortright 1953, p. 215].
- **Tertials:** long, pointed, blackish with a green gloss and white central stripes ("tertials, greenish black, streaked with white" [Kortright 1953, p. 215]; "tertials dusky black, with faint green reflections, and marked toward the end with an indistinct mesial stripe of grayish white" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 526]) (photo: bold white-striped tertials lying over the rear flank).
- **Rear flank and stern:** a **white or buffy-white patch on the upper rear flank**, in front of the black-edged undertail and below the tertials, on most eclipse males whose rear is visible, from mid-September (photo: 402522476 on 16 September, 243877198, 137574786, 96837128, 138208907, 319473865). It corresponds to the alternate male's "white patch at the base of the tail, on each side" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 525]. It is absent on females, whose rear flank is buff and scalloped like the rest of the body (photo). Paint it on the eclipse male at about 70% of the alternate male's brightness; it is the eclipse male's third mark after the eye and the rufous flank.
- **Tail:** "Central feathers dusky brown, with pale edgings, outer feathers becoming whiter" [Kortright 1953, p. 215]; whitish at the sides (photo).
- **Feet:** orange to orange-red ("Legs and feet bright orange-red" [Phillips 1925, p. 12]); hidden while floating.

### 4.2 Female and juvenile

- **Head and neck:** "greyish buff, streaked and spotted with dusky brown; crown and streak through eye, darkest; throat, and area at base of bill, palest and least streaked" [Kortright 1953, p. 216]; a plain, rather pale head with a dusky eye-line (photo).
- **Iris:** dark brown (photo; [Leukering 2012]).
- **Bill:** "olive grey or olive brown, shading to greenish at tip, finely spotted with black, edges and lower mandible orange, same shape as in male" [Kortright 1953, p. 216]; "Bill brown, mandible orange" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 527]; "Bill greenish brown with the margins of the culmen yellower. Lower mandible dull orange" [Phillips 1925, p. 12]. On the photos the whole side of the bill reads bright orange from 30 m, with a darker olive-grey culmen ridge, sometimes spotted (photo). AAB: "Their very large orange bill is their most notable field mark" [AAB-ID].
- **Body:** buff-brown with bold dark U-shaped centres and pale fringes on the breast, flanks and scapulars; "chest, greyish buff, spotted with brownish ashy; sides, brownish ashy, feathers broadly bordered with greyish buff" [Kortright 1953, p. 216]. Juveniles are darker and plainer above, "lack of light edgings", and more spotted below [Phillips 1925, p. 12].
- **Wing:** "Lesser and middle coverts, greyish blue" (duller than the male's), speculum "less metallic green" [Kortright 1953, p. 216]; at rest usually hidden.
- **Tertials:** "brownish, with olive wash and margined with ashy buff" [Kortright 1953, p. 216].
- **Tail:** "grey brown, becoming whiter" [Kortright 1953, p. 216]; white-edged outer feathers visible at rest (photo).
- **Feet:** orange.

### 4.3 Telling a female shoveler from other female dabblers at 30-50 m

- The bill: "distinctly longer than the head and destroying the otherwise fairly sleek lines of the duck" [Johnsgard 2010, p. 290]; "the long slender necks and conspicuously large bills" [Bent 1923, p. 141]; "The bill readily identifies the female Shoveller" [Kortright 1953, p. 217].
- The posture: "The female is plain brownish buffy, but has the same flat rakish head and large bill as the male; even at some distance, this will distinguish it from other mottled-brown ducks" [Kortright 1953, p. 217]; "It is a medium-sized duck that tends to sit with its rear a bit higher out of the water almost like its bill is pulling its front half down" [AAB-ID]; "The male sits low on the water with bill pointing in a downward slope" [Kortright 1953, p. 217]; "on the water the Shoveller keeps the head well drawn in, with the bill often resting on the surface" [Phillips 1925, p. 20].
- The behaviour: head down, skimming for long spells, rarely upending (section 7).
- So at 30-50 m the female is told by (1) the long, orange-sided spoon held angled down toward the water, (2) the front-low, stern-high carriage with the head drawn in on a short neck, and (3) head-down skimming. A female Mallard or Gadwall has a shorter, flatter bill held level, a longer neck carried up, and sits level or stern-low.

### 4.4 Correction to today's `norsho.ts`

The block model's `male` variant is the alternate drake (green head, white breast, rufous flank). The detailed `male` variant should be the eclipse male, and the `marks` text should say something like "in late September the drake is still in eclipse: a female-like brown duck with a dark head, a yellow eye, a blackish bill and rufous flanks". Its `eye: '#e8c22a'` suits the eclipse male; the female's iris should be dark brown, not `#2a2a2a` black.

## 5. Structure

- **Head:** "flat rakish head" [Kortright 1953, p. 217]; "sloping forehead" (male) [Kortright 1953, p. 217]; "The thick head, short neck and huge bill of both sexes are easily noted in flight" [Kortright 1953, p. 217]. On the photos the forehead runs almost straight up from the culmen base into a rounded crown, highest above or just behind the eye, and the nape is full; the head looks large and square behind a bill that continues the line of the forehead (photo). Visible eye 5.4-6.0 mm across (photo-measured on `norsho-female-side-2.jpg` and `norsho-eclipse-side.jpg`): **`eye.radius` ≈ 2.9 mm**, set high on the head, level with or just above the culmen base.
- **Bill:** section 2.4. Culmen about 1.4 times the head (section 2.2), carried about 30° below level at rest.
- **Neck:** "The neck is rather short, and on the water the Shoveller keeps the head well drawn in, with the bill often resting on the surface" [Phillips 1925, p. 20]; yet in flight "the long slender necks and conspicuously large bills" [Bent 1923, p. 141]. At rest the neck is drawn down into the breast and looks thick; alert birds raise it (the crown then stands 83-94 mm above the water against about 85 at rest; section 6).
- **Body on the water:** "a medium-sized duck that tends to sit with its rear a bit higher out of the water almost like its bill is pulling its front half down" [AAB-ID]; "The male sits low on the water with bill pointing in a downward slope" [Kortright 1953, p. 217]. Photo-measured: the back line **rises toward the rear by 8-9°** (neck-base mantle to the top of the tertials: 8.7° on `norsho-female-side.jpg`, 7.8° on `norsho-eclipse-side.jpg`), and the mantle at the base of the neck stands only 18-39 mm above the water against 38-57 mm at the tertials (section 6). The breast is full and meets the water under the neck; the stern is higher, with the tail held clear of the water.
- **Tail:** short, pointed feathers: "Tail short, the feathers acute" [Baird et al. 1884, p. 525]; "while the middle rectrices in a female of *S. clypeata* measure about 3.75 inches" (95 mm; against *S. platalea*'s longer, more cuneate tail) [Baird et al. 1884, p. 526]. AVONET tail 75.1 mm (M 79, F 71) [Tobias et al. 2022]. **Shape `wedge`** (pointed central feathers, graduated), or `rounded`. Carried just above the water, cocked up 10-20° on some birds (photo: the tail tip stands 26-29 mm above the water on the two relaxed females and 47 mm on the male, section 6).
- **Folded wing on the water:** the flank feathers cover the lower half of the folded wing; above them a band **about 15-25 mm deep** of scapulars and long tertials lies along the back (photo-measured on `norsho-female-side-2.jpg`), with the dark primary tips crossing over the rump. The **tail tip reaches 22-39 mm past the wingtip** (photo-measured: 22 mm on `norsho-female-side-2.jpg`, 23 on `norsho-female-side.jpg`, 39 on `norsho-eclipse-side.jpg`). The blue forewing and green speculum are mostly hidden at rest; "Their powdery-blue shoulder patch is sometimes visible at rest" [AAB-ID gallery]. The long tertials are "acute" and in the male "greenish black, streaked with white" [Kortright 1953, p. 215]; the juvenile male's are "dull, olive brown, and rounded, not pointed as in adult" [Kortright 1953, p. 216].
- **Feet:** webbed (palmate), three front toes joined by webs; "hind toe without lobe" [Kortright 1953, p. 216]; "their small feet being ill adapted for making headway in rough water" [Millais 1902, p. 59]. Tarsus 35.6-38.1 mm and middle toe 41.9-44.5 mm (claw not stated) [Baird et al. 1884, p. 527]; tarsus 35-40 (M) and 37 (F) [Phillips 1925, p. 12]; AVONET tarsus 33.8 [Tobias et al. 2022]. Colour: "Legs and feet bright orange-red" (male) and "nearly as in the male" (female) [Phillips 1925, p. 12]; "feet, orange red to Vermillion" (male), "feet, orange" (female) [Kortright 1953, pp. 215-216]; orange on every photographed bird (photo). Hidden while floating; shown when upending and on birds standing on logs (`norsho-standing.jpg`).
- **Size against the neighbours** (for the viewer): "Larger than an American Coot, smaller than a Mallard" [AAB-ID]; Mallard wing 263 and mass 843 g against 226 and 613 [Tobias et al. 2022, species means].

## 6. Floating posture

**Method.** On three side-on photos of swimming birds I read the outline off tick-bordered enlargements and scaled each by its culmen chord (62 mm for the females, 65 mm for the male; sections 2.1, 8). All three were taken from slightly above: the near-side flank meets the water lower in the image than the breast and stern do. The breast-front and stern contact points lie in the bird's midline plane, so I measured heights from the straight line through them (the midline waterline). The offset of the near-flank waterline (12-22 mm) against a body half-width of about 65-80 mm puts the camera 10-13° above level (computed); vertical heights shrink by cos(13°) = 0.97 at most, so I did not correct them.

**Draft (below the waterline) cannot be seen**, so it comes from the body depth and from buoyancy:
- **Body depth**, back to belly: on the standing female in `norsho-standing.jpg` the body is 0.42 as deep as it is long from the front of the breast to the undertail (photo-measured). Applied to the 0.23 m waterline length, that is about **95 mm** (Estimate).
- **Buoyancy:** a 0.61 kg bird (section 2.1) displaces 610 cm³. Taking the body as an ellipsoid 240 × 130 × 95 mm (the width is an Estimate), the submerged fraction of its volume is 0.39, which for an ellipsoid means the water reaches **0.42 of the body depth**, a draft of about **40 mm** (computed). With a width of 150 mm the draft is 0.39 of the depth (37 mm).
- **Photo check:** 95 mm of body with 45 mm above the water at the back leaves 50 mm below, 0.53 of the depth. The two estimates bracket **0.42-0.53**.
- **Recommendation:** put the midline waterline so that **about 45% of the body depth is below it**; the block model's `draft: 0.45` already sits there. With the photo-measured 45 mm of back above the water that means a body depth of about 82 mm and a draft of 37 mm; with the 95 mm estimate, a draft of 43 mm and 52 mm above. Build to the traced profile, set the waterline at 45%, and check that the back lands 38-57 mm up. Pitch the body so the back line rises about **8° toward the tail**; the breast then sits deeper than the stern, which is the "front-heavy" look.

**Posture changes.**
- **Relaxed:** neck drawn in, crown about 85 mm up, bill 29-33° below level with its tip 13-37 mm above the water ("the bill often resting on the surface" [Phillips 1925, p. 20]).
- **Alert:** neck raised; crown up to 94 mm, bill steeper (43°) and tucked back toward the breast (photo, `norsho-female-side.jpg`).
- **Skimming:** head lowered and pushed forward, the bill 20-35° to the surface with its distal half or all of it in the surface film (video, section 7.3), "with its head half submerged" [Bent 1923, p. 140]; "the head is buried low" [Millais 1902, p. 60]; heads under to or past the eye on the photos (185373888, 336069809; appendix). Body position on the water does not change visibly (photo).
- **Upending:** the body tips forward with the tail up and the head and neck under; shovelers "retain the position for a shorter time than the Mallard and ... hold themselves less perpendicularly" (Gundlach 1875, in [Phillips 1925, p. 20]). One upending bird among the photos (327273699, 10 October 2025): tail and rear flank up, body about 45-60° from level (photo, distant).

## 7. Swimming and feeding behavior

### 7.1 What the sources say

- Skimming: "The shoveller seldom tips up to feed by semi-immersion, but paddles quickly along, skimming the surface, with its head half submerged so that whatever is found is taken into the mouth, tasted by the sensitive tongue, and sifted out through the pectinated bristles of the bill if not wanted" [Bent 1923, p. 140]. "This swimming and feeding pose is a highly characteristic one of the Shoveler; the head is buried low and held in such a position that everything is gathered and filtered across the tongue ... All this time the head is kept down, and not raised frequently as with the other surface-feeders when they have obtained some small morsel of food. It is only when a large substance, such as a big water-beetle ... is caught that they throw up the head and swallow" [Millais 1902, pp. 59-60]. A merganser feeding "with neck outstretched, and with the bill held just at the surface of the water, and at a slight angle, so that the head was submerged about to the level of the eyes" was "exactly the manner of feeding usually employed by the shoveller" (Swarth 1911, in [Bent 1923, p. 9]).
- Side to side: "Northern Shovelers often have their heads down in shallow wetlands, busily sweeping their bills side to side" [AAB-ID]; "swinging them side to side to filter out tiny crustacean prey. Sometimes large groups swim in circles to stir up food" [AAB-LH].
- Tipping up: "the Mallard-like tipping is occasionally indulged in. When feeding in this way they are said to retain the position for a shorter time than the Mallard and to hold themselves less perpendicularly (Gundlach, 1875)" [Phillips 1925, p. 20]; McKinney "noted that they predominantly feed at the surface, and to a lesser extent by tipping-up" [Johnsgard 2010, p. 296].
- Circling: "A male and female Shoveler get close together, one behind the other, and commence swimming rapidly round in a circle, the bill of each through which the water passes being immersed to the forehead and held close to the stern of the duck in front, and thus they spin round and round for a minute or two, creating a muddy vortex" [Millais 1902, p. 60]. "Three or four Shovellers will form in line following one after the other and then the leader will swing round in an ellipse, turning and following the hindmost, each one apparently straining through its bill the water stirred by the paddling feet of the one before it. Thus round and round they go with chattering bills" (Forbush 1929, in [Kortright 1953, pp. 218-219]). "The curious habit ... of swimming in close circles one behind the other ... appears to be more common in the spring than in the winter" [Phillips 1925, p. 25]. Captive pairs "would pick out a spot near shore and start the rotary swimming movement, gaining great speed and keeping it up for hours when left undisturbed" (Brüggemann 1876, in [Phillips 1925, p. 30]). "The birds feed in small groups, each bird rotating in a 'pinwheel' motion that stirs up the suspended material in the water column (Bellrose 1976)" [Webb & Brotherson 1988, p. 283]. "All the species of shovelers often forage in small groups, with each bird dabbling in the wake of the one in front (Johnsgard, 1965)" [Johnsgard 2010, p. 297].
- Time budget: drakes on a Utah cooling pond, 28 January-31 March 1984, 10:30-16:30, 15-minute focal samples: **feeding 24.3%, swimming 28.1%, resting 21.9%, preening 16.9%, courtship 6.3%, flight 2.6%** [Webb & Brotherson 1988, p. 283, Table 1]. Night feeding: "Shovellers feed mostly at night, especially in places where they are much pursued by gunners ... they will often remain until the shades of night" on open water by day (Pearson 1916, in [Bent 1923, p. 142]); "well able to feed by night" [Phillips 1925, p. 20].
- Group size: "ordinarily travel in very small groups numbering from three or four to six or eight" [Phillips 1925, p. 21]; "I have never seen over 40 in one company, and very often they pass by in twos and threes" (Pearson 1916, in [Bent 1923, p. 142]); "small flocks of from five to twenty" on open water [Millais 1902, p. 59].
- More on method: "Usually feeds by immersing part or all of head, but occasionally tips up, and rarely filters bottom sediments or mud. Usually does not feed on land. Often feeds cooperatively in groups" [Granholm n.d., p. 1]; "rarely tip up, but filter mud through their bills, swimming with their heads outstretched, bills skimming the water's surface" [BirdWeb]; "Shovelers have been observed diving for food on only a few occasions" [Johnsgard 2010, p. 296].
- Speed: "when he cares to do so, he can move faster on the water than any of the fresh-water ducks"; the captive drake was "rushing at full speed along the water" at rising beetles [Millais 1902, pp. 60-61]; "paddles quickly along" [Bent 1923, p. 140]; circling pairs "swimming rapidly round" [Millais 1902, p. 60]. No source gives a speed.
- Daily pattern: "Most active early and late in day"; "In midday, rests on mud or sand beside water (Palmer 1976)" [Granholm n.d.]; toward evening or on cold days "they become unusually restive, and spend their time swimming quickly about and skimming the surface of the water for floating weed and insects" [Millais 1902, p. 59].
- Other time budgets (not read, **Snippet**): wintering shovelers in north-eastern Algeria spent 63% of the day roosting (sleeping, resting and swimming) and 27% feeding [Khemis et al. 2017] (Snippet).
- Status: Washington autumn movement "begins in August, peaks in September, and continues into November" [BirdWeb]; California "winter resident (late August to early May)" [Granholm n.d.].

### 7.2 What the Willamette photos show

A rough count over the 126 records (appendix), photo selection bias included:
- Of about 20 birds caught feeding, **one was upending** (327273699, 10 October 2025); the rest had the bill, face or whole head in the water while swimming (e.g. 138050550, 185373888, 244539903, 336069809, 343455446, 320600461).
- Heads went under to the eye or past it (185373888, 336069809); skimming birds held the bill flat along the surface with the forehead just above it (244539903 in duckweed).
- Several records show birds swimming in a line one behind another (316921876, three in a line; 62044671).
- Most birds in the photos were swimming with the head up (about 50) or loafing, preening or standing on logs (about 10).

### 7.3 Video estimates

No source gives speeds, bout lengths, circle sizes or rotation rates, so these come from CC-licensed video. YouTube refused this machine's downloads, so the clips are Wikimedia Commons files and one Internet Archive mirror of a Flickr video. A helper agent measured them on my instructions (frames extracted at up to the full frame rate, tiled into labelled contact sheets and read by eye; camera pans removed by correlating stationary background; the bird's position tracked as the centroid of its chestnut flank or white breast and checked by eye). **Everything in this section is an Estimate from video**, from one to a few birds per clip, mostly spring and winter birds, not late-September ones.

- The clips:
  - [Rhododendrites 2020]: one male-female pair circle-feeding, New York, 18 November 2020; oblique high angle, near-fixed camera. 1920×1080 at 59.94 fps, 0-36 s.
  - [Rhododendrites 2021]: a flock of 50-100 in Prospect Park Lake, Brooklyn, 27 November 2021. 1920×1080 at 59.94 fps, 0-67 s.
  - [AO1Uta 2016]: a pair cruising (0-12 s) and feeding side-on (32.5-62.5 s), Niedersächsische Elbtalaue, Germany, 9 April 2016. 1280×720 at 29.97 fps.
  - [Plomp 2009]: a pair spinning side-on (0-48 s), skim-feeding in tandem (67-127 s), the male skimming close up and end-on (128-194 s), then cruising (195-221 s), Netherlands, 21 March 2009. 720×576 anamorphic (corrected), 25 fps.
  - [Seto 2017]: a dense feeding raft of 60-100, Central Park, New York, 9 February 2017. 1280×720 at 25 fps.
- **Scale:** the relaxed floating length, bill tip to tail tip, taken as 0.30 m ("BL" below), cross-checked against the 64 mm bill where it was side-on (the bill scale gave values 10-20% higher).

**Skim feeding: two modes.**
- **Travelling skim:** 1.0 BL/s, **0.32 m/s** (0.28-0.37; n = 3 one-second windows) [Plomp 2009, 117.1-120.1 s, reed-referenced]. The male then slowed over 2 s and stopped to lift his head.
- **Pottering skim, nearly on the spot:** median **0.03 m/s** (10th-90th percentile 0.007-0.12, maximum 0.14; n = 29 one-second windows) [AO1Uta 2016, 32.7-62.2 s]; at most 0.03 m/s [Plomp 2009, 124-127.8 s].
- **Bill angle to the surface:** **20-35°, typically 25-30°**, with the distal half or the whole bill in the surface film; in the travelling skim the eye stays about 3-4 cm above the water [Plomp 2009, 119 s, 128-143 s; AO1Uta 2016, 18 head crops].
- **How deep the head goes** (share of samples):

| Clip and window | Bill only | Bill and face, to the eye | Whole head, past the eye | Head up |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Travelling skim, male [Plomp 2009, 67-121.5 s, 2 Hz, n = 80] | 65% | 35% | 0% | 0% |
| Pottering (dipping) skim, male [AO1Uta 2016, 34.7-62.0 s, 4 Hz, n = 110] | 22% | 33% | 43% | 2% |
| Rafts [Rhododendrites 2021, 8-23 s, about 140 bird-samples; Seto 2017] | - | most males | some; single birds with head and upper neck under, bill near vertical, body level (34.5-37.3 s) | about 4-10% |

- **Bouts:**
  - Travelling skim: head down for **at least 55 s** without a break (the start is cut by an edit), then up for about **2 s**, then down again [Plomp 2009, 67-124 s]. This fits Millais's "All this time the head is kept down" [Millais 1902, p. 60].
  - Pottering (dipping) skim: face or head down for **0.75-2.75 s (median 1.75, n = 11)**, raised for **0.25-1.0 s (median 0.5, n = 12)**; whole-head-under runs 0.25-2.0 s (median 1.0); a dip cycle of about **2.3 s** [AO1Uta 2016].
- **Side-to-side sweep: not detected.** On the male seen end-on [Plomp 2009, 149-153.5 s, 25 fps] the head's residual sideways motion was about 1 mm (noise), with no peak at 1-10 Hz; only a slow 0.3-0.6 Hz drift of about 6 mm peak to peak from the body turning. One bird for 3.3 s, low confidence: if a sweep exists here it is under about 1 cm. AAB's "busily sweeping their bills side to side" [AAB-ID] may describe mud-filtering in shallows rather than surface skimming on open water.

**Circle feeding (spinning).**
- **Birds per circle: 2** in both measurable clips (pairs). In the big flock, knots of 5-15 birds milled with bodies touching and individuals turned about 90° in about 1 s, but no coherent circle could be measured [Rhododendrites 2021; Seto 2017].
- **Rotation:**
  - [Plomp 2009, 0-48.3 s]: 12 revolutions of **3.0-4.75 s (median 3.4, mean 3.63; about 100°/s)**, counterclockwise from above for all 12.
  - [Rhododendrites 2020]: **2.2-2.8 s per revolution while turning** (130-165°/s), with pauses of 0.8-1.9 s at one heading, so 3.4-4.4 s per revolution on average (82-105°/s); clockwise for all three bouts. The female turned slightly slower than the male (3-3.8 s per 360°): the pair is not rigidly locked.
  - **Direction is constant within a bout** and differs between pairs.
- **Geometry:** the two birds' centres are **0.70-0.99 BL apart (mean 0.85 BL, about 0.25 m)** [Rhododendrites 2020, 4 frames, foreshortening corrected], so each centre runs on a circle about 0.25 m across and the whole swept disc is about **0.5-0.6 m** across. The pair alternates between lying side by side head-to-tail (antiparallel) and a V with the heads together, bodies 60-120° apart. About 1 BL apart in the side view [Plomp 2009].
- **Bout length:** **3.6-7.7 s** per spinning bout [Rhododendrites 2020, n = 3], between which the pair fed side by side without turning (8 s) or turned irregularly; at least **48 s** continuous in [Plomp 2009] (cut by an edit). Millais's "a minute or two" is the long end.
- **Heads under** to the eye or crown almost the whole time; brief raises of 0.25-0.5 s.

**Tip-ups: none** in about 70 bird-minutes of feeding (mostly the rafts). The deepest posture was the head and upper neck under with the body level, not tipped [Rhododendrites 2021, 34.5-37.3 s]. So upending is rarer than about 1 per 70 bird-minutes in these clips; with the one upending bird among about 20 feeding birds in the Willamette photos (section 7.2), it is a rare event (Estimate).

**Cruising and loafing.**
- Cruising with the head up: **1.2-1.5 BL/s, 0.36-0.45 m/s** [AO1Uta 2016, 1-10 s]; 1.4-1.7 BL/s, 0.42-0.51 m/s (n = 22 one-second windows) [Plomp 2009, 197.6-219.2 s]; 0.60 m/s on one frame scaled by the bill. **Typical 0.45 m/s** (0.36-0.6): the same as today's `swim` cruise speed.
- Loafing with the head up: under 0.02 m/s [Plomp 2009, 122.5-124 s].
- Head posture while cruising: neck drawn in, the head sitting on the shoulders, the bill **27-35° below level** (n = 3 frames), matching the photos' 29-33° (section 2.4).

**Spacing, feeding but not circling.**
- A feeding pair: **0.7-1.2 BL** centre to centre, the male following the female nose to tail [AO1Uta 2016, 36, 45, 55 s].
- Tandem skim: the male's bill **0-0.3 BL behind the female's tail**, held for at least 6 s [Plomp 2009, 115-121 s]: "each bird dabbling in the wake of the one in front" (Johnsgard 1965, in [Johnsgard 2010, p. 297]).
- Dense rafts: **0.5-1.0 BL**, bodies touching or overlapping [Rhododendrites 2021, 56 s; Seto 2017, 8 and 31 s].

**Could not be measured:** tip-up frequency, duration and angle (none occurred); a side-to-side sweep; circles of more than two birds; distances between knots in a flock; the female's own skim numbers.

- Male iris yellow (high saturation, hue about 40-55°) and female iris dark brown (low luminance).

## 12. Open questions and confidence

**High confidence**
- **Late-September males are in eclipse.** Four old authorities, Pyle's specimen dates (adult body moult 7 October-4 February, pooled with the two blue-winged teals) and the Willamette photos (25 of 25 sexable September males in eclipse; the first transitional males on 6 October) agree.
- The eclipse male's marks: yellow iris, dark bill, grey to sooty head, rufous scalloped flanks, and (photo) the white rear-flank patch.
- The female-type's marks: orange-sided bill, dark brown iris (modern source and every Willamette photo; the older sources' "yellow" female iris is contradicted), mottled buff-brown body.
- Linear measures (wing, culmen, tarsus, mass) from five sources that agree within their ranges.
- The bill's base and tip widths (15-16 and 28-32 mm) and the widest point in the distal third.
- Behaviour in words: skimming with the head half submerged, side-to-side sweeping, rare and brief tip-ups, group circling with each bird in the wake of the one in front.

**Medium confidence**
- **The width profile between base and tip** (section 2.4): four outlines, only one scaled, one of them dried; they agree on the shape to within about ±0.08 of the widest width except at the extreme tip.
- **The depth profile**: two females, one culmen-scaled scale each, matching AVONET at the nostril.
- **Floating heights** (back 38-57 mm, crown 81-94): three photos, each shot from 10-13° above the water, each scaled by one bird's culmen (±5%). The recommended values are means.
- **Male share** of about half: sexing from photos favours yellow-eyed males.
- **Tail past wingtip** 22-39 mm: three photos.

**Low confidence or not verified**
- **Draft (0.42-0.53 of body depth)**: no photo shows it; it rests on a body depth taken from one standing bird and an ellipsoid buoyancy estimate with an assumed body width.
- **Head length (45 mm)**: an Estimate; the feathered-outline ratios (1.0-1.56) scatter widely.
- **Tibia and the side toes**: Estimates; hidden while floating.
- **The white rear-flank patch as an eclipse mark**: photo evidence only (six birds); no source describes it for the eclipse plumage. If later evidence says it is a sign of moult, drop it from the eclipse layer list.
- **Lamellar density** (Nudds & Bowlby 1984): Snippet only, not used for the model.
- **The Algerian time budget** (Khemis et al. 2017): Snippet only.
- **All video numbers** (section 7.3): one to a few birds per clip, spring and winter birds in Europe and New York, measured by a helper agent on stated methods; I checked its methods but not every frame. The no-sweep result rests on one bird for 3.3 s. The skim-mode split (travelling against pottering) and the tip-up rate are Estimates.

**Sources I could not read** (not relied on for any number): Palmer 1976 (*Handbook of North American Birds*, vol. 2; lending-only), Pyle 2008, the *Birds of the World* account (Dubowy et al. 2020), DuBowy 1985, both Guillemain et al. 2000 papers, Kooloos et al. 1989, Johnsgard 1965, McKinney 1970, and "Molts and Plumages of Ducks (Anatinae): An Evaluation of Pyle (2005)", *Waterbirds* 34: 481-494 (https://doi.org/10.1675/063.034.0411). Oregon sources (Marshall et al. 2003, *Birds of Oregon*; eBird bar charts) were not reached; the Oregon timing rests on BirdWeb (Washington), Granholm (California) and the iNaturalist records.

**Worth doing if higher confidence is needed**
- A caliper series of bill widths at fixed stations on museum skins (the profile's shape rests on four outlines).
- A side-on photo from water level of a swimming shoveler, to measure the waterline without the 10-13° camera correction.
- Timing skim bouts, sweep rate and circling from longer, fixed-camera video (section 7.3).
