# Red-winged Blackbird: measurements, late-September plumage and ages, field marks, perched posture, and behaviour

Date: 2026-10-13
Purpose: source data for the detailed Red-winged Blackbird (`rewbla`), lofted in code like the Belted Kingfisher (`belkin1`), and for the cast and settings of the birds in Willow Lake's reed bed. The scene: Willow Lake, Willamette Valley, Oregon, a late-September morning. The player stands at (0, 1.6, 0); the reed bed is x −16..−3, z −2..−14, so its stems lie 3.6 m to about 21 m away. Today's block male sits at (−8, 1.9, −5), **9.4 m** away. Through binoculars (7.5° on a 1080-pixel-tall view) a bird at 10 m is drawn at about **1.2 mm per pixel**; through the telescope (2°) at about **0.32 mm per pixel** (section 4.4). The epaulet's anatomy, the song spread's kinematics, and the reed and cattail stems with how blackbirds grip and straddle them are in the companion document, `2026-10-13-blackbird-display-and-reeds.md`; this one gives the bird itself, its resting and alert posture, its late-September habits, and the numbers.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt or an abstract; I could not read the page, so treat it as unverified. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I read points by hand on a grid laid over a cited photo (or on a silhouette cut from it by colour) and scaled them by the exposed culmen (male 20 mm, female 17 mm, section 2.5), cross-checked by the folded wing's chord where the wrist could be placed (section 5.4). "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated. "By eye" means a judgement from the photos without a measurement.

## 1. Summary

- **Race:** the valley's birds are the Northwestern Red-wing, ***A. p. caurinus***, a "Common permanent resident of counties west of Cascades" [Gabrielson & Jewett 1940, p. 525], whose map takes in Portland, Salem, Corvallis and Eugene [p. 523, fig. 17]. It is **not a "bicolored" race**: the adult male's median coverts are "clear buff, unmarked with black" [van Rossem 1926, p. 230], deep ochraceous-buff in winter [Ridgway 1902, p. 341]. The old Willamette "Bicolored Red-wing" specimens "prove to be *A. p. caurinus*" [Gabrielson & Jewett 1940, p. 606].
- **The sexes differ a great deal in size.** Ridgway's *caurinus* (9 of each sex): wing **123.2 / 103.1** mm (male / female), tail **91.7 / 77.5**, culmen from base **24.4 / 20.8**, tarsus **29.5 / 26.9**, middle toe **21.8 / 19.1**, skins **223 / 189** long [Ridgway 1902, p. 341]. Masses about **61-71 g / 42 g** [Grinnell & Storer 1924, p. 401; Searcy 1979, p. 358]. The female is **0.85 of the male** in every length but the legs (tarsus 0.91), and about 0.69 of his mass (Computed).
- **Model lengths**, bill tip to tail tip as traced: **about 206 mm (male)** and **about 175 mm (female)** (Photo-measured; section 6). Perched birds are a little shorter than skins.
- **The female does not need her own trace, but she does need her own size.** Her proportions match his within 2% (culmen/wing, tail/wing, bill depth/culmen), so the male's trace scaled by **0.85**, with her own tarsus (27 mm, 2 mm longer than scaling gives), is honest at every footprint in the reed bed. **The schema can't express this today: `variants` are paint only**, and the kingfisher's sexes shared one size (section 2.7). A side-on female profile good enough to trace is in hand if the plan prefers it (section 8).
- **Late-September plumage:** both sexes are fresh from a **complete** moult that most birds finish by about 1 October [Meanley & Bond 1970, p. 27]. Adult males are black with buff and rusty edges on the back, scapulars and tertials; at rest the red shoulder is usually hidden and **only the buff bar of the median coverts shows** [Mayr in Bent 1958, pp. 127-128]. First-year males are blackish, "much veiled with buffy and ferruginous edgings", with **orange, black-spotted** shoulders and black-mottled median coverts [Dwight 1900 in Bent 1958, p. 135]. Females, adult and first-year alike at a distance [Unitt 2004, p. 228], are dark brown, heavily streaked, with a buff eyebrow and peach-buff throat, and *caurinus* females have "beautiful deep chestnut" edges [Unitt 2004, p. 228]. No bird in juvenal plumage was found in the valley's late-September photos.
- **iNaturalist, valley box, 13 September to 12 October:** 114 records (82 observer-days). 68 are of males, 26 of females, 20 can't be sexed; counted as birds seen per observer-day, **74 males and 75 females** (section 3.3). Of 35 males that could be aged, 31 look adult and **4-5 first-year**. Flocks of 6 to about 1,000; groups of 2-6 are common, often both sexes. **18% of records are in cattails, tules or marsh edge**; the rest on trees, shrubs, posts, wires, fields and feeders.
- **Versions: `male`, `female`, `firstYearMale`. Cast: five birds as a loose group in the reed bed: two `male`, one `firstYearMale`, two `female`** (section 3.4). One male sings.
- **Singing males are expected, at a low rate.** Five valley records show males singing or in song spread on five days, 23 September to 6 October (section 7.3). A *caurinus* male recorded on Vancouver Island on 24 September sang **13 songs in 270 s** (one every 21 s) [XC159672]. Eastern sources say red-wings "Ordinarily ... do not sing at all in the fall" [Saunders in Bent 1958, p. 142]; the Pacific Northwest's resident birds evidently do.
- **What reads:** through binoculars at 10 m (1.2 mm/px) the male is 170 px long, his buff covert bar 2.5-4 px deep, his red shoulder 16-29 px when shown; the female's streaks (2-3 mm) and eyebrow (2.5-4 mm) are 2-3 px. At 20 m (2.4 mm/px) her streaks blur into a mottled brown and his buff bar is 1-2 px. The telescope resolves everything in the bed, the first-year's black epaulet spots included (section 4.4).
- **Behaviour:** after the dawn flight to feed, autumn birds return to the cattails, "where they rested, preened, sang, and bathed for much of the remainder of the day" [Orians 1961, p. 289]. They feed by "gaping", the bill pushed into a cattail head or stem and forced open [Orians 1961, p. 295]. They "cling to upright stalks ... and often sway back and forth" [Grinnell & Storer 1924, p. 401]. Flight is "bursts of rapid wingbeats, between which are slight intermittent pauses, producing a somewhat wavy motion" [Bent 1958, p. 139], at **10.0-11.3 m/s** [Evans & Drickamer 1994, p. 155]. **No flight-initiation distance exists for the species**; related birds flush at 8-14 m from a walking person [Blumstein 2006].
- **Design points the decisions didn't anticipate** (section 12.1): per-version size; the epaulet must be hidden by default and shown only by pose, not painted as a fixed red patch; the guide's "red-and-yellow shoulder" is eastern wording; the catalog's call timing (`every: [8, 25]`, `prefer: 'song'`) is far too much song for autumn.

## 2. Measurements

The primary tables I could read are Ridgway's *Birds of North and Middle America*, Part II (1902; sexed means for *caurinus* and the other races), van Rossem's 1926 revision of the western races (sexed, with bill depth at the base), and AVONET's seven skins. Gabrielson & Jewett 1940 repeat Ridgway's *caurinus* ranges in inches. Pyle's *Identification Guide* (1997), Dunning's sexed masses, and the Birds of the World account (Yasukawa & Searcy) could not be read.

### 2.1 Ridgway 1902 (sexed skins, by race)

The race of the Pacific Northwest is *A. p. caurinus* Ridgway 1901, whose type is an adult female from Cedar Hill, Vancouver Island [Ridgway 1901, p. 154]. Its range is the "Northwest coast district, from British Columbia (Vancouver Island and coast of mainland) south through western Washington and Oregon to northern California (Mendocino County, May 20)" [Ridgway 1902, p. 341]. *Nevadensis* (Grinnell 1914) had not been described; Ridgway put Great Basin birds under *neutralis*, and the bicolored birds in a separate species, *A. gubernator* [pp. 326-328, 339-340].

"Similar to *A. p. phœniceus* but wing and bill longer, the latter more slender; adult male with buff of middle wing-coverts deeper (deep ochraceous-buff or ochraceous in winter plumage); adult female rather more heavily streaked with black below and, in winter plumage, with upper parts much more conspicuously marked with rusty" [Ridgway 1902, p. 341].

### 2.5 The bill: which culmen

Ridgway and van Rossem give the culmen "from base", measured to the skull under the forehead feathers, and AVONET's "culmen" is the same. What shows on a live bird, from where the forehead feathering meets the culmen to the tip, is shorter:
- AVONET's nostril-to-tip is 16.3 mm (males) and 13.5 (females), and the nostril, "small, oval ... posteriorly in contact with feathering" [Ridgway 1902, p. 320], adds about 3-4 mm to the feathering (Estimate). That gives **19.5-20 mm and 16.5-17 mm**.
- On the profile male (`rewbla-137083235-234055602.jpeg`) the culmen from the feathering is 51-52 px, and the folded wing, wrist to tip, 311 px. Taking the wing as Ridgway's 123.2 mm gives 0.396 mm/px and a culmen of **20.4 mm**. On the profile female (`rewbla-315924151-570755212.jpeg`) the same check gives 0.51 mm/px and **17 mm** (Photo-measured).
- So **the exposed culmen is about 20 mm (male) and 17 mm (female)**, about 4 mm less than the museum culmen. These are the scales for every photo in this document, and the model's `culmen`.
- **Depth at the feathering:** van Rossem's depth at the base is 11.7 (M) and 10.2 (F); AVONET's at the nostril 9.2 and 8.0. On the sharp head of `rewbla-407442685-748750030.jpg` the bill is 148 px deep at the feathering against a 244 px culmen, which at 20 mm is **12 mm** (Photo-measured). Adopted **11 mm (M) and 9.5 mm (F)**.
- **Width at the feathering:** AVONET's 5.7 (M) and 5.3 (F) are at the nostril; Ridgway says only "its basal width much less" than the depth. No front-on photo shows the bill's base square on. Adopted **7.5 mm (M) and 6.5 mm (F)** (Estimate), a depth/width of about 1.45.

### 2.6 The foot

Anisodactyl, three toes forward and a stout hind toe. "Tarsus decidedly longer than culmen, its anterior scutella distinct; middle toe, with claw, as long as tarsus or slightly shorter; lateral toes with claws reaching to base of middle claw; hallux much shorter than lateral toes, but much stouter, its claw decidedly shorter than the digit" [Ridgway 1902, p. 320]. Legs and feet: "deep black" in the adult male [p. 330]; "dark horn color or dusky" in the female [p. 331]. The grip is strong: on a habitual approach to a nest "the stout sharp claws of the feet perforate the tules and leave series of punctures, three in a row, in the tough blades" [Grinnell & Storer 1924, p. 401].

No source gives the claws or the hind toe. From Ridgway's proportions (Estimate): middle claw about 7 mm (M) and 6 (F), so that middle toe and claw (29 / 25) is a little short of the tarsus; hind toe about 12 mm with a claw of about 9 (M), 11 and 8 (F). The tibia is feathered black to the joint; about 5 mm of it shows below the belly (Estimate, by eye on the side-on photos).

**Plainly: she needs her own size, not her own trace.** A per-version scale of 0.85 on the male's trace, with her own leg lengths and her own paint, is honest. But **the current schema can't do it**: `detailSchema.variants` is a record of paint layers only, and every version of a species shares one `profile`, one set of `measurements`, and one `length`. The plan must add per-version geometry (a `scale` and measurement overrides on a version) or give the female a second trace. If it chooses a second trace, `rewbla-315924151-570755212.jpg` is a sharp, strictly side-on female (section 8). The first-year male is 3-5% shorter-winged than adults [Searcy 1979, p. 358; Selander & Giller 1960, p. 210], which nobody could see; he can share the adult male's size.

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

**The moult.** Both age classes moult completely after breeding, the young as well as the adults, so by late September nearly all birds wear fresh feathers.
- "A complete postjuvenal molt, beginning in August, the time varying for the earlier and later broods, produces the first winter plumage" [Bent 1958, p. 135]; "Begins usually from 45 to 60 days after the young bird has left nest. This is a complete molt except for the retention (by some individuals) of a few underwing coverts" [Meanley & Bond 1970, p. 22]. Of first-year males, 16% replace every feather; most keep a few hidden underwing coverts [Selander & Giller 1960, pp. 205, 208-209].
- "A complete postnuptial molt occurs in August, at which young and old become practically indistinguishable" [Bent 1958, p. 135, quoting Dwight].
- "The molting of most Red-winged Blackbirds is nearly complete by 1 October. At this time a few birds still have some pin feathers in the head region, and the central tail feathers and the inner secondaries are only partly unsheathed. Virtually all Red-winged Blackbirds have completed their molts by the middle of October" [Meanley & Bond 1970, p. 27]. While moulting, "they have a ragged or 'moth eaten' appearance and usually a slower and more labored flight" [p. 27].

**Valley breeding dates**, which set when the last young begin their moult: "egg dates between May 3 and June 6, mostly around Portland" [Gabrielson & Jewett 1940, p. 525]; "Breeding records: May 24 to July 15" (Lane County) [Gullion 1951, p. 145]; in Benton County the earliest egg was 15 April, with a second nesting peak in mid-June [Evenden 1950]. Young of the latest broods, fledged in mid-July, would begin moulting in early September and finish in October or later (Computed from Payne's 45-65 + 60-70 days). **So in late September most birds are fresh or finishing; a few first-years and adults may still show short central tail feathers or a scrappy head; birds in full juvenal plumage should be rare.**

**Adult male.**
- Summer: "Uniform deep black, with a very faint greenish blue gloss in certain lights; lesser wing-coverts bright poppy red or vermilion (varying to scarlet or even, more rarely, to orange-chrome); middle coverts wholly buff or ochraceous-buff (paler at tips, sometimes almost white in midsummer birds); bill, legs, and feet deep black; iris brown" [Ridgway 1902, p. 330].
- **Winter (fresh, as in late September):** "Similar to the summer male but buff of middle wing-coverts deeper (more ochraceous-buff or buffy clay color) and interscapulars and scapulars narrowly margined with rusty" [p. 330]; "lustrous greenish black, feathers of head and back, greater wing coverts and tertiaries edged more or less (according to the individual) with buff and ferruginous brown. Below, the edgings are paler or absent. The bright scarlet-vermilion 'shoulders' are acquired together with the rich ochraceous buff median coverts" [Dwight 1900 in Bent 1958, p. 135]. "The full brilliancy of the spring plumage is produced by wear, the buff and brown edgings disappearing" [Bent 1958, p. 135]. "Nonbreeding males often look scaly and have incomplete red shoulder patches" [AAB-ID].
- *caurinus*: "buff of middle wing-coverts deeper (deep ochraceous-buff or ochraceous in winter plumage)" [Ridgway 1902, p. 341].
- **At rest, the red is mostly hidden.** Of males on cattail stalks early in the season: "They were rather fluffed up and only the yellow margin of their shield showed" [Mayr in Bent 1958, pp. 127-128]. "The adult male Red-wing when absorbed in feeding is a plain blackbird with a pale yellow stripe on his shoulder or one with a narrow band of red. The color may even be entirely covered up by the prevailing blackness of his costume" [Townsend in Bent 1958, pp. 129-130]. Of Utah birds, "while sitting, none showed their red epaulets ... Still only the yellowish crescent showed on the wing" [Barnes in Bent 1958, p. 165]. The companion document covers how the red is covered and uncovered.

**First-year male** (after the preformative moult).
- "'entire plumage, including wings and tail,' is 'greenish black much veiled with buffy and ferruginous edgings, palest below and faint or absent on primaries and rectrices. Lesser wing coverts ('shoulders') dull orpiment-orange, each feather with subterminal bars or spots of black. Median coverts rich ochraceous buff usually mottled with black subterminal areas chiefly on the inner webs, the shafts usually black'" [Dwight 1900 in Bent 1958, p. 135].
- "Black; scapulars and interscapulars broadly margined with rusty and light grayish buffy; pileum and hindneck more or less streaked with the same; innermost greater wing-coverts and tertials broadly edged with light rusty or buffy ...; lesser wing-coverts more or less intermixed with black (except in older birds) and middle coverts with more or less of black (mostly black in younger birds), the red of lesser coverts more orange than in adults; black of under parts more or less broken by dull whitish or buffy margins to feathers, and superciliary region showing a more or less distinct indication of a buffy or dull whitish stripe" [Ridgway 1902, pp. 330-331]; "Winter birds in immature plumage have the lighter markings more distinct and more pronouncedly rusty and buffy than spring examples" [p. 330, footnote].
- "Black with feathers of upper parts edged with brown or buff, and those of underparts edged with buff or white. Bird has speckled appearance. Shoulder patch usually orange with black mottling, especially in the yellow band (median coverts) bordering the orange. Some immature males have reddish shoulder patch like adult, but have black flecks in yellow band. Also, some have blackish shoulder patch" [Meanley & Bond 1970, pp. 22-23].
- "First-year males look like mature males but with rusty edgings on their black feathers. They have red wing-patches, but they are generally paler than mature males and may be darkly spotted" [BirdWeb].
- Such birds can be mistaken for bicolored birds, whose median coverts are black: "Failure to appreciate certain of these differences has led to some of the many erroneous extralimital records of 'gubernator'" [van Rossem 1926, p. 217].

**Female** (adult and first-year).
- Summer: "Above dusky, varied with paler streaks and edgings; ... lesser coverts broadly margined with brownish gray or red, or both (often extensively red); middle coverts black, broadly margined terminally with white or pale buffy; a broad superciliary stripe of white, finely streaked with dusky, usually becoming buff or salmon color anteriorly ...; malar region and under parts dull white (the chin, throat, and malar region often buff or salmon pink), the under parts of the body broadly streaked or striped with dusky (sometimes almost black), these stripes broadest on flanks; ... bill dusky (the mandible usually paler, more or less horn colored); legs and feet dark horn color or dusky" [Ridgway 1902, p. 331].
- **Winter (fresh):** "the lighter markings of upper parts more conspicuous, more pronouncedly buffy and rusty, and the sides of head and under parts more or less tinged with buffy (superciliary stripe sometimes clear buff)" [p. 331].
- ***caurinus*:** "adult female rather more heavily streaked with black below and, in winter plumage, with upper parts much more conspicuously marked with rusty" [Ridgway 1902, p. 341]; "Females richly marked in strongly contrasting colors" [van Rossem 1926, p. 230]; "In western North America, the opposite extremes are found in *A. p. caurinus* of the Pacific Northwest, in which most of the edges are a beautiful deep chestnut, and in *A. p. mailliardorum*", whose edges are narrow and dull [Unitt 2004, p. 228]. "In the West, the Red-winged and Tricolored are in fresh plumage in the fall only. Through the winter and spring the feather edges ... wear off" [Unitt 2004, pp. 228-229].
- **Age:** "in female Red-winged Blackbirds first-year and older birds are essentially identical" [Unitt 2004, p. 228]. First-year females "Usually lack ... pink of chin and throat (which may be buffy, yellow, or light salmon) and crimson of shoulder patch (which may be rusty, orange, or grayish)" [Meanley & Bond 1970, p. 23]; "a pinkish or salmon tinge is often found in females in any of these plumages, especially about the chin and head, and an orange or crimson tinge may show on the 'shoulders' of the older birds" [Dwight 1900 in Bent 1958, pp. 135-136]. In Washington "around 10-15% of the females have pinkish to reddish throats" [Paulson 2022]. In western females "first fall females often have a certain amount of red on the lesser coverts" [van Rossem 1926, p. 218].
- "Females are crisply streaked and dark brownish overall, paler on the breast and often show a whitish eyebrow" [AAB-ID]; females "can resemble sparrows but differ in behavior, have a spikier bill, and a darker lower belly" [Audubon].

**Juvenal plumage** (before the moult): "dull brownish black (no red at this stage), the feathers edged with buff ... Below pinkish buff, ochraceous on the chin, thickly streaked ... with brownish black. Obscure superciliary line ochraceous-buff" [Dwight 1900 in Bent 1958, p. 135]; "superciliary and malar stripes, chin, and throat yellowish (ocher yellow or buff-yellow)" [Ridgway 1902, p. 331]. Not seen in the valley's late-September photos (section 3.3).

**Is *caurinus* "bicolored"?** No. Bicolored males (*californicus*, *mailliardorum* of central California) have "middle wing-coverts black or with black tips" [Ridgway 1902, p. 326]. *caurinus* "is, except in the extreme southwest corner of its range, essentially an immaculate buff winged form" [van Rossem 1926, p. 226]. In Oregon, "We occasionally get a bird with little or none of the buffy edging to the wing coverts but that otherwise has the characteristics of either the Northwestern Red-wing or the Nevada Red-wing" [Gabrielson & Jewett 1940, p. 606]. The valley's photos show the border as buff to ochraceous-orange; none showed a black median-covert bar on an adult (section 3.3).

### 3.2 Subspecies

The valley's birds are *caurinus* in every treatment I could read:
- "Northwest coast district, from British Columbia (Vancouver Island and coast of mainland) south through western Washington and Oregon to northern California" [Ridgway 1902, p. 341].
- "In Oregon: Common permanent resident of counties west of Cascades" [Gabrielson & Jewett 1940, p. 525]; the map's area 2 (*caurinus*) covers the Willamette Valley [p. 523, fig. 17]. "Johnson (1880), who listed it from the Willamette Valley (Salem and Forest Grove) as 'very abundant in summer'" [p. 525].
- The AOU's *caurinus* breeds "along the coast from southwestern British Columbia (Courtenay, Abbotsford) to northwestern California (Eureka, Requa) ... Winters throughout its range" [AOU 1957, p. 528]. It names no Oregon locality; *nevadensis* breeds in "eastern Oregon (Gateway, Prospect)" [p. 528].
- *nevadensis* (east of the Cascades) differs little in the male; its females are "decidedly less buffy than *caurinus*" [van Rossem 1926, p. 228], their "bright rusty edging on back and wings replaced by ashy and pale ochraceous" [Grinnell 1914, p. 107].
- "I cannot find that it differs materially in its habits from the other California races" [Bent 1958, p. 168]. Paint the valley's birds as *caurinus*: the male's median-covert border deep buff to ochraceous, the female dark, heavily streaked, with chestnut edges.

### 3.4 Recommendation

**Versions:**
- **`male`**: adult male in fresh basic plumage: glossy black, the back, scapulars, greater coverts and tertials edged rusty and buff (narrowly), the head and nape with a few brown tips; the red shoulder with its deep buff to ochraceous border, **hidden at rest except for the buff bar**. Paint from section 4.1.
- **`female`**: adult and first-year alike (Unitt). *caurinus* colours: dark brown above with chestnut and buff edges, heavily streaked dark brown on buff below, darkest on the belly and flanks, a long buff eyebrow, peach-buff throat. A trace of rusty-red on the shoulder is honest on some (van Rossem 1926, p. 218; Ridgway's "often extensively red"), not required.
- **`firstYearMale`**: blackish, heavily veiled with rusty and buff edges, scaly or speckled; brown crown and a faint buff eyebrow; the shoulder orange, spotted black; the buff bar mottled black. Paint only, on the adult male's size.

**Cast: five birds, a loose group in the reed bed: two `male`, one `firstYearMale`, two `female`.** Basis:
- **How many:** in western Oregon "the migrating flocks and summer congregations are much smaller than those east of the mountains" [Gabrielson & Jewett 1940, p. 525]; Brewer's Blackbirds "frequently associated with the Red-winged Blackbirds, although mixed flocks remain comparatively small" [p. 529]. Around Portland "in September the blackbirds congregate in flocks of considerable size and remain until November" [Jewett & Gabrielson 1929, p. 30]; in Lane County "Records of 1 to 2800 birds evenly distributed through the year" [Gullion 1951, p. 145]. In the valley's photos the groups perched together are of 2 to 6 (median about 3). A bed of 13 × 12 m holding a handful of birds, with flocks passing over or dropping in, is honest. **Five** is the top of "a few"; four (drop a female) is as honest if performance asks.
- **Which sexes:** the sexes segregate in autumn, "though by no means complete" [Orians 1961, pp. 289-290], and "Males revert to flocking behavior as soon as they quit their territories" [Nero 1956, p. 16]. But in the valley's records small groups of both sexes (6-8) are about as common as single-sex ones (8), and birds seen per observer-day are half female (section 3.3). Two and two, with a first-year male, matches both.
- **Which ages:** first-year males are a minority in the photos (11-17% of aged males) but certainly commoner than that; one in five birds is honest. No juvenile version: none was found, and the moult is complete by October [Meanley & Bond 1970, p. 27]. A first-year female is the `female`.
- **A loose group:** spread through the bed, 1-6 m between neighbours, one or two birds sometimes on the same stem cluster (Estimate, from the photos' groups of 2-6 within a metre or two of one another).
- **Singing males: yes, one.** The male at the existing `reed-perch` point (9.4 m) is the singer (section 7.3). A second male singing once in a while is honest; not all five should sing.

## 5. Structure

### 5.1 The bill

- A compressed cone, "elongate-conical or cuneate, compressed, with straight or nearly straight outlines"; the culmen "straight or nearly so ... elevated and arched basally, more or less flattened ... ending posteriorly in an obtuse point" into the forehead; the gape's "rictal portion strongly deflexed"; the gonys "straight or nearly so" [Ridgway 1902, p. 320]. "*A. p. caurinus* has the feeblest bill of all the western forms" [Grinnell 1914, p. 108]: long and slender for a red-wing.
- On the side-on heads (`rewbla-407442685-748750030.jpg`, `rewbla-385332115-705419729.jpg`) the culmen and gonys are straight to the tip, the tip sharp and in line with the bill's axis; the depth falls almost linearly from the base to the tip, a little fuller in the basal third (photo). The gape line runs straight back and turns down under the eye.
- For the model: `bill.taper` about **0.9-1.0** with no curve (straight, `curve` 0); depth / width at the base about 1.45 (section 2.5).

### 5.2 Eye and head

- **Eye:** iris "brown" [Ridgway 1902, p. 330], dark; visible diameter about **4.5 mm** (photo: 55 px on `rewbla-407442685-748750030.jpg` at 0.08 mm/px; 9.5 px on the female at 0.5 mm/px), so `eye.radius` **2.3 mm** (male) and **2.2 mm** (female).
- **Where it sits:** its centre about **12 mm** behind the culmen's feathering point and level with the bill tip on the alert male (`rewbla-407442685-748750030.jpg`); **10 mm** behind and 5 mm below on the female (Photo-measured). The eye to the bill tip is about **30 mm** (male) and **25 mm** (female) (Photo-measured; culmen / eye-to-tip 0.63-0.67).
- **Head:** long, with a flat forehead running into the culmen and a flat crown in the male; the crown about **9 mm** above the eye's centre in the male and **12 mm** in the female, whose head looks rounder (Photo-measured; one or two photos each).

### 5.3 Body

"Stocky, broad-shouldered" with "a hump-backed silhouette while perched" [AAB-ID]. Front-on, the male's breast is **58-63 mm** across, fluffed on a cold morning, at the culmen's scale (`rewbla-407442685-748750030.jpg`; Photo-measured); sleeked, about 50-55 (Estimate). The female's front-on breast is 1.7 times her head's width (`rewbla-407001559-747859805.jpg`).

### 5.4 Folded wing

- "Rather long and pointed"; the eighth, seventh or sixth primary longest [Ridgway 1902, pp. 319-320].
- Primary projection past the secondaries (Kipp's distance) **29 mm (M), 25 (F)** [Tobias et al. 2022, raw rows]: about a quarter of the chord.
- On the profile male the wing's tips lie under the tail about one-third of the way from its base to its tip, the two wings' tips slightly offset (photo, `rewbla-137083235-234055602.jpeg`); the folded wing covers the flank from the shoulder to the vent.
- The wrist sits under the front of the shoulder patch; the lesser coverts (the red) lie on the forearm, the median coverts (the buff) as a row below them (photo). The companion document gives the epaulet's feather tracts.

### 5.5 Tail

"More or less rounded (sometimes nearly even), the rectrices broadest near ends" [Ridgway 1902, p. 320]: `tail.shape` **`rounded`**, 92 mm (M) and 77.5 (F), three-quarters of the wing. "Males often sit with tail slightly flared" [AAB-ID]. Some birds in late September will still have short or growing central feathers [Meanley & Bond 1970, p. 27].

### 5.6 Legs and feet

Tarsus 29.5 / 26.9 mm, longer than the culmen; middle toe 21.8 / 19.1; the hind toe stout with a strong claw (section 2.6). On the photos the toes wrap a stem completely; on vertical stems one foot is set above the other (`rewbla-317454570-573633273.jpg`, `rewbla-137083235-234055602.jpeg`); about 15-25 mm of tarsus shows below the belly when the bird stands up, little when it crouches (photo). The companion document covers the grip and straddle.

## 7. Behaviour

The quantitative studies are from California (Orians), New York (Allen), Maryland and the South (Meanley), the Great Lakes (Nero) and Pennsylvania (Peek); none is from the Willamette Valley, and none counts what an autumn bird does minute by minute. Where they disagree I say so; the settings (section 11) mark what is sourced and what is chosen.

### 7.1 Status in the valley

- "Very common permanent resident (80/117—68%). Records of 1 to 2800 birds evenly distributed through the year. No variation in local populations evident with changing seasons" (southern Willamette Valley; "very common" = recorded on 45-69% of record days) [Gullion 1951, pp. 133, 145].
- Around Portland "A permanent resident, but much more common during migration than at other seasons. It usually winters in small straggling flocks in sheltered places along the river bottoms ... Again in September the blackbirds congregate in flocks of considerable size and remain until November, when their numbers decrease markedly" [Jewett & Gabrielson 1929, p. 30].
- In Benton County "the bulk of the local summering population moved out of the study area in late July" [Evenden 1950, p. 43].
- Brewer's Blackbird, the commonest companion: "Records of up to 2000 birds frequent from early October to early April" [Gullion 1951, p. 145]; "In western Oregon, it is frequently associated with the Red-winged Blackbirds, although mixed flocks remain comparatively small" [Gabrielson & Jewett 1940, p. 529]. European Starlings and Brewer's Blackbirds appear with red-wings in the valley's photos (section 3.3).

### 7.2 Flocks, roosts, and the day in late September

- "Outside the breeding season Redwings are highly gregarious, travelling and feeding in flocks of varying size, and roosting together in favored sites, usually over water" [Orians 1961, p. 289].
- **The day:** at a California marsh in autumn, "the morning exodus lasted only about 30 minutes. After the main morning feeding, the birds returned to the cattails where they rested, preened, sang, and bathed for much of the remainder of the day. Another major feeding flight in midafternoon preceded roosting for the night. Though the marsh was always full of birds at mid-day, there was a steady movement in and out" [Orians 1961, p. 289]. In New York the morning flight from the roost begins "a few minutes before sunrise, flocks are continually in sight for about thirty minutes" [Allen 1914, p. 107]; "Blackbirds move out of roosts each morning at about dawn or shortly after and return in the evening, usually before sunset" [Meanley 1965, p. 223]. **So by mid-morning a reed bed may hold birds resting, preening and singing, with others coming and going.**
- **Sexes:** "A striking feature of Redwing social organization during the fall and winter is the segregation of the sexes which, though by no means complete, is very pronounced" [Orians 1961, pp. 289-290]; "Males revert to flocking behavior as soon as they quit their territories" [Nero 1956, p. 16]. East of the Cascades "They migrate in sex flocks" [Gabrielson & Jewett 1940, p. 524]. The valley's resident birds are often in mixed groups (section 3.3).
- **Mixed flocks:** "immense flocks of redwings mixed with grackles, cowbirds, and starlings feeding in the open fields" in late summer and early fall [Bent 1958, pp. 138-139]; at a California roost "Redwings and Tricolors were by far the most numerous but there were also many Brewer Blackbirds ..., Cowbirds ..., Yellow-heads and Starlings" [Orians 1961, p. 289].
- **Flush and drop back:** in a New York marsh during the moult "he may hear a rush of wings ahead of him as a flock of birds breaks from cover and drops again into the flags a short distance beyond" [Allen 1914, pp. 105-106].

### 7.3 Song in autumn

- **The sources disagree, by region.** In Connecticut and New York the song "terminates in late July or early August ... Ordinarily red-wings do not sing at all in the fall, but once, October 31, 1937, I found a small flock of males, several of them singing" [Saunders in Bent 1958, p. 142]. "It is given by the males alone, from first arrival until the middle of July, and again, to some extent, in the fall" [Allen 1914, p. 89]; "In the fall, singing is transiently renewed between October 14 and 17" [Allen 1914, p. 88]. In California in autumn the birds in the cattails "rested, preened, sang, and bathed" [Orians 1961, p. 289]; roosting birds "begin a chorus that may continue in part through much of the night" [Meanley 1965, p. 225].
- **The valley's photos, late September:** males singing or in song spread on **five days**: a male in song spread among about six males in a sunflower field (record 39, 23 September 2023), singing on a wire (69, 24 September 2025), singing repeatedly on an oak sapling (86, eight photos, 3 October 2024), singing on a roof vent (77, 6 October 2025), and in full song spread beside a first-year male (101, 24 September 2026). Five of 68 male records (7%).
- **xeno-canto, September and October, Oregon, Washington, British Columbia:** five recordings in September (two from Washington, three from British Columbia), none in October, none from Oregon; two are of song:
  - **XC159672** (Ian Cruickshank, Rocky Point, Metchosin, Vancouver Island, 24 September 2012, 07:33, 270 s; *caurinus* country): a male sings **13 songs, at 15.7, 31.6, 63.3, 81.9, 92.3, 117.9, 143.0, 175.6, 186.5, 210.6, 231.9, 252.8 and 269.5 s**: one every **21 s** on average (10-32 s), **2.9 a minute** (Computed, read off the full sonogram). Between them, check calls almost every second: "quieter, higher check calls from adult female, louder check calls from adult male" (recordist's note).
  - **XC159674** (the same place, 07:40, 297 s): **12 songs** (2.4 a minute), a gap of 80 s filled with descending whistles, then song again with a flock's chatter behind it (Computed, sonogram).
  - XC676902 (Barry Edmonston, Swan Lake, Victoria, 25 September 2021, 18:00, 79 s): "Calls from single individual in tall tree ... with four other individuals calling from another tree about 20 meters distance": loud calls every 3-8 s (sonogram).
  - XC436852 (Bruce Lagerquist, Gloyd Seeps, Grant County, Washington, 18 September 2018, 18:30; *nevadensis* country): "Large flock of birds, 100+, dispersing for the day from their roost"; flight calls. XC1040466 (Greg Irving, South Jameson Lake, Douglas County, Washington, 11 September 2025; *nevadensis*) is calls.
- **For scale, the breeding season:** "Approximately six vocalizations per minute were given by male Redwings on their territories ... the average duration of a song is slightly less than 1.5 seconds" [Orians 1961, p. 304]; "males sing short, stereotyped, 'conc-a-ree' songs coupled with a visual display 1-9 times per minute" [Smith & Norman 1979, p. 83]; about 2 songs a minute in April falling to 1 in May, in bouts [Peek 1971, p. 386].
- **So:** a Pacific Northwest male in late September may sing in morning bouts at about the breeding rate (2-3 songs a minute, each about 1.5 s); most males in a group don't. **One singer in the cast, in bouts**, is the honest reading; no source gives how long the bouts last or how often they come (Estimate in section 11).

### 7.4 Calls

- "The call notes can be put into two groups, the check notes and the whistles ... The first is the flight note, which may be represented by the word chuck. This is the note which first attracts our attention to a flock flying over. The second is a scolding note ... check ... given whenever one intrudes"; a whistle "is the alarm note; it is given when the nest is approached, and arouses all of the other birds within hearing" [Allen 1914, pp. 89-90].
- "The common call of the red-wing, usually written chack, often sounds to me more like tsack. An alarm note ... is a downward slurred peeah" [Saunders in Bent 1958, p. 141]; "both sexes, adult or young, when excited utter a sharp chack" [Grinnell & Storer 1924, p. 400].
- "The typical call ... is a distinctive, matter-of-fact check ... all year round, in flight and while feeding ... a fast, scolding chak chak chak" [AAB-Sounds]; females' calls and songs are "a series of three to five short chit or check notes" [AAB-Sounds]; a female song of "a rapid series of relatively brief, sharp song components, each sounding like 'chit'" [Hovekamp 1996, p. 378].
- **Rates:** none published outside the breeding season. In XC159672 a male and a female together give check calls about every 1-2 s while he sings (Computed, sonogram); in XC676902 one bird calls every 3-8 s. **Estimate** for a settled bird: a check every 10-40 s; every 1-5 s when alarmed, arriving, or near a singing male.

### 7.5 In the reeds

- **Activities:** resting, preening, singing, bathing [Orians 1961, p. 289]; in the valley's photos perching still and fluffed, preening, stretching, feeding at cattail heads (section 3.3). No autumn time budget exists; on a breeding territory males perched 73% of the time, "most of their time singing and the rest preening or resting quietly ... little time was spent in flight" [Peek 1971, p. 385].
- **Gaping:** "Regular gaping movements are also used in cattails and in grassland, the bill being inserted into the vegetation and then gaped to expose any insects and seeds within" [Orians 1961, p. 295]; red-wings have been seen "splitting open dead ragweed stalks in the winter, thereby exposing pupae" [p. 295]. In October one held a samara "against the twig on which it perched and in this way evidently detached the wing" [F. H. Allen in Bent 1958, p. 137]. Autumn food is mostly seeds: "The consumption of weed seeds amounts to 97 percent in November" [Bent 1958, p. 136]; "In fall and winter they eat weedy seeds ... as well as native sunflowers and waste grains" [AAB-LH].
- **Moving about:** "On the ground the redwing walks deliberately, or runs, or hops rapidly when trying to keep up with a feeding flock" [Bent 1958, p. 138]; among stems they clamber up and down a stalk and hop or flutter to the next (by eye, from the photos' varied heights on one stem cluster; no source counts it).
- **Bill-up:** record 106 shows a male with the bill pointed up on a pine top, a posture of the display repertoire; the companion document covers displays.

### 7.6 Flight

- "In the air the flight of the redwing is characteristic; it flies with bursts of rapid wingbeats, between which are slight intermittent pauses, producing a somewhat wavy motion. The flocks are in orderly formation, wheeling and turning in unison" [Bent 1958, p. 139]; "Direct Flight, Undulating" [Audubon].
- **Speed:** **10.02 ± 0.82 m/s** at midday (n = 5) and **11.30 ± 0.75 m/s** in the evening (n = 10), Doppler radar, birds "not in a state of alarm" 10-60 m from the observer and under 20 m up [Evans & Drickamer 1994, p. 155].
- **Height:** at dusk "the last birds flying toward the roost ... just skim over the top of the marsh vegetation" [Meanley 1965, p. 224].
- **Wingbeat:** no measured value found. Pennycuick's cruising formula [Pennycuick 1996; exponents as widely quoted, not checked against the paper], with 65 g, a 0.37 m span and about 0.021 m² of wing (Estimate), gives **about 10 Hz** for the male and 11 for the female (Computed). The block spec's 7 Hz is low.

### 7.7 Tolerance of a still person

- **No flight-initiation distance for this species** in Blumstein 2006 (150 species), Møller et al. 2014 (193 species) or Livezey et al. 2016's 650-species database. Related birds approached on foot at about 0.5 m/s: Song Sparrow 8.2 ± 3.9 m, White-crowned Sparrow 8.6 ± 5.3, Common Starling 13.6 ± 9.0, American Robin 11.2 ± 7.4 [Blumstein 2006, pp. 398-399]; Brown-headed Cowbird about 8.4 m [Møller et al. 2014, File S1, Computed from log10 FID 0.924]. Incubating females flushed from the nest at 19.8 m with the male present and 10.4 m without (abstract only) [Burton & Yasukawa 2001].
- Out of the nesting season territorial males "seem much less concerned with human beings on territory" [Peek 1971, p. 392].
- The valley's photos are mostly from urban park wetlands (Fernhill, the Beaverton greenways, Bethany ponds, Eugene's parks), many of them close, sharp portraits of birds at ease (section 3.3).
- **So:** a person standing still is tolerated at a few metres by park birds, and the player never walks toward them. **Birds perching from 5 m out are honest; the 3.6 m nearest stems at the edge** (Estimate). A bird that does come within 5 m should stay briefly and call.

The catalog's call timing for `rewbla` (`every: [8, 25]`, `prefer: 'song'` in `src/audio/calls.ts`) is about the breeding rate for one bird: it should become check calls from the group and songs only from the singer in bouts.

## 12. Open questions and confidence

- **Measurements: high** for wing, tail, tarsus, toes and the museum culmen (Ridgway and van Rossem, sexed *caurinus*, read from page images). **Medium** for the exposed culmen (20 / 17 mm), which no source gives: it rests on AVONET's nostril-to-tip plus an estimated nostril, and on two photos scaled by the wing chord. **Low-medium** for the bill's width at the base (an Estimate; AVONET gives it only at the nostril) and for the claws and hind toe.
- **Masses: medium.** Sexed values only from Grinnell & Storer (*nevadensis*) and Searcy (eastern Washington males); Dunning's sexed values were not read.
- **Sex difference and the female's size: high** (two independent tables agree on 0.84 and 0.91).
- **Plumage and versions: high** for the sexes and the fresh basic plumage; **medium** for the first-year male's frequency (4-5 of 35 aged males in photos biased toward adults). Pyle 1997 was not read; the moult and ageing rest on Dwight, Ridgway, Meanley & Bond, Linz et al., Selander & Giller and van Rossem.
- **Sex mix: medium-low.** 74 males and 75 females per observer-day, but one flock of 35 females makes half of that; males are surely over-photographed.
- **Field marks: high.** The pixel reasoning is computed from the optics; feature sizes are photo-measured on a few birds.
- **Posture: medium.** Three side-on photos give the lengths and angles (205-206 mm for two males agree well); the profile male is only 800 px.
- **Autumn song: medium.** The valley's photos and two Vancouver Island recordings show it; eastern sources say it is unusual; no source gives bout lengths or how often males sing in autumn.
- **Call rates, perch bouts, moves, idles: low** (Estimates; no time budget or call rate exists outside the breeding season).
- **Flight speed: medium** (one radar study, Illinois, May). **Wingbeat: low-medium** (Computed, formula exponents not checked).
- **Tolerance: low.** No flight-initiation distance exists for the species; related species and the urban valley records stand in.
- **Snippet-only or abstract-only claims:** Burton & Yasukawa 2001; Muma & Weatherhead 1989; Payne 1969 (only through Greenwood et al. 1983, Linz et al. 1983 and Johnsen et al. 1996); Orians & Christman 1968 (only through Peek 1971).

### 12.1 What should change the design

1. **Per-version size.** The female is 0.85 of the male (tarsus 0.91). `variants` are paint-only and every version shares one profile and one `length`. The plan needs a per-version `scale` with measurement overrides (or a second trace for the female); without it the female is either a giant or the male a dwarf, a 30-pixel difference at 10 m.
2. **The epaulet is hidden at rest.** In late September what shows on a resting male is the buff bar; the red appears when he sings, displays, flies, or shifts his wing. Painting a fixed red patch on the folded wing would be wrong most of the time; the red must be covered and uncovered by pose (the companion document's mechanics), and the atlas must paint the red under the covering feathers, not over them.
3. **The guide's marks** call the shoulder "red-and-yellow"; the valley's *caurinus* has a deep buff to ochraceous border, and the field mark at rest is that buff bar. The block bird's female colours (pale grey-brown) are too pale for a *caurinus* female.
4. **A third version, `firstYearMale`,** paint only, and **a cast of five** rather than one bird at a point; the existing `reed-perch` point becomes the singer's stem.
5. **Calls:** the group's sound is check calls; song comes from one male in bouts. The catalog's song recordings are from Port Townsend in February and Olympia in April; XC159672 and XC159674 (September *caurinus* song with check calls) are better matches but are **CC BY-NC-ND 3.0**, which may not allow trimming; XC676902 (September calls) is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.
6. **Distance:** the bed's nearest stems are 3.6 m from the player; nothing gives a flight distance, so keep birds from about 5 m out unless the plan accepts the edge.
7. **Moult:** a late-September bird may have short central tail feathers or a scrappy head [Meanley & Bond 1970, p. 27]. Not worth a version; a per-bird tail shortened 10-20% on one bird would be honest and cheap if the builder allows it.
