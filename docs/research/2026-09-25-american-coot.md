# American Coot: measurements, late-September age classes, field marks, bill and frontal shield, floating posture, and feeding on open water

Date: 2026-09-25
Purpose: source data for the detailed American Coot (`y00475`) model and the `coot` swim style: the swimming posture (waterline at y = 0), the bill with its own width profile and a frontal shield, lobed feet, and species-true foraging on `swim`. The player watches six coots on `swim` in the `near-water` zone, 10-23 m from the vantage, on Willow Lake, Willamette Valley, Oregon, on a late-September morning. Binoculars are 7.5° wide and the telescope 2° (`src/player/optics.ts`).

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt or an abstract of a paywalled paper; I could not read the paper, so treat it as unverified. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo or video frame and scaled it by a published bill measurement (stated each time). "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated.

**Scale convention for all photo measurements.** The coot's culmen is ambiguous because the shield changes size with season and state. I scaled every photo by the **bill proper**, Ridgway & Friedmann's "culmen from frontal shield", **31.8 mm** (tip to where the straight culmen meets the rising shield), and checked it against the culmen from the nostril, **14.2 mm** [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 214]. On five photos the nostril check came out 13.4-15.5 mm, within Ridgway's 13-16 range (sections 2.6 and 5.5). Scaling by the culmen *including* the shield would have made the scale 25-40% wrong on birds with small autumn shields.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements, mean of the sexes** [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, p. 50, Table 7; about 660 females and 795 males collected September to June]: total length 369 mm, wing chord 183, tail 51.7, tarsus 55.6, middle toe (no claw) 74.0, culmen including the shield 45.6, bill height 10.7 and width 11.1 at the feathering, head length (rear of skull to bill tip) 63.3. Males are 4-9% larger in each linear measure (computed). Mass about 640 g (Dunning, via [Tobias et al. 2022]); "600-700 g" [AAB-ID].
- **Bill and shield:**
  - The bill proper is 30-34 (31.8) mm; with the shield it measures 41-54 (46.1) mm in males and 33.5-51 (42.3) in females [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 214]. The bill is "moderately compressed" [p. 205]: 11.1 mm wide at the feathering [Eddleman & Knopf 1985] but only 5.2 mm wide at the nostril against a depth of 9.7 mm [Tobias et al. 2022, 5 skins, computed].
  - Photo-measured (two birds) the bill proper is **15.3-15.8 mm deep at its base**, 10.5 mm at mid-length and 12.6 mm a quarter of the way from the base. The dark subterminal band sits about 2-10 mm from the tip.
  - Shield: "The flat shield of non-breeding coots ... is about 2.1 mm. thick, 4 to 8 mm. wide, and 4 to 10 mm. long"; in breeding birds shield and callus reach "over 3.6 mm. thick, as much as 14 mm. wide, and up to 17 mm. long" [Gullion 1951, p. 158].
  - On four autumn and winter adults the shield rises **3-11 mm** above the culmen junction along the forehead, and its top lies **11-15 mm ahead of the eye centre and 2-8 mm above it**. The red-brown callus was **4 × 5 mm on one October bird** and only a speck (about 1.5 mm) or absent on the others (photo-measured).
- **Age: model an adult and a first-autumn juvenile, four adults and two juveniles.**
  - Of 127 ageable birds in 133 Willamette iNaturalist records from 11 September to 12 October, **96 are adults, 28 recognisably young (20 clearly juvenile, 8 near-adult) and 3 half-grown**: **22-24% young** (appendix).
  - Only 14 of the 28 young calls are confident, so the floor is 11%.
  - There is no clear trend within the window. By November only 1 of 17 ageable birds still looks young.
  - Collected samples on migration lakes held 41% (September) and 52% (October) juveniles [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, Table 3, computed]. Iris and head colour aged every September juvenile in that sample.
  - Young birds are the adults' size (culmen-shield 2.5 mm shorter) [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, p. 47]. They show a grey or grey-brown head with a pale, mottled throat and foreneck, a grey or greyish-ivory bill with a faint or no ring, a small flat shield without a callus, and a brown to brownish-red iris [Gullion 1954, pp. 394-396; Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213; photos].
- **Floating** (photo-measured on two side-on October birds, heights above the waterline): back **35-41 mm**, tail tip **9-18 mm**, bill tip **72-86 mm**, crown **108-132 mm**. The waterline runs about **250-270 mm** from bow to stern.
  - Buoyancy gives a draft of about **42 mm (39-44)** (computed from a 640 g bird and an assumed hull; section 6), so **about half the body depth (0.50-0.55)** is under water.
  - "It is a strong rapid swimmer, floating higher in the water than the ducks or the gallinules, with the back more level, less submerged forward" [Bent 1926, p. 363].
- **Head pump**: about **2.2 pumps a second** (period 0.42-0.50 s) on two cruising birds. The bill tip travels about **5 cm fore and aft** relative to the body (about 17% of the bill-to-tail length) and 2-3 cm up and down (**Estimate**, video). The pump runs only while the bird swims.
  - "Head-bobbing of the coot is not necessarily in unison with the movements of the feet. When the bird is feeding on plant material this head movement is fairly slow, but it is quickened when insects are being hunted on the water's surface" [Gullion 1952, p. 84].
  - A cruising, feeding coot moved at about 0.2 m/s (**Estimate**); a travelling flock passed a dock at "approximate speed of 0.5 m/s" [Trenchard 2012, p. 11].
- **Feeding on open water:**
  - Surface pecking in bouts of **2-3 pecks a second** (**Estimate**, video).
  - Head-dips of about **1 s** with the head and neck under and the body level (video).
  - Occasional tip-ups (1.5 s on one video) and short dives. American Coot dives averaged **1.3-1.5 s** in water 25-45 cm deep and **2.5-3.5 s** at 52-74 cm, with 12.9 and 24.6 s pauses between dives [Batulis & Bongiorno 1972, p. 666]; videos gave 1.7-5.5 s.
  - The dive usually starts with a **leap**: "at other times it leaps clear of the water like a grebe, with its wings pressed close to its sides, its body describing an arc, and the head entering as the feet leave the water" [Bent 1926, p. 364]. This is when the lobed feet show.
  - In autumn diving is uncommon: "Coots rarely dived while foraging from October through mid-December" [LeSchack & Hepp 1995, p. 430]. Feeding took ">62%" of wintering coots' day [McKnight 1998] (Snippet).
- **Tracing references** (scratchpad, section 8):
  - Adults: `amecoo-side-oct-reago.jpg` (CC BY 2.0, 14 October, 0.248 mm/px) for the floating profile and waterline, and `amecoo-side-adult-wildreturn.jpg` (CC BY 2.0, 6000 px, 0.109 mm/px) for the bill.
  - Juvenile: `amecoo-juv-local-williams.jpg` (CC BY-NC, Benton County, 7 October) and `amecoo-juv-local-haight.jpg` (CC0, Lane County, 17 September).
  - Feet: `amecoo-feet-hansen.jpg` (CC0), `amecoo-feet-walking-local-patricia.jpg` (CC BY-NC, Beaverton, with the bill for scale), `amecoo-feet-walking-cook.jpg` (CC BY 2.0) and the harvested guide photo.

Genus statements that set proportions [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, pp. 205-207]:
- **Bill**: "Bill about as long as or slightly shorter than head, moderately compressed, its width at posterior end of nostril equal to about two-thirds its depth at same point, the latter equal to decidedly less than half the length of commissure; gonys very decidedly shorter than mandibular rami, straight or very slightly convex, rather strongly ascending terminally ... culmen nearly straight (sometimes faintly concave or depressed) for basal half (approximately), gently but very distinctly decurved distally, rounded, or at least not distinctly ridged".
- **Shield**: "frontal shield (in adults) extending backward as far as middle of eye (sometimes as far as posterior angle of eye), more or less tumid (sometimes much swollen and strongly convex both longitudinally and transversely), longer than broad (sometimes only slightly so, however), its posterior extremity either convex or contracted into an obtuse angle, its surface either smooth or slightly corrugated".
- **Nostril**: "nasal fossa extending for at least half the length of maxilla (from loral feathering) ... nostril nearly midway between loral feathering and tip of maxilla, in lower-anterior portion of nasal fossa, longitudinal ... very narrowly, almost linear".
- **Wing and tail**: "Wing relatively short, very concave beneath ... Tail a little more than one-fourth as long as wing, rounded, the rectrices broad and moderately firm, 12-14 in number."
- **Leg and foot**: "Tarsus less than one-third as long as wing, about as long as inner toe, without claw (or shorter) ... lateral membranes of toes very broad, the segments conspicuously convex, their lateral lobes with transverse scutella similar to those on top of toes; claw of outer toe sometimes reaching to base of middle claw, but usually falling short of the latter; inner toe decidedly shorter than outer; hallux (without claw) about as long as basal phalanx of outer toe; claws moderate in size, very slightly curved".
- **Key to the races** [p. 207]: *F. a. americana*: "Frontal shield dark chestnut to reddish brown; not wrinkled ... Under tail coverts white ... Frontal shield smaller, not over 13 mm. in length." The Caribbean races have a shield "over 14 mm. in length" and a bill "16-18 mm. deep at base" (*grenadensis*) or "less than 15 mm. deep at base" (*columbiana*). Gullion reads this "frontal shield" length as the callus length [Gullion 1951, p. 164].

### 2.4 The frontal shield and callus in the literature

- **Anatomy**: "The frontal shield of the American Coot ... is a fleshy protuberance extending dorsocaudad onto the forehead from the upper mandible." The reddish part is a separate horny layer: "The reddish portion, or callus, is horny or corneous in texture and is distinctly an accessory to the shield proper, the latter being white and continuous with the rhinotheca of the maxilla in the American Coot" [Gullion 1951, p. 157]. The callus is "about 0.05 mm. thick" [p. 157]: a coloured skin, not a lump. The lump is the swollen shield under it.
- **Size** [Gullion 1951, p. 158]: "The flat shield of non-breeding coots (Fig. 1A) is about 2.1 mm. thick, 4 to 8 mm. wide, and 4 to 10 mm. long. In breeding coots the swollen shield and callus (Fig. 1E) may be over 3.6 mm. thick, as much as 14 mm. wide, and up to 17 mm. long." Callus sizes reached "12 X 15 mm." and "14 x 17 mm." in two testosterone-implanted males, and "12 X 15 mm." in one control male [p. 164].
- **Shape states** [Gullion 1951, p. 160]: "I considered a shield flat when it was concave, semi-swollen when it was smooth, and swollen when a convexity was apparent."
- **Season**:
  - The shield grows with territorial behaviour, and loss of territory brings "eventual regression to the flat shield characteristic of immatures in mid-winter" [p. 160].
  - But "birds permanently paired and defending territory throughout the year, whether resident or migrant, retain the enlarged shield as long as they remain paired and on territory" [p. 158]. At Lake Merritt, Oakland, Gullion found "swollen frontal shields on a large proportion of these coots in the fall" [p. 157].
  - "certain very old (to eleven years) banded migrant birds develop and retain through the winter knobbed, much enlarged shields" [p. 164].
  - Breeding females' shields "are as large as those of males" [p. 164].
- **Variation**:
  - "I have recognized fifteen distinct callus patterns among the 130 coots handled ... no two birds have identically the same callus-shape" [Gullion 1951, p. 163].
  - "At least four American Coots have been handled that had very rudimentary calli. One, in fact, had only a reddish spot on an otherwise white frontal shield" [p. 164].
- **Immatures**: "The frontal shield on immature coots is characteristically flat" [Gullion 1954, p. 396]. The juvenal "frontal shield very small" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213]. At 70 days the "Frontal shield flesh colored"; at 75 days "frontal shield reddish" [Gullion 1954, p. 394, Table 3].
- **Display**: coots in aggressive display "erect the neck feathers behind the shield, forming a black background which further emphasizes the shield-size" [Gullion 1951, p. 163].

- **Bill profile.** The culmen is straight and the bill's depth falls almost linearly from about 15.5 mm at the base to about 10.5 mm at mid-length. It then drops faster to a blunt, slightly decurved point. The gonys angle, where the lower edge turns up, lies in the distal third (photos; "gonys ... rather strongly ascending terminally" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 205]).
- **Bill axis.** In side view the culmen runs at 48-50° to the gonys-line's 20-24°. The mid-line (bisector) is used for "perpendicular depth" above (W, C).
- **Shield in autumn.** None of the three October-November birds had a swollen shield, and only one had a visible callus. Culmen including the shield ran 34.7-40.8 mm against Eddleman & Knopf's 45.6 (all seasons, measured with calipers to the shield's rear end). A late-September adult should carry a small to medium, smooth shield with a small callus.
- **Photo scale uncertainty.** The bill proper varies 30-34 mm (±6%) between birds, and the nostril checks agree within 6%.

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

**Growth and the juvenile's appearance** [Gullion 1954, pp. 391-396, Table 3], from 43 marked young in the San Francisco Bay area:
- At about 25 days "the breast becomes whitish, contrasting sharply with the otherwise very dark plumage of the young bird" [p. 391].
- 31-55 days: "The light breast is soon replaced by a medium-gray breast, the white undertail coverts appear, and the top of the head develops a dark haloed appearance. At the end of this period, the bird has its full juvenile plumage and has assumed the body proportions of the adult" [p. 395].
- "At about 60 days the first dark adult plumage appears as two black spots laterally and just above the waterline on the breast. This dark plumage rapidly spreads over the entire body ... the immature coot flies at about 75 days of age" [p. 395].
- At 70 days: "Top of head dark gray, back gray-brown, rump reddish-brown, throat medium gray ... Bill light, mottled ivory distal to nares, base smooth medium gray. Frontal shield flesh colored; iris grayish on perimeter becoming reddish-brown towards pupil; legs gray-green" [p. 394, Table 3].
- At 75 days: "bill uniform grayish-ivory, iris reddish, frontal shield reddish". At 80 days: "Immature resembles adults in all characters except for grayish bills and gray-green feet and legs". At 120 days: "Bill begins to look adult; from this time on immatures can be separated from adults only on basis of leg color" [p. 394, Table 3].
- "At about four months of age the bill of the immature resembles that of the adult sufficiently that age identification cannot always be certain, though some immatures retain a mottled bill in excess of five months" [p. 396].
- "An American Coot that is ten or eleven years old will have reddish-orange legs, but otherwise the plumage is much the same as in a five-months-old immature coot" [p. 396].

**The juvenal plumage in words**:
- "Juvenal (sexes alike).—Entire top and sides of head, hind neck, and entire upper parts of body varying from pale ashy olive-brown to chaetura drab ... entire under parts much lighter than in adult—pale olive-buff with ashy light neutral gray bases to the feathers showing through as an undertone; sides of head, neck, and body, the flanks and vent washed with ashy pale olive-brown; under tail coverts white as in adult; frontal shield very small; color of unfeathered areas as in adult, except that bill lacks the subterminal band" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213].
- "In this full juvenal plumage the upper parts vary from 'hair brown' to 'chaetura drab' more brownish on the back; the chin, throat and neck are 'deep neutral gray,' mottled with grayish white; and the under parts are mottled with neutral grays and whitish. During the fall and winter a gradual molt of the contour plumage produces steady progress towards maturity; but traces of immaturity persist all through the first year; young birds have much more white in the under parts, chin, throat, and belly, and they have not yet developed the white bill and frontal shield" [Bent 1926, p. 362].
- "Juveniles are pale grayish below with a pale bill" [AAB-ID, gallery caption, a July juvenile]; "Juveniles appear paler with greenish-gray legs" [BirdWeb]; "Immature paler" [Audubon].

**How long young birds stay recognisable** [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, pp. 44-46]:
- Juvenile traits [Table 2]:
  - Iris: "Shades of brown, ferruginous (41), or browns with flecks of scarlet".
  - Bill: "Pearl gray (81), olive gray (42), light neutral gray (85) ... grayish horn color (91)".
  - Head plumage: "Blackish neutral gray (82), dark neutral gray (83), medium neutral gray (84) ... often with white feathers intermixed".
- Adult traits [Table 2]: iris "geranium (12), scarlet (14), flame scarlet (15) ... or intermediate red colors"; bill "White, white with tip pale neutral gray (86) or pearl gray (81)"; head "Jet black (89)".
- September: iris colour aged 16 of 16 juveniles and 13 of 13 adults, head colour 17 of 17 juveniles, and bill colour 17 of 17 juveniles [Table 3].
- "Juveniles began to acquire adult bill color in October, when only 74% of juveniles retained juvenile bill color ... Color of the irides and head plumage remained effective for aging coots through October for 94% of the birds examined. By the end of November 20% of all juveniles had attained adult appearance in a least 1 of the 4 age criteria ... All juveniles we examined had attained adult soft part and plumage colors by 1 January" [p. 46].
- Their collected samples were 17 juveniles and 24 adults in September, and 200 juveniles and 185 adults in October [Table 3]: **41% and 52% young** (computed). These were birds shot on migration lakes in Oklahoma, Texas and South Carolina, not a census.

**Adults in autumn**:
- **Moult**:
  - "Adults have a complete molt in August and September" [Bent 1926, p. 362].
  - Captive birds dropped all their flight feathers nearly at once in late July and early August and regrew the primaries in "a little over a month". "During the period of wing molt coots become extremely shy ... They are disinclined to fly for a period of about four weeks" [Gullion 1953a, pp. 102-103].
  - So a late-September adult is in fresh plumage.
- **Autumn grey bills**: "On several captive coots it was noted that the normally ivory-colored bill took on a decidedly grayish coloration either during the molt period or in late summer prior to the commencement of molting. By September 10 five of the seven captive coots had developed decidedly grayish bills, and this same grayish bill character has been observed on birds in the field. This coloration often makes it difficult to distinguish between adults and early season birds-of-the-year during the fall season" [Gullion 1953a, p. 103]. Bill colour was unreliable for ageing in October because of "fading of bill color in post-breeding adults" [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, p. 53].
- **Leg colour**: "by late September, the tarsal color of all adults had faded from the vivid colors typically found during the breeding season ... By late September, tarsal colors of age-classes 0 and 1 were indistinguishable" [Crawford 1978, p. 538].
- **Winter plumage**: "Similar to the above, but with the frontal shield smaller, the back more extensively washed with dull olive brown; the under parts paler, the whitish tips to the feathers being broader" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213].

**Local status**:
- Coots are "permanent residents in the lowlands" of Washington, and "Large congregations form August through December on bodies of water" [BirdWeb].
- A 2026 flock study was made "across numerous sites in the Willamette Valley of Oregon" [Smith & Hopkins 2026] (abstract only).
- Breeding runs late: the iNaturalist sample below includes three half-grown young on 14 and 20 September.

**Timing arithmetic for Willow Lake** (computed; hatch dates are an **Estimate**, because the Willamette breeding calendar was not read): a chick hatched on 1 June is 116 days old on 25 September; one hatched on 1 July is 86 days; one from a late or second brood ("1-2 broods" [AAB-LH]) hatched on 15 July is 72 days. So late-September young range from Gullion's 70-day bird (grey head, mottled ivory-grey bill, brownish iris) to near-adult birds with only a greyer head, a greyish bill and a dull eye.

- "Juvenile" means clearly young: a grey or grey-brown head no darker than the body, pale mottling on the throat and foreneck, and a dull grey or grey-horn bill with no white and no ring, often with a flat pale shield. "Near-adult juvenile" means the plumage is nearly adult and only one or two traits remain, usually a sooty-grey rather than jet-black head with fine pale speckling.
- **Confidence of the young calls**: 8 high, 6 moderate, 14 low.
  - 9 of the 28 rest on one trait. Four of them are in one distant, soft flock photo (17195492), called by head colour.
  - Counting only the high and moderate calls, young are **11%** (14 of 127).
  - The adults are also partly low-confidence: many distant birds were called adult on a black head and white bill alone, so near-adults may hide among them.
- **Within the window**, split another way: 11-30 September 18% young (14 of 77), 1-12 October 28% (14 of 50). The numbers are small and the difference is not reliable.
  - What does change is the kind of young bird. Near-adult juveniles go from 1 before 26 September to 7 after it, and the half-grown young (3) are all before 21 September.
- 72 records show at least one adult and 27 at least one young bird; 38 hold no ageable bird.
- **Life-stage annotations** are nearly absent: 1 record in the window (316921874, "Adult"). All dates in the box carry 111 "Adult" and 24 "Juvenile" annotations; July-August has 9 and 15, November 3 and 0.

- Most unageable birds are in distant or backlit flocks (one Clark County raft alone holds about 75).

- **Comparison, 15 July-31 August** (25 random records): 12 adults, 9 juveniles, 1 near-adult and 5 downy or half-grown young. In midsummer young make up **56%** of ageable birds (37% without the downy young). Downy young appear from 30 July to 28 August, and one adult sits on a nest on 30 July.
- **Comparison, November** (20 random records): 16 adults and 1 near-adult juvenile (low confidence): **6%** young. This matches Eddleman & Knopf's finding that juveniles take on adult colours through November and December [p. 46].
- **Bias.** iNaturalist photographers favour close, single, striking birds, and adults, with their white bills and red eyes, are the striking ones. Young birds keep to cover and may be under-photographed. The 24% is therefore a floor, and Eddleman & Knopf's collected 41-52% may be a ceiling if naive young are easier to shoot.

### 3.3 Recommendation

**Model two plumages: the adult and a first-autumn juvenile. For six birds, four adults and two juveniles.**
- One young bird in three sits between the photo tally (22-24%, 11% on confident calls only, a floor) and the collected samples (41-52%).
- A single juvenile is too easily missed; two let the player compare them in one field of view.
- If only the clear, grey-billed juvenile type counts, the photos give 16% (20 of 127), and **five adults and one juvenile** is the conservative alternative.
- The young share falls to about 6% by November, so the late-September date matters: it is the last month in which young coots are common and still obvious.
- The juvenile to model is Gullion's **80-100-day bird**:
  - Grey (not black) head and neck with a pale throat and foreneck.
  - Paler, browner grey body, the same undertail as the adult.
  - A greyish-ivory to grey bill, without the white of the adult, the ring faint or absent.
  - A flat, small, pale shield with no callus.
  - A brown to brownish-red eye and grey-green legs.
- The half-grown downy young (three records, all from Forest Grove) are too rare to model.
- Size: the juvenile uses the adult's measurements, with the culmen-shield 2.5 mm shorter [Eddleman & Knopf 1985].

## 4. Field marks of each recommended plumage

Colour words come from the sources; I have not invented hex values. "(photo)" marks my own observation on the reference photos of section 8.

### 4.1 Adult (sexes alike), late September

- **Head and neck**: "Entire head and neck dark slate-black to black with a slight gloss that shows very faint dark bluish or greenish reflections at times" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213]; "Charcoal gray with blacker head" [Audubon]; jet black (Smithe 89) [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, p. 44].
  - Where the black ends (photo, R1, R2): the black hood runs down the neck to the base of the neck. On the foreneck it meets the slate breast **20-35 mm above the waterline**, a soft change over about 10-15 mm. On the hindneck it fades into the slate mantle where the neck enters the back.
  - At 10-23 m this black-against-slate contrast and the white bill are the adult's strongest marks (Estimate).
- **Body**: "entire upperparts of body and the upper wing coverts slate, sometimes with a faint olive-brownish wash on the lower back ... entire underparts (other than neck) ashy neutral gray washed with slate, especially on the breast, sides, and flanks; the tips of the feathers of the lower breast and of the mid-abdomen whitish" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213].
  - In fresh autumn plumage the flank and side feathers show pale grey tips in overlapping scallops, and the lower flank above the waterline reads paler grey than the back (photo, R1).
  - "Coots are dark-gray to black birds with a bright-white bill and forehead" [AAB-ID].
- **Wing**: "bend of the wing with some white; inner secondaries like the back, outer secondaries and primaries dull clove brownish with a slate cast, the outer secondaries broadly tipped with white; the outer web of the outermost primary partly white" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213]. "The white tips of the secondaries show up well in flight as a good field mark" [Bent 1926, p. 363]. At rest the folded wing is covered by the scapulars and flank feathers, the primaries are hidden, and the white trailing edge does not show (photo, R1, R2).
- **Tail and undertail**: "rectrices dark slate-black ... under tail coverts white" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213].
  - The white undertail coverts show as a **white wedge on each side under the tail**. On R1 the patch is about **30 mm long and 12 mm tall**, its top about 14 mm above the water, and it tapers to a point under the tail tip (photo).
  - In the normal posture "the tail is held horizontally with the under tail coverts inconspicuous", showing "only a small patch of white". The alarm posture lifts the tail "exhibiting the white under tail coverts but not expanding them"; displays expand them [Gullion 1952, pp. 83-84, 89].
- **Bill**: "bill white or pale horn, banded subterminally with maroon or dark reddish brown" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213]; "a white bill tipped in a ring of black" [AAB-ID gallery].
  - The band lies **about 2-10 mm from the tip**, 3-7 mm wide, clearest on the lower mandible and the lower edge of the upper (photo).
  - The tip beyond the band is white to greyish.
  - Some autumn adults have "decidedly grayish" bills [Gullion 1953a, p. 103].
- **Shield**: white, continuous with the bill; "With good views note the red eye and the white shield that is topped with red" [AAB-ID gallery]; "At close range you may see a small patch of red on the forehead" [AAB-ID].
  - The callus is "dark reddish brown or chestnut" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213], "Frontal shield dark chestnut to reddish brown; not wrinkled" [p. 207].
  - On R1 it is a dark reddish-brown cap about 4 × 5 mm on top of the shield, sitting against the black forehead feathers, so at range it reads as a dark notch at the shield's top (photo).
- **Iris**: "red to reddish brown" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213]; geranium to flame scarlet [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, p. 44]. It is bright red on every sharp local adult (appendix). Visible eye about 5-6 mm across (section 2.6).
- **Legs and feet**: "tarsi and toes greenish, claws black" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213]; "The legs are yellow-green" [AAB-ID].
  - In the breeding season the colour runs by age from green (yearlings) through yellow-green and yellow to yellow-orange and red-orange (4+ years) [Crawford 1978, pp. 538-539]. It fades by late September [p. 538].
  - On the foot photos the tarsus is yellow to yellow-green, the toes olive-yellow along their tops, and the lobes pale blue-grey with dark scale lines (section 5.5).

### 4.2 First-autumn juvenile, late September

- **Head and neck**: grey to grey-brown, **no darker than the body** or only slightly so. The crown is darker ("the top of the head develops a dark haloed appearance" [Gullion 1954, p. 395]) and the feathers are often frosted with fine pale tips (photo, `amecoo-juv-local-williams.jpg`, `amecoo-juv-local-zimm.jpg`).
  - The chin, throat and foreneck are paler, "mottled with grayish white" [Bent 1926, p. 362]. On the local juveniles this is a whitish-grey throat patch running down the foreneck (photo, Haight, Macrhybopsis).
  - Head plumage "Blackish neutral gray ... dark neutral gray, medium neutral gray ... often with white feathers intermixed" [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, p. 44].
- **Body**: upperparts "pale ashy olive-brown to chaetura drab", underparts "much lighter than in adult—pale olive-buff with ashy light neutral gray bases" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213]. The body is browner and paler than the adult's slate, and the flanks carry more whitish tips (photo).
- **Undertail**: "under tail coverts white as in adult" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213]. The same white wedge as the adult (photo, Zimm).
- **Bill**: greyish: "Pearl gray ... olive gray ... light neutral gray ... grayish horn color" [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, p. 44]. On the local birds it is dull blue-grey or grey-horn, sometimes paler ivory-grey toward the tip (photo, Williams).
  - The subterminal band is "absent" or with "edges indistinct" [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, p. 42]; the juvenal bill "lacks the subterminal band" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213].
- **Shield**: small and flat, greyish or flesh-coloured, **no callus** [Gullion 1954, pp. 394, 396]. It barely rises above the culmen line in side view (photo, Williams).
- **Iris**: brown to reddish-brown [Eddleman & Knopf 1985, p. 44; Gullion 1954, p. 394]. It is orange-brown on the Williams bird (photo).
- **Legs**: "gray-green" [Gullion 1954, p. 394]; "greenish-gray" [BirdWeb].
- **A near-adult juvenile** looks like an adult with a sooty-grey instead of jet-black head: the front bird of `amecoo-pair-local-rakestraw.jpg` (12 October) has a red eye, a white bill and a red spot on its shield, but a sooty-grey head against the adult behind it (photo).

## 5. Structure

### 5.1 Head and neck

- "Plump waterbird with a rounded head and a sloping bill" [AAB-ID gallery]; "On land appears rather chickenlike" [AAB-ID gallery]; "a plump, chickenlike bird with a rounded head" [AAB-ID].
- The head is round, with the crown highest over or just behind the eye. The forehead rises steeply behind the shield (photo, R1, R2, C).
- The skull behind the bill is about **31.5 mm** long (computed, section 2.5); head width at the orbits is 15.1 mm [Eddleman & Knopf 1985].
- The neck is thick and fully feathered. It is held nearly vertical when swimming (the "normal posture ... head erect" [Gullion 1952, p. 84]), and bends forward in the pump (section 7).
- In the photos the neck's front edge rises from the waterline 35-50 mm behind the bill tip. The head sits over the breast, so the bill tip overhangs the breast by 35-50 mm (photo, R1, R2).

### 5.2 Bill and shield

- **Bill proper** (sections 2.1, 2.6): 31.8 mm along the culmen. Depth, both mandibles, perpendicular to the bill axis (photo-measured, W and C):

| Along the bill, from the tip | 0% | 25% | 50% | 75% | 100% (base) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Depth, mm | 0 (blunt point) | 5.4-8.0 | **10.5** | **12.6-12.7** | **15.3-15.8** |
| Width, mm | 0 | ≈ 3 (Estimate) | **5.2-6.5** (at the nostril) | ≈ 8.5 (Estimate) | **11.1** (upper mandible at the feathering) |

  - Width sources: 11.1 at the feathering [Eddleman & Knopf 1985]; 5.2 at the nostril [Tobias et al. 2022, 5 skins]. Ridgway gives width "at posterior end of nostril ... about two-thirds its depth" [p. 205], which on a 10 mm depth is 6.5 mm.
  - The nostril lies "nearly midway between loral feathering and tip of maxilla" [p. 205]; on the photos its anterior end is 13.4-15.2 mm from the tip (section 2.6).
  - The 25% and 75% widths are **Estimates**, interpolated.
  - **Cross-section**: taller than wide over the distal two-thirds (width / depth about 0.5-0.6), becoming wider than deep only at the flared base where the gape runs back under the eye. **Top view**: a narrow, pointed wedge, widest at the base. Unlike a duck's, it has no nail and no flattening.
  - **Shape in side view**: culmen straight for the basal half to two-thirds, "gently but very distinctly decurved distally" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 205]. On the photos the tip sits about 1-1.5 mm below the straight extension of the basal culmen (photo, W, C; low confidence).
  - The lower edge (gonys) runs straight at a shallower angle and turns up toward the tip in the distal third ("rather strongly ascending terminally").
  - The mouth line runs from the tip back to under the front of the eye, gape 35.1 mm [Eddleman & Knopf 1985].
- **Shield**: a white plate continuous with the bill, rising from the culmen junction up the forehead.
  - In side view the culmen line **bends upward by about 30-40° at the junction**, and the shield's front face climbs steeply to a rounded top.
  - The shield's rear edge meets the black forehead feathers along a line that runs down and forward to the lores (photo, R1, C, W).
  - **Autumn adult**: shield about **9 mm** along the forehead from the junction (3-11 on four birds), about **7 mm** wide (Gullion's flat 4-8), 2-2.5 mm thick; top 11-15 mm ahead of and 2-8 mm above the eye centre. Callus about **4 × 5 mm**, dark reddish-brown, capping the shield's top.
  - **Breeding or territorial maximum**: shield and callus up to 17 mm long, 14 mm wide and more than 3.6 mm thick [Gullion 1951, p. 158], swollen and convex.
  - **Juvenile**: shield flat and small (about 4-5 mm along the forehead, Estimate from "characteristically flat" [Gullion 1954, p. 396] and the photos), the same colour as the bill or flesh-grey, no callus.
  - Ridgway's "extending backward as far as middle of eye" describes the shield's rearmost reach on specimens, where the shield runs back under the forehead feathers. On live autumn birds in side view the visible white ends 11-15 mm ahead of the eye (photos).

### 5.3 Body on the water

- "floating higher in the water than the ducks or the gallinules, with the back more level, less submerged forward" [Bent 1926, p. 363]. In side view the back line is nearly level, highest over the rear flank where the tertials and scapulars stand up. It falls at the rear to a short stern with a slightly cocked tail (photo, R1, R2).
- The breast is full and rounded, meeting the water under the head. The front of the body is rounded like a hull, not a sharp prow (photo).
- Waterline and heights: section 6.

### 5.4 Tail and folded wing

- **Tail**: 51.7 mm, 0.28 of the wing, "rounded, the rectrices broad" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 205]; "their tiny tail" [AAB-ID].
  - At rest on the water the tail tip is **9-18 mm above the water**, pointing back and slightly up, 10-20° above level (photo, R1, R2).
  - The white undertail patches sit under and beside it (section 4.1).
  - In alarm the tail is lifted, showing more white [Gullion 1952, p. 89]. On the Neff video the tail is raised about 30-45° with both white patches showing while the bird turns and looks around (**Estimate**, video).
- **Folded wing on the water**: the wing is short (wing / total length 0.49, computed) and lies under the scapulars and flank feathers. Only a strip of the folded wing shows along the upper side, below the back line, ending under the raised tertials (photo, R1, R2).
  - The primaries are hidden and do not reach the tail.
  - The tertials stand slightly above the back line at the rear, the highest point of the back at 37 mm above water on R1 (photo).
  - Model the folded wing as mostly hidden: a shallow shell whose lower edge is roughly 10-15 mm above the waterline (Estimate).

### 5.5 Legs and lobed feet

- **Lengths**: tarsus 55.6, middle toe 74.0 without the claw (claw 12.8), hind toe 22.9 [Eddleman & Knopf 1985]. The inner toe is "decidedly shorter than outer"; the hallux "about as long as basal phalanx of outer toe" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 206].
- **Lobes**: "lateral membranes of toes very broad, the segments conspicuously convex, their lateral lobes with transverse scutella" [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 205].
  - On the Hansen photo the middle toe carries **three scalloped lobes**, one per segment, each a rounded flap on both sides of the toe, with a notch between lobes at each joint (photo).
  - One lobe per phalanx gives two on the inner toe and four on the outer. Ridgway describes segments but gives no count, so these counts are an **Estimate**.
  - **Lobed toe width**: about **8-12 mm** across the widest lobe, the toe itself 3-4 mm wide, so each lobe projects about **2.5-4 mm** to each side (photo-measured, two photos):
    - `amecoo-feet-hansen.jpg`: 10-12 mm, scaled by the middle toe with an assumed 45° view (±25%).
    - `amecoo-feet-walking-local-patricia.jpg`: 7.5-10.4 mm, scaled by the bill proper (213.6 px, 0.149 mm/px; nostril check 15.5 mm). This assumes the feet are at the head's distance from the camera.
  - The segments of the middle toe are about 25-30 mm long (photo, Hansen, at its assumed scale).
- **Colour**: tarsus yellow-green to bright yellow; toes olive-yellow on top; the lobes pale bluish-grey to greenish-grey with dark scale lines along the joints and edges; claws black (photo, Hansen, Cook, harvested photo) [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941, p. 213]. In juveniles legs and feet are grey-green [Gullion 1954].
- **Folding**: Townsend "has noted that it folds its toes as it lifts its foot" [Bent 1926, p. 364]. On the harvested photo the lifted toes are folded, lobes flattened along the toe (photo).
- **When the feet show**:
  - Leaping into a dive, when "the head entering as the feet leave the water" [Bent 1926, p. 364].
  - In a tip-up, when the feet paddle at the surface (video, Leon).
  - In clear shallow water, trailing under the bird (photo, `amecoo-foot-swimming-gallagher.jpg`: one foot extended back and down, toes folded, visible through clear water).
  - Walking out onto a bank or log (video, Savannah 34-38 s).
  - In fights, when "the fight starts with both birds sitting on their tails, propped against their wings on the water. Their adversary is grasped by the breast with the long claws of one foot, leaving the other foot free to slap the opponent" [Gullion 1952, p. 88]; the harvested guide photo shows exactly this.
  - While "splattering" across the surface [Gullion 1952, p. 86].
- **Bare tibia**: the lower tibia is bare; older birds show "red-orange color on the distal end of the tibia" [Crawford 1978, p. 539]. On one standing photo the bare part is about 0.4 of the tarsus, **about 20 mm** (photo, `amecoo-standing-collison.jpg`; low confidence). Legs are hidden while the bird floats.

## 6. Floating posture

**Method.** R1 and R2 are side-on photos of floating adults (Riverlands, Missouri, 14 October), taken from a bank about 18-21° above the water. Two things follow from that angle:
- The waterline on the near flank appears lower in the photo than the waterline under the bird's midline, by the flank's half-width × sin(angle): about 70 px (17 mm) on R1.
- Heights were therefore measured from the **midline water level**, found as the midpoint between each point and its reflection (bill tip and crown). It also agrees with the waterline at the breast front.
- Heights are scaled by the bill proper (section 2.6) and corrected by cos(angle) ≈ 0.95.

| Height above the waterline, mm | R1 | R2 | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|

**Draft** (computed, not photographed; the water hides it):
- Equilibrium means the bird displaces its own mass: 640 g → 640 cm³ of water.
- Take the hull under the waterline as half an ellipsoid 25-26 cm long (the measured waterline length) and 11-12 cm wide. That width is an **Estimate**: no source gives it, and I could not measure a coot square from the front.
- Then draft = 640 / ((2/3) π × 12.8 × 5.75) ≈ **4.2 cm** (3.9-4.4 over the ranges).
- Body depth ≈ 37 + 42 ≈ **80 mm**, and the **draft is 0.50-0.55 of the body depth**. The block model's `draft: 0.45` is close.
- A lighter bird (600 g) or a wider hull lowers the draft by a few millimetres. Air in the plumage makes the real bird float a little higher than a solid hull would, so the true draft is likely nearer the low end.

**Relaxed, alert, feeding** (video and photos; **Estimate** unless cited):
- **Relaxed or loafing**: neck drawn down so the head sits on the shoulders, crown about 80-90 mm above water; body level; tail low (Neff video 14-17 s, Savannah 7-10 s).
- **Normal swimming**: "The head is erect, the tail is held horizontally with the under tail coverts inconspicuous. The wings are held close to the body" [Gullion 1952, p. 84]. Neck upright, crown 110-130 mm (R1, R2); pumping (section 7).
- **Alert**: neck stretched up, crown up to about 140 mm; tail lifted "exhibiting the white under tail coverts but not expanding them" [Gullion 1952, p. 89].
- **Surface pecking**: body level. The neck arches forward and down so the bill tip reaches the water 30-60 mm ahead of the breast, and the crown drops to about 50-60 mm above water (Savannah 1.4-4 s).
- **Head-dip**: the head and neck go under; the breast sinks a little and the body pitches nose-down about 10°. The back stays above water and the tail stays level (Savannah 10.8-11.8 s).
- **Tip-up**: the body pitches 45° to vertical, tail and undertail up, feet paddling (Leon 22.25-23.75 s).
- **Aggressive (patrol, charge)**: "pulls its head down and slightly forward, the neck feathers are erected to form the ruff, the tail is slightly depressed" [Gullion 1952, pp. 85-86]. In the charge "the neck is extended forward on a horizontal plane ... The bird swims rapidly leaving a noticeable wake" [p. 86].

## 7. Swimming and feeding behavior

### 7.1 What the sources say

**Head pump**
- "As the coot swims in an unhurried manner, its head bobs, applying the principle of parallax to its feeding (cf. Grinnell, 1921). Head-bobbing of the coot is not necessarily in unison with the movements of the feet. When the bird is feeding on plant material this head movement is fairly slow, but it is quickened when insects are being hunted on the water's surface" [Gullion 1952, p. 84].
- "When either swimming or walking it nods its head in step with its foot movements, like a dove or a hen" [Bent 1926, p. 363]; "nods head while swimming" [Audubon]; coots "propel themselves through the water by pumping their heads back and forth" [BirdWeb].
- Coots and moorhens "bob their heads both when walking on the ground and when swimming"; "In species like coots which bob their heads while swimming there is no need for a stabilization of balance" [Necker n.d.] (a web summary of Necker 2007, not read). For walking birds the same page says the head is "kept stable with regard to the environment while the body moves continuously forward", then thrust forward.

**Feeding modes**
- "A slow and meticulous forager, the American Coot plucks at plants while walking, swimming, dabbling with its head just underwater, or in full dives" [AAB-LH]; "Typically feeds from the surface of the water but sometimes dives" [AAB-ID gallery].
- "dabbles at surface of water, upends in shallows, dives underwater (propelled by feet), grazes on land" [Audubon].
- "It feeds largely on the leaves, fronds, seeds and roots of aquatic vegetation ... Much of this food must be obtained by diving to moderate depths, at which it is an expert" [Bent 1926, p. 362]. Wetmore saw a coot "eating algae and slime that had collected on dead tule stems floating in the water. It fed eagerly on this material, seizing and stripping one piece after another" [Bent 1926, p. 363].
- Local photos show the same. About 15 of the 133 local records show a bird pecking at the surface with the bill dipped, swimming through duckweed or algae, or with a strand of weed hanging from the bill. One (137382891, 1 October) shows an adult with algae draped over its back, just surfaced. Only one shows a dive (244091347, two humps of water), and none a tip-up (appendix notes).

**Diving**
- **Dive times and pauses**, Louisiana, April [Batulis & Bongiorno 1972, p. 666]:
  - "The average diving times of four coots in pond I ranged from 1.3 ± 0.2 to 1.5 ± 0.2 seconds while the mean diving time of four other coots in pond II ranged from 2.5 ± 0.2 to 3.5 ± 0.4 seconds." Pond I was 25-45 cm deep (mean 36.8), pond II 52-74 cm (mean 60.1).
  - Table 1: dives per minute 2.8 ± 0.3 (0.7-4.2) and 1.2 ± 0.1 (0.9-1.6); pauses between successive dives 12.9 ± 0.7 s (1.0-63.0) and 24.6 ± 1.9 s (7.0-50.0); 214 dives by 10 coots and 34 dives by 5 coots.
  - "The calculated dive/pause ratios for the two areas are 0.11 (pond I) and 0.13 (pond II)."
  - The birds surfaced "with 'strings' of vegetation in their bills" and ate them on the surface.
- **The jump-dive**:
  - Townsend: it "often goes under water with very little effort; at other times it leaps clear of the water like a grebe, with its wings pressed close to its sides, its body describing an arc, and the head entering as the feet leave the water" [Bent 1926, p. 364].
  - Australian Coots "dived energetically, sometimes leaping from the water"; "Before diving, birds appeared to sight the weed by cocking their head sideways. After diving, a bird sometimes emerged holding in its bill a length of weed, which it proceeded to pick at" [Bakker & Fordham 1993, p. 133].
- **Eurasian Coot**, Italy: "Diving times showed a mean value of 2.59 sec (± 1.81 SD; n = 186). A large proportion of diving times (about 72%) were between 1 and 3 sec" [Fortunati & Battisti 2011] (abstract only: **Snippet**).
- **Longer dives**:
  - Day-old young stayed under "nearly three minutes", clinging to vegetation (Grinnell et al. 1918, in [Bent 1926, p. 360]).
  - A 25-28-day chick made "one dive of 15 seconds" [Gullion 1954, p. 393].
  - A chased female dived and surfaced "about 75 feet away" [Gullion 1954, p. 372].
  - A beaten fighter "often escapes by swimming long distances under water" [Gullion 1952, p. 88].
- **Autumn**:
  - "Coots rarely dived while foraging from October through mid-December" on an Alabama reservoir with Eurasian watermilfoil [LeSchack & Hepp 1995, p. 430].
  - In winter "As food abundance declined, reduced density of suitable food items resulted in fewer surface pecks and more time-consuming dives" [McKnight 1998] (abstract only: **Snippet**).
- **Willamette Valley flocks**: "outer-edge coots dove significantly less frequently than inner coots ... Overall dive frequency and dive time decreased with increasing flock size" [Smith & Hopkins 2026] (abstract only: **Snippet**; the numbers are behind a paywall).

**Time budget**
- Wintering coots in Alabama: "Feeding accounted for a substantial amount of time spent by ... coots (>62%)" [McKnight 1998] (abstract: **Snippet**).
- Breeding pairs in Iowa (spot samples every 30 s, 468 bird-hours) [Ryan & Dinsmore 1979, p. 707, Table 2]: feeding 49-69%, locomotion 5-27%, loafing 1-11%, maintenance (preening, bathing) 8-18%, aggression under 7%.

**Flocks, chases and splattering (note)**
- "Splattering.—This display is a rapid charge ... while it runs over the water with flapping wings" [Gullion 1952, p. 86]. The coot is called "spatterer" for "rising noisily from the water; running along the surface, it beats the water with wings and feet" [Bent 1926, p. 363].
- A charging coot makes other coots "take evasive action while still 100 feet from the charging bird" [Gullion 1952, p. 86].
- After a coot surfaces with food, "other coots appeared sometimes to attempt to snatch from the foragers, causing sudden directional changes while a short chase ensued (~1 s)" [Trenchard 2012, p. 6]. Gadwalls and wigeons also rob coots [Eddleman et al. 1985, p. 466].
- Flock formation:
  - Winter flocks sit "in a nearly solid mass" or "in open formation ... swimming about and feeding in their usual animated way" (Maynard; Williams, in [Bent 1926, pp. 365-366]).
  - In loose feeding flocks, coots are "visibly separated at proximities of at least one body length" in the disordered phase. Measured densities were 0.17-0.70 coots per square body length [Trenchard 2012, pp. 7-8], a grid spacing of **1.2-2.4 body lengths, 0.4-0.9 m** (computed with his 0.37 m body length). Trenchard's is an arXiv preprint, not peer reviewed.

### 7.2 Video estimates (Estimate)

No source gives pump rates, peck rates, head-dip durations or swimming speeds, so these come from CC-licensed video. Each clip was downloaded, frames were tiled at 3-12 per second, and where stated a template tracker followed the head and the body. The clips are listed under Video in the References; the main one is [Homler 2024] (Savannah NWR, Georgia, 23 April 2024), one adult side-on in 4K at 23.98 fps.

**Head pump**
- On Savannah 30.0-31.9 s (a feeding adult swimming side-on), the head-to-rear distance peaked at 30.42, 30.88 and 31.34 s: a **period of 0.46 s, 2.2 pumps per second**.
- Aligned on the undertail, the bill tip moves about 120-140 px at 1920 px width. The bill including the shield spans about 96 px there, so the travel is **about 5-6 cm, 1.3 × the culmen with shield, about 17% of the bill-to-tail length**.
- In the forward phase the neck angles about 30° forward from vertical and the head rises 2-3 cm (tracker, dy 58 px).
- On the Shoreline Park clip (0-4 s, swimming left) the tracked head position has minima at 0.30, 1.45, 1.94, 2.36, 2.84, 3.24 s and maxima at 1.27, 1.70, 2.15, 2.57, 3.07 s: **period 0.42-0.50 s, about 2.2 per second**.
- The pump looks hold-and-thrust, as in walking birds: the head nearly stops against the water, then shoots forward. At about 0.2 m/s and a hold of half the cycle, the body advances about 5 cm during the hold (computed), matching the measured travel.
- **Not while idle**: on Savannah 7-10 s the bird drifts with its head steady.

**Swimming speed**
- Savannah 30.0-31.5 s: the undertail moved 766 px against a floating leaf patch in 1.5 s at about 0.42 mm/px (1920-px scale), **about 0.2 m/s** while feeding. The tracker's scores fell to 0.56 on the rear, so this is rough.
- A travelling group went "approximate speed of 0.5 m/s" [Trenchard 2012, p. 11].

**Surface pecking**
- Savannah 1.4-4.0 s at 12 fps: the bill went to the water at 1.42-1.67, 1.83-2.25, 2.42, 2.83-3.00, 3.25, 3.58 and 3.92 s: **about 2.5 pecks a second**, some held for 0.2-0.4 s.
- The body stays level; the neck arches forward and down; the head comes up about 2-3 cm between pecks rather than to full height.
- Bouts of 2-8 s are separated by 1-5 s of swimming or looking up (0-12 s of the clip).

**Head-dip**
- Savannah 10.8-11.8 s: the head and neck go under for **about 1.0 s**. The body stays level with a slight nose-down tilt; the tail does not rise; the back stays well above water. The bird surfaces with the bill at the water and pecks again.

**Tip-up**
- Leon (Québec, 15 June 2013, an adult feeding a chick) 22.25-23.75 s: the head goes under and the body tips from about 45° to near vertical, tail up with the white undertail showing, for **about 1.5 s**. A splashing dive follows (24.0-24.5 s).
- Tip-ups appear in the sources only as "upends in shallows" [Audubon]. None appears on the Savannah clip (40 s of an adult feeding in open water).

- **The jump-dive in motion** ([Breckenridge 2015], 4 fps):
  - The head dips and the bird lunges forward over about 0.25 s.
  - The rear lifts until the body is near vertical, tail up.
  - The head enters and the feet leave within the next 0.25 s, with a splash.
  - Entry takes **about 0.5 s** in all.
- **Surface intervals** between dives in a bout: 4-11 s ([Breckenridge 2015], an adult feeding young) and 19 s (Fairview Park); 12.9 and 24.6 s [Batulis & Bongiorno 1972].
- These dives are in spring and summer; the adults in [Breckenridge 2015] were feeding chicks.

These are single birds on 4-6 clips. Treat them as settings, not measurements.

## 12. Open questions and confidence

**High confidence**
- The linear measurements: two large samples agree. Ridgway & Friedmann (31 M, 26 F skins) and Eddleman & Knopf (about 1,460 fresh birds) are within 1-3% for wing, tail and tarsus.
- The bill proper of 31.8 mm and the nostril distance of 14.2 mm [Ridgway & Friedmann 1941]. Four photos reproduce the nostril distance within 6% when scaled by the bill proper.
- The juvenile's traits and how long they last: Gullion 1954 (43 marked young) and Eddleman & Knopf (540 juveniles) agree. The juvenile is adult-sized.
- Shield sizes in the flat and swollen states [Gullion 1951].
- Dive times of 1.3-3.5 s in shallow water [Batulis & Bongiorno 1972], with a Eurasian Coot mean of 2.6 s (abstract).
- The field-mark wording in section 4, quoted from Ridgway & Friedmann, Bent, Eddleman & Knopf, Gullion, AAB, BirdWeb and Audubon, and matching the photos.

**Medium confidence**
- **The age mix.** The photo tally (24% young, n = 127 ageable birds) is a floor biased toward adults. The collected samples (41-52%) come from other regions. Four adults and two juveniles is a judgement between them.
- **Waterline heights** (back 35-41, bill tip 72-86, crown 108-132 mm): two photos of possibly the same bird at one site, corrected for an 18-21° camera angle by reflection geometry, and scaled by a single bill measurement (±6%).
- **The bill depth profile**: two photos in close agreement (10.5 mm at mid-bill on both).
- **The autumn shield** (3-11 mm along the forehead, callus 0-5 mm): four birds, only one with a callus bigger than a speck. But the notes on 25 of the local September-October records mention a red-brown or dark callus, so on Willamette adults at this season the 4 × 5 mm callus is probably typical.

**Low confidence or not verified**
- **The draft** (0.042 m): computed from buoyancy with an assumed hull width. It is not observed, since the water hides it; plumage air probably makes the bird ride a little higher.
- **Head-pump rate and amplitude** (2.2 per second, 5 cm): two birds, one in April in Georgia, one in February in California, measured by template tracking whose body-anchor scores fell to 0.3-0.6. The period agrees on both, but the amplitude rests on one.
- **Swimming speed** (0.2 m/s): one clip, tracker scores 0.56-0.8.
- **Peck rate, head-dip and tip-up durations**: one or two clips each.
- **Dive durations from video**: spring and summer adults feeding chicks (3.8-5.5 s) and a distant winter bird (1.7 s); not late September. Distances moved underwater are rough.
- **Lobe width and count**: two photos, one scaled by an assumed viewing angle and one by the bill at an assumed equal distance; 7.5-12 mm.
- **Bare tibia** (20 mm): one photo.
- **Bill width** at the quarter points: interpolated.
- **Snippet-only claims**: McKnight 1998's ">62%" and the shift from pecks to dives, Fortunati & Battisti's 2.59 s, and Smith & Hopkins's Willamette flock-size result are abstracts only.
- The iNaturalist ageing was done by a sub-agent on stated criteria; I viewed eight of its records myself (section 8) and they agreed.

**Sources I could not read** (not relied on for any number)
- **Birds of the World** (Brisbin & Mowbray 2002): Appearance, Measurements and Behavior sections, paywalled.
- **Pyle 2008**: not online.
- **Fredrickson 1968** (coot measurements by sex and age), **Gullion 1952b** (sex and age determination), **Burton 1959**, **Alisauskas 1987**: *J. Wildl. Manage.* and *Auk* papers not reached, or not on SORA.
- **Desrochers & Ankney 1986** (feeding behaviour of adult and juvenile coots, *Can. J. Zool.*): paywalled.
- **Smith & Hopkins 2026** (Willamette Valley dive frequency and duration by flock position): abstract only. This is the most relevant study for Willow Lake and worth buying.
- **Marshall et al. 2003** (*Birds of Oregon*) and eBird bar charts: not reached. The local timing rests on BirdWeb, the iNaturalist records and Gullion's growth table.
- **Macaulay Library** video: not used.

**Worth doing if higher confidence is needed**
- Buy Smith & Hopkins 2026 for Willamette dive frequency and dive time.
- Film a late-September coot flock side-on at water level on a Willamette pond to measure the pump, pecks, dives and spacing on the right birds in the right month.
- Measure a head-on photo for the hull width, which sets the draft.
- Measure the built model's heights and compare them with section 10.
