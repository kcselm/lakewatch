# Double-crested Cormorant: measurements, late-September plumage and age, field marks, how it floats awash, diving and fish-handling, wing-drying, flight, and the cast for a valley lake

Date: 2026-10-04
Purpose: source data for a detailed Double-crested Cormorant (`doccor`, *Nannopterum auritum*, formerly *Phalacrocorax auritus*) model and its styles: swimming and diving on the lake, perching and spreading its wings on snags, and flying low over the water. The scene is the same as the pelican's and the goose's: a Willamette Valley lake, Oregon, on a morning of about 20-30 September. The player stands on the bank with binoculars (7.5° vertical field) and a telescope (2°: about 1.3-2.9 mm of bird per pixel at 40-90 m on a 1080-pixel-tall view).

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means the text was visible only as a search-engine excerpt of a paywalled page (chiefly Birds of the World); treat it as unverified, and its wording may be the search engine's paraphrase. "Abstract only" means I read the abstract, not the paper. "Computed" means my own arithmetic on cited data. "Photo-measured" means I measured it in pixels on a cited photo (section 8) and scaled it by the exposed culmen (57 mm, section 2.7) unless stated. "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated. Two helper agents read sources for me (museum measurements and plumage; behaviour); I checked the key figures (the BBR regional table and Pennycuick's Table 3) against page images myself.

## 1. Summary

- **Measurements** (mean of the sexes, the Pacific subspecies *albociliatus*; mm): exposed culmen **57**, bill **17 deep and 12 wide** at the nostril with a hooked nail a third of its length, wing chord **315**, tail **165** (12 stiff feathers), tarsus **65**, outer toe about **86**, wingspan **1.20 m**, mass about **2.0 kg**, stretched length 0.78 m [Dawson 1923; BBR 1884; Tobias et al. 2022; AAB-ID]. Males are 2-6% larger and 12-15% heavier ([BoW-A], Snippet); the model is the mean.
- **Model length** (bill tip to tail tip, swimming with the neck upright) about **0.60 m**; 0.79 m on a standing bird with the neck stretched, 0.69 m in flight (photo-measured).
- **Two versions.** Of 52 valley birds close enough to age in late-September photos, 28 were dark adult-type birds and 24 pale-breasted young (section 3.2). Paint the **non-breeding adult** (black with a faint green gloss; back and wing coverts grey-brown to bronze, each feather edged black, so the saddle looks scaled; **no crests** by September; face and throat skin orange-yellow; iris green-turquoise; feet black) and a **juvenile** (grey-brown head; whitish-buff throat, foreneck and breast shading to a dark brown belly; paler scaled back; yellow face). Hex estimates in sections 4.1 and 9.
- **How it floats: awash.** The back stands only **about 25 mm** above the water, the crown 0.165 m, the tail lies flat on the water; about **80% of the body's depth is under water** (photo-measured and computed from a published buoyancy of 2.7 N/kg). The bill is held level to 30° up, median about 15°.
- **Diving:** a smooth forward slide from the surface (a small leap now and then), foot-propelled, wings closed. Wild dives about **22 s** (17-34), surface pauses about **10-20 s**, bouts of a few to 17 dives [Ross 1974 via Enstipp et al. 2006; BoW-B, Snippet; Enstipp et al. 2006]. Fish come up crosswise, are juggled to go **head first**, swallowed with forward-upward jerks and followed by a head-shake [Bartholomew 1942].
- **Wing-drying:** upright on a snag, piling, rock or log, wings about three-quarters spread with the wrists up and the hands drooping, the tail spread and propped; only when wet; facing **into the wind** [Hennemann 1983, 1984; Sellers 1995]. Bouts of minutes, about 4-5 minutes in all in calm air (Great Cormorant figures). **9% of perched birds** in the valley photos had their wings spread.
- **Flight:** **5.0 Hz** wingbeat, **14.5 m/s**, nearly continuous flapping [Pennycuick 1990]; low over water (under 1.5 m); neck straight out with a slight upward bend, head carried level; take-off a pattering run with both feet striking together.
- **Cast:** in the valley photos cormorants are mostly alone or in twos and threes; at most five together. **Four birds**: three on a snag (two adults, one juvenile), one fishing.
- **Sound:** silent away from colonies; at most a grunt at take-off or landing.

## 2. Measurements

The valley's birds belong to the Pacific population, *albociliatus* (the "Farallon" or "Lesser White-tufted" Cormorant of the old books), which breeds "from southern Oregon to Lower California ... in many lakes in the interior" [Bent 1922, pp. 257-261] and north along the coast and lower Columbia [ODFW]. The tables I could read are Baird, Brewer & Ridgway (1884), Ridgway's *Manual* (1887), Dawson (1923), AVONET's four skins and Owre (1967). The sexed masses of Birds of the World I could read only as a search excerpt (Snippet). **Palmer 1962, Pyle 2008, Dunning 2008 and Glahn & McCoy could not be read.**

### 2.1 Baird, Brewer & Ridgway 1884 and Ridgway 1887 (museum skins)

The species as a whole: "Total length, about 29 to 33 inches; extent, 45 to 55; wing, 11.20-14.00; tail, 5.60-8.50; culmen, 1.90-2.55; tarsus, 2.05-2.90; longest toe, 3.05-3.75. (A marked increase of size to the northward.)" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 151]. The "longest toe" is the outer toe; there is no bill depth or middle-toe figure. ### 2.5 Sexual dimorphism

- "males average about 2-6% larger than females (12-15% by mass)" [BoW-A] (Snippet). The three mass samples above give males 14-19% heavier (computed).
- Bill depth separates the sexes best of the bill measures: wintering birds in Mississippi, "male, 17.6 mm ± 0.1 SE (range 15.5-20, n = 160); female, 15.8 mm ± 0.2 SE (range 14-18, n = 40)"; "wing chord, culmen depth, and culmen length were the most useful variables" (Glahn & McCoy, via [BoW-A]; Snippet).
- The sexes are alike in plumage [BBR 1884; AAB-ID]. A 5% difference in length does not read at 40-90 m. **The model is the mean of the sexes.**

## 3. Late-September plumage and age in the Willamette Valley

### 3.1 What the sources say

- **The adult in breeding dress** (for contrast; none of it shows in late September): "Head, neck, rump, and entire lower parts, glossy black, with a faint lustre of dull bluish green; back, scapulars, and wings, dull grayish brown, each feather conspicuously and broadly bordered with black; tail uniform dull black. A tuft of narrow, lengthened, curved feathers on each side the crown, springing from behind and above the eye, these feathers ... wholly pure white (in Pacific coast examples) ... Maxilla black, mottled with grayish or dull yellowish along the sides; mandible yellowish or pale bluish, mottled with dusky; loral region and gular sac deep orange; eyelids and whole interior of the mouth bright cobalt-blue, the former sometimes dotted with white; iris bright grass-green; legs and feet deep black" [BBR 1884, vol. II, pp. 150-151].
- **The crests are long gone by September.** "All of the subspecies of Phalacrocorax auritus habitually shed their crests during the season of incubation, the long curling plumes serving merely as courtship and nuptial adornments" [Bent 1922, p. 256]; "These nuptial plumes are apparently shed during the nesting period, as they are seldom seen after the season is well advanced" [p. 248]; "crests are present only briefly, early in the breeding season" [BoW-A] (Snippet). None of the valley's late-September photos shows a crest (section 3.2).
- **The adult in fall and winter.** "Similar to the above, but tufts of the head wanting, and the bare skin of the lores, gular pouch, etc., deep yellow instead of orange, and the blue of the mouth and eyelids absent" [BBR 1884, vol. II, p. 151]; "Without crests; less glossy; and colors of soft parts paler" [Dawson 1923, vol. 4, p. 1937]. The adult molts completely "in the late summer" [Bent 1922, p. 248]; Definitive Basic is "present primarily Aug-Jul" [BoW-A] (Snippet). The ODFW summary: "Adults have deep, coppery feathers, bordered in black, that glisten with a green iridescent and offset a rich orange throat atop a long curved neck" [ODFW]. "Nonbreeding birds are dark overall with orange-yellow skin around the bill and chin" [AAB-ID, photo caption].
- **The eye.** "iris bright grass-green" [BBR 1884, p. 151]; "Iris bright green" and, for Florida birds, "Iris light green, margins of eyelids light blue, spotted with white" (Audubon, in [BBR 1884, pp. 150-151]); "irides nile green", "The bird's eye is of a handsome blue-green ... matches the 'pipings' which border the loral areas" [Dawson 1923, pp. 1937, 1941]; "striking aquamarine eyes that sparkle like jewels, and a mouth that is bright blue on the inside" [AAB-O].
- **The young bird of the year.** "Head and neck grayish brown, lighter next to the gular sac, darker on the crown and nape; back, scapulars, and wings, dull brownish gray, the feathers bordered with dusky brown; rump dusky brown; primaries and tail dull grayish black; lower parts light fawn-color, darker on the sides, anal-region, and crissum. Bill dull brownish yellow, nearly black on the culmen; gular sac deep chrome yellow; iris greenish gray; legs and feet deep black. Young, in winter: Similar to the above, but throat, jugulum, and breast paler, sometimes quite white" [BBR 1884, p. 151]. "The first winter plumage is complete in September ... each brown feather being bordered with brownish black, but it is dull and lusterless, instead of glossy greenish black; the under parts are dull brown, lighter on the throat and belly than elsewhere; the colors become lighter and duller as the season advances, by wear and fading" [Bent 1922, p. 247]; first-winter birds "have a light-gray breast shading down to black on the lower belly" [p. 239]. "Juveniles have a pale neck and breast that gradually blends into its darker belly" [AAB-ID, photo caption]; "Immatures are browner overall, palest on the neck and breast" [AAB-ID].
- **The juvenile in the modern account** (after Pyle; Snippet): "Juvenile (First Basic) Plumage ... present primarily Jun-Mar. Head mostly dark brownish gray to pale brown, shading to grayish white on chin; throat whitish with brown mottling. Upperparts and upperwing coverts dark grayish brown with darker feather margins providing scaly appearance, darkest on lower back and rump; feathers shorter and more pointed than in later plumages and lacking gloss ... remainder of underparts mottled brown and whitish" [BoW-A]. On the iris the sources disagree: "Juveniles have brown irises" [BoW-A] (Snippet) against "iris greenish gray" [BBR 1884].
- **Second-year birds.** "Second Basic Plumage ... present primarily Jul-Jun. Similar to Definitive Basic ... feathering dark brown with less gloss; head, neck and breast washed brown or variably mixed with brown and glossier black feathers"; the molt into it runs July to January and often leaves "1-4 juvenile outer primaries"; full adult dress comes "at Third or Fourth Basic" [BoW-A] (Snippet). "Young male (2nd year?): Head, neck, and forebreast grayish brown, lighter below and on sides; darker on crown and behind; remaining underparts, lower back, etc., rich dark brown; back and wings much as in adult" [Dawson 1923, p. 1937]. The first postnuptial molt "is often not completed, however, until the latter part of the winter" [Bent 1922, pp. 247-248].
- **Molt never grounds them:** an "irregular, often unilateral molt of both remiges and rectrices"; no flightless period [Owre 1967, p. 50]. A late-September bird may show a gap or two in the wing in flight.

### 3.3 Recommendation

- **Two versions.**
  - `adult` (the default): non-breeding adult. Head, neck and underparts black with a faint green gloss; back, scapulars and wing coverts grey-brown to bronze, each feather broadly edged black (a scaled saddle); primaries, secondaries and tail black; no crest; face and throat skin orange-yellow; iris green-turquoise; bill dark grey above, paler and yellowish below; feet black.
  - `juvenile`: the bird of the year. Head and hindneck grey-brown; chin, throat, foreneck and breast pale buff to off-white, shading through mottled brown to a **dark brown-black belly, flanks and thighs**; back and wing coverts paler grey-brown, scaled with darker edges; flight feathers and tail blackish-brown; face skin yellow; bill pale horn-yellow with a dark ridge; feet black; iris greenish (photo) to brown (Snippet).
  - A third `secondYear` (adult pattern, browner, mottled pale on the throat and breast) is not worth a version at 40-90 m.
- **Cast:** see section 7.6.

### 3.4 Status in the valley in autumn

- "the most abundant and widespread of the three cormorant species found in Oregon, occurring inland and on the coast"; "a common breeder in spring and summer at bays and estuaries, and on islands and cliffs along the coast and lower Columbia River" [ODFW].
- The Willamette Valley checklist marks it as found on "ponds and lakes", not breeding, "occasionally detected" in spring and fall, "rarely" in summer and "regularly" in winter [WV checklist].
- "Double-crested Cormorants are commonly seen perching on dead trees and utility poles when they are not fishing" (Fernhill Wetlands, January 2010); "Double-crested Cormorants always put on a good show along the Willamette River in Portland" (March 2015); "this Double-crested Cormorant was taking advantage of the sun to dry his wings" (Tillamook, 6 October 2020); "Three American White Pelicans swim with Double-crested Cormorants on Smith Lake" (16 October 2011) [Rakestraw 2010-2022].
- "Permanent residents on the coasts and in Puget Sound", eastern migrants "departing in October and returning in March"; "common year round" in western Washington; "gregarious throughout the year ... form dense nocturnal roosts, and travel in flocks during migration" [BirdWeb].
- `data/birds.json` gives the species a frequency of 0.525 and 1.0 for September in the game's own hotspot data (small sample; source not traced).
- **For the scene:** one to three cormorants on a valley lake on a late-September morning is honest; the photographed groups never exceed five.

## 5. Structure

### 5.1 Bill

- "Bill small (shorter than the middle toe) ... maxillary unguis always prominent and strongly hooked; nostrils obliterated; lores, orbital region, lower jaw, chin, and upper part of throat naked" [BBR 1884, p. 144]; "Bill stout ... the terminal nail, or unguis, more or less arched, strongly hooked, and occupying one-third, or more, of the entire length of the upper mandible" [Ridgway 1887, p. 77]; "thin, strongly hooked bills, roughly the length of the head" [AAB-ID].
- **Profile** (photo-measured on `doccor-juv-perch.jpg`, 0.44 mm/px): the culmen is straight from the forehead to the nail, which arches and hooks down about **5-6 mm** below the lower outline of the upper mandible; the bill is about 17 mm deep at the feather line and about **9-10 mm** deep at two-thirds of its length (Estimate from the photo); the cutting edge is nearly straight.
- **Width:** 12 mm at the nostril, narrowing evenly to the nail (Estimate: 9 mm at half its length, 6 mm at the nail).
- **The gape** runs back under the eye: on `doccor-juv-perch.jpg` the corner of the mouth lies about **1.8 culmens (103 mm) behind the bill tip**, just below and a little in front of the eye's centre (photo-measured).
- **The bare skin:** a triangle of orange-yellow from the lores forward of the eye, round under the eye and down onto the throat (the small gular pouch), its rear edge a straight line across the throat about level with the back of the eye: "Basal outline of the gular pouch extending straight across the throat or projecting slightly back along the median line" [BBR 1884, p. 150]; "gular sac small, scarcely distensible" [Ridgway 1887, p. 73]. On `doccor-juv-perch.jpg` the bare throat skin reaches only about 10-15 mm below the lower mandible at its deepest (photo-measured): no pouch to speak of.

### 5.2 Head and eye

- **Head length**, occiput to bill tip: **0.165 m**, about 2.9 culmens (photo-measured on `doccor-juv-perch.jpg`). Owre's skulls measured 117-131 mm, mean 124 [Owre 1967, Table 23, p. 108] (*floridanus*).
- **The eye:** the iris is about **11 mm** across (26 px on `doccor-juv-perch.jpg`), so the model's eye radius is **about 5.5 mm**; its centre is 85-88 mm behind the bill tip (section 2.6), about 0.5 culmen behind the forehead feathering, and a little above the line of the culmen's base. The eye sits high and forward in the face, in the bare skin of the lores (photo: `doccor-head-laporte.jpg`, `doccor-head-portrait.jpg`).
- **The crown** is low and flat, rising behind the eye to a rounded hind-crown; the forehead slopes straight up from the culmen (photo).

### 5.3 Neck

- **Long, thick, and kinked.** "small heads on long, kinked necks" [AAB-ID]; "a distinctive S-shaped crook in their neck" [AAB-O].
- **The cervical column is heavier and shorter than an anhinga's:** "the more massive, shorter cervical vertebrae. The cervical vertebrae of Phalacrocorax cannot be drawn as far dorsocaudad and cannot be arched in position for rapid forward motion as they can be in the Anhinga" [Owre 1967, p. 106]. A cormorant has 18 cervical vertebrae, the most in its order; a z-shape "may be present but obscured by their shorter vertebrae and greater soft-tissue bulk in the neck" [Fleming & Black 2026] (summary of the paper, not read in full). The anhinga's hinge at the eighth vertebra does not apply.
- **Length** (no source measures it): on `doccor-juv-perch.jpg` the hindneck from the back of the skull to where it meets the mantle is about **0.20 m** along its curve, about 3.5 culmens (photo-measured). Erect, the neck raises the crown about **0.23 m** above the top of the shoulders (`doccor-juv-perch.jpg`); drawn back into the resting S, about **0.11 m** (`doccor-adult-perch-2.jpg`) (photo-measured).
- **Thickness:** about **56-66 mm** fore and aft at mid-height (photo-measured on the swimming and perched birds), about a culmen; the head is barely wider than the neck.
- **Shapes seen:** erect and nearly straight with a forward curve at the throat (alert, perched or swimming); a tight S with the head on the shoulders (resting); a long forward-and-down reach (handling a fish, `doccor-swim-fish.jpg`); straight out in front in flight (section 7.4).

### 5.4 Body, wings and tail

- **Body:** a long, narrow cylinder. In profile on `doccor-juv-perch.jpg`, breast to the base of the tail is about **0.38 m** and the depth across the body square to its axis about **0.13 m** at mid-body; on `doccor-swim-side.jpg` the floating back is 0.40 m from the mantle at the waterline to the tail tip (photo-measured). **Width** across the closed wings: no rear or top view with a scale; **Estimate 0.12 m**, from the depth and the narrow back seen from behind on `doccor-adult-back.jpg` and `doccor-adult-perch-3.jpg` (not measured).
- **Wings closed:** "Wings rather short, concave, reaching but little beyond the base of the tail" [BBR 1884, p. 144]. The folded primaries end over the root of the tail (photo).
- **Tail:** "tail variable as to length, usually rounded or graduated, the feathers stiff, with very rigid shafts, which are exposed almost to the base of the tail, on account of the much abbreviated coverts" [BBR 1884, p. 144]; 12 feathers; about 165 mm, a third longer than a duck's relative to the wing (tail / wing 0.52). Perched, the tail hangs down along the perch or is pressed against it as a prop (photo: `doccor-juv-perch.jpg`, `doccor-spread-travis.jpg`, `doccor-adult-perch-3.jpg`). Swimming, it lies flat on or just under the water.
- **Plumage texture:** "Plumage very compact, usually dark-colored and glossy" [BBR 1884, p. 144].

### 5.5 Legs and feet

- **Totipalmate:** all four toes webbed; "powerful totipalmate feet" [Bent 1922, p. 248]; the outer toe the longest: "Middle toe longer than, or about equal to, the tarsus, the outer toe much longer, and the inner about as much shorter" [BBR 1884, p. 144]. The hind toe turns forward and inward and is webbed to the inner toe (photo: `doccor-adult-perch-3.jpg` shows the broad black web across the perch).
- **Short, stout, set far back:** tarsus 65 mm; perched birds stand upright on them with the body at 35-45° and the tail as a third leg (photo).
- **Colour:** black (all sources and every photo).
- **Under water:** the feet do the work: "propelled by powerful, webbed feet" [BirdWeb]; "feet are held ventral to the posterior end of the body" (Stolpe, in [Owre 1967, p. 60]).

## 6. How it floats

### 6.1 What the sources say

- "They may be seen swimming low in the water, often with little more than their heads and sinuous necks showing" [BoW-I]; "Their heavy bodies sit low in the water" [AAB-ID]; "When swimming atop the water, cormorants ride very low, and often only their long necks are evident" [AAB-LH]; "On the water they sit low, with the head and bill usually tilted slightly upward" [AAB-O]. "They swim with back and shoulders emergent ... While at the surface, cormorants frequently submerge the head as though looking or listening for something below" [Owre 1967, p. 60] (Florida birds).
- **Why so low.** Cormorant plumage is partly wettable: "each cormorant body feather has a loose, instantaneously wet, outer section and a highly waterproof central portion", the birds keeping "a thin layer of air" [Grémillet et al. 2005] (abstract only). The air trapped in the plumage of six Double-crested Cormorant carcasses was "0.13±0.04 l kg–1", "similar to that of great cormorants and well below the volume of other avian divers"; "buoyancy is reduced in cormorants ... [2.7 N kg–1 at the surface for P. auritus (Lovvorn and Jones, 1991a)]" [Enstipp et al. 2006]. Buoyancies "of cormorants much lower ... than those of ducks" [Lovvorn & Jones 1991] (abstract only). "Cormorants have less preen oil than other birds, so their feathers can get soaked rather than shedding water like a duck's" [AAB-LH]. Great Cormorants keep dive bouts under 18 minutes "so that the added mass of retained water did not exceed 6% of their body mass", which reduces "the buoyancy of the dry bird by 18%" [Ribak et al. 2005a] (abstract only); a Double-crested Cormorant gains "typically 1-3%" of its mass in water (Mahoney 1984, via [Sellers 1995, p. 33]).
- **Sinking lower when alarmed:** no source found; not modelled.

The photo agrees: a body about 0.13 m deep (section 5.4) with 23-25 mm showing has 80-82% of its depth under (computed). **Recommended: draft 0.105 m on a 0.13 m body; about 80% of the body's depth under water.** Compare the goose's 30% and the pelican's 30%: the cormorant floats nearly awash.
- **Swimming speed at the surface:** no measurement found. Under water: "Underwater speeds for double-crested cormorants are not reported, but about 1.5 m/s for other cormorant species" [BoW-B] (Snippet); Great Cormorants under water averaged "1.5 m s(-1)" [Ribak et al. 2005b] (abstract only). **Estimate: 0.3-0.5 m/s cruising on the surface**, about one body length a second, from the pelican's 0.57 m/s and the goose's similar figure; a slow paddle between dives.

## 7. Behaviour

### 7.1 Diving and foraging

**How it dives.**
- "diving only from the surface, and never when flying" (Cooper, in [BBR 1884, p. 153]); "dive from the surface and hunt their prey underwater using powerful strokes of their totipalmate feet" [BoW-I]; "their long slender bodies and powerful totipalmate feet are highly specialized for rapid swimming, without the aid of wings" [Bent 1922, p. 248]; "Wings are too large to be used as the principal means of underwater propulsion, which comes from feet" [BoW-B] (Snippet).
- **Two kinds of dive.** Solitary birds "seem [to] slide beneath the water"; in large fishing flocks birds "leap completely out [of] the water [as] they dive", with "vertical splashes two [or] three feet" high [Bartholomew 1942, p. 16] (words in brackets restored in a damaged scan). "disappear with an easy gliding dive" (Taverner, in [Bent 1922, p. 249]). In captivity "deep dives were typically initiated with a pre-dive leap ... which rarely occurred at the onset of shallow dives" [Enstipp et al. 2006]. **For the game: a lone bird on a shallow valley lake mostly does the smooth forward slide (head first, the body arching after it, the tail last), with a small leap now and then.**
- **Under water** (captive birds in a 10 m tank): the descent at "about -70°"; "Stroke frequency during the early descent phase of deep dives (3.42±0.25 strokes s–1)", a mean of "1.8 and 2.1 strokes s–1 for deep and shallow diving"; "It took cormorants ∼10 s to reach the tank bottom (10 m)"; they "ascended passively" [Enstipp et al. 2006]. The feet "are held ventral to the posterior end of the body" (Stolpe, in [Owre 1967, p. 60]). Not seen by the player.

- A bout ended when the bird stayed "at the surface for longer than 100 s" [Enstipp et al. 2006].
- **Adopted for the game:** dives of **22 s** (uniform 15-30), surface pauses of **12 s** (8-20), bouts of **6-15 dives**, then a rest of minutes on the water or a flight to a perch.

**Depth.** Nova Scotia mean 4.7 m, maximum 7.9 m; Oneida Lake and Lake Ontario often 10-15 m; the deepest recorded 25.8 m [BoW-B] (Snippet); "Prefers water less than 9 m ... may catch fish as deep as 22 m" [CDFW]. A valley lake 1-5 m deep is fished to the bottom.

**Surfacing with a fish.**
- The fish comes up "usually held crossways [in] its bill" and is "shifted [so] that [it] will [go] down head first"; "[I] have never seen [a] fish swallowed other than head foremost"; of "literally thousands [of] fish caught ... only once have [I] seen [a] fish tossed into the air". Swallowing is "[a] series [of] jerks [in] which the head [is] thrust forward and slightly upward ... with larger fish, four [or] five thrusts may [be] necessary"; afterwards the bird "invariably shakes its head vigorously from side [to] side" [Bartholomew 1942, p. 18]. An eight-inch flounder: "Fully fifteen seconds elapsed before the fish reached the base [of] the neck" [p. 19].
- "They catch a fish crossways ... then there is an upward flirt of the bill and the fish is swallowed" (Taverner, in [Bent 1922, p. 249]); "Both juggle and mouth fish to position them for swallowing head-first" [Owre 1967, p. 106]; prey is subdued by "shaking and hammering on the water. Then prey may be thrown in the air, to be caught and swallowed headfirst" [BoW-B] (Snippet); for crayfish, "hammering the prey on the water to shake its legs off, then flipping it in the air and catching it headfirst for easy swallowing" [AAB-LH].
- **For the game:** most surfacings bring nothing up (in fishing flocks about "one [in] every six [or] seven birds returning ... has [a] fish" [Bartholomew 1942, p. 16]); a small fish is swallowed at once with 1-2 forward-upward jerks (about 1 s); a large one (one in five catches, Estimate) is held crosswise, juggled for 3-15 s, swallowed in 4-5 jerks, and followed by a head-shake. A toss in the air is the rare exception.

**Feeding in groups.** "concerted" fishing "seldom develop[s] [in] groups ... fewer than ten [or] twelve birds"; the flock forms a "long, narrow, but closely packed, line ... [at] right angles [to] the direction [of] movement", birds from the rear flying over to land in front, so that "one-quarter [to] one-half [of] the birds are usually beneath the water" [Bartholomew 1942, p. 14]; small flocks of under twenty fish in a "roughly circular formation" at "one bird [to] each square yard" [p. 17]; "feeds cooperatively in flocks of up to 600" [CDFW]. **Not for this scene:** the valley photos show swimming cormorants alone (20 of 21 records).

**Time budget.** "57% of daylight hours loafing ..., 18% actively foraging, 18% on water but not actively fishing, and 8% flying" (King, Glahn & Andrews 1995, wintering birds, via [BoW-B]; Snippet); "Their main two activities are fishing and resting, with more than half their day spent on the latter" [AAB-LH]; "fishing alternate[s] with much longer periods [of] rest" [Bartholomew 1943, p. 14].

### 7.2 Wing-drying

**What the sources say.**
- "On bright, sunny days it frequently stands on some convenient perch in an upright position, with its wings outstretched in spread-eagle fashion" [Bent 1922, p. 249]; at the Columbia's mouth birds sat on fish-trap stakes "for hours ... with their wings half spread" (Kobbe, in [Bent 1922, p. 257]). "After fishing, cormorants retire to high, airy perches to dry off and digest their meals—rocks, wires, tops of dead trees, ship masts" [AAB-LH]; "they stand on docks, rocks, and tree limbs with wings spread open to dry" [AAB-ID].
- **Why:** "The resulting wetness of the feathers is the proximate cause of the cormorant's characteristic habit of wing-spreading" [Rijke 1968] (abstract only). In this species the posture is "displayed only by wet individuals and was never accompanied by gular flutter", and its frequency "was not correlated with ambient (shade) temperature or solar intensity" [Hennemann 1984] (abstract only); "Double-crested cormorants exhibited spread-winged postures only when wet" [Hennemann 1983] (abstract only).
- **Orientation, this species:** Double-crested Cormorants "stood erect regardless of sun position, and orientated more strongly to the wind" (Hennemann, as summarised by [Sellers 1995]).
- **Durations and details, Great Cormorant** (the only study with numbers; [Sellers 1995], read in full):
  - Wing-spreading followed time in the water in "117 (93%)" of 126 cases; "On landing Cormorants rested for a minute or so before holding their wings outstretched", "almost always accompanied by spreading of the tail" [p. 28].
  - "Up to nine individual periods"; a median of 4 bouts in calm air and 2 at wind force 4 or more; "55 of 101 ... less than 4 minutes ... only 8% devoted more than 10 minutes"; the longest "18.5 minutes" [p. 29]; total time spreading = 5.974 − 1.052 × wind force (Beaufort), 4.75 min on average at force 0-1 [p. 30].
  - "62.5% of birds orientated within one compass point of the wind direction", "81% at wind speeds of 4 or more"; in calm air "51%" faced directly away from the sun [p. 32].
  - The wings were "three-quarters spread" in 55% of cases, "fully spread" in 28%; in winds of force 4 or more never more than about half spread; the wings were fanned or flapped during "just under half" of the bouts; rain stops it [p. 33].
- **The posture on the photos** (`doccor-spread-travis.jpg`, `doccor-spread.jpg`, `doccor-spread-durfee.jpg`, the game's `doccor.jpg`): body nearly upright (60-80° from level), neck in a loose S with the head up and the bill level or a little raised; the humeri out to the sides, the **wrists raised to about shoulder height or above**, the hands and primaries hanging down and out so that the trailing edge droops; the wing open about three-quarters; the tail spread and pressed against the perch as a prop. On `doccor-spread-travis.jpg` the near wing reaches 0.39 m from the shoulder to the wing tip (photo-measured), about two-thirds of a stretched half-span (computed).
- **In the valley photos:** 9 of 100 perched birds (9%) had their wings spread (section 3.2).
- **Adopted for the game:** after a dive bout the bird flies or swims to a perch, stands for **about 1 minute**, then spreads for bouts of **0.5-3 minutes** (2-4 bouts, about 4-5 minutes in all in calm weather, less in wind), with the wings folded for 10-60 s between; it faces **into the wind** (within ±20°), or, in calm, **away from the sun**; it flaps the spread wings slowly now and then. At any moment about **1 perched bird in 10** has its wings spread.

### 7.3 Perched and loafing

- "they stand on docks, rocks, and tree limbs" [AAB-ID]; "often perch on rocks, sandbars, or pilings near fishing sites" [BirdWeb]; "commonly seen perching on dead trees and utility poles when they are not fishing" [Rakestraw 2010-2022]; "perching conspicuously on buoys, posts, or floating timbers" [Bent 1922, p. 257]. In the valley photos: dead snags and stumps standing in the water, pilings, posts, logs and rocks (section 3.2).
- **Posture:** "holds body nearly vertical, with neck in S-shape and bill tilted slightly upward" [BoW-B] (Snippet). On the photos the body stands **35-45° above level**, the tail hanging down the perch, the legs short and set far back (photo-measured on `doccor-juv-perch.jpg` and `doccor-adult-perch-2.jpg`). Standing height, perch to crown: **0.57 m** with the neck erect (0.566 and 0.577 on two birds) and **0.42 m** with it drawn in (photo-measured).
- **Spacing on a shared snag:** about a body width apart, rarely touching (photo: `doccor-snag4.jpg`, `doccor-snag5.jpg`).
- **Roosts:** "dense nocturnal roosts" [BirdWeb]; "hundreds to thousands" at roosts [BoW-B] (Snippet); on San Francisco Bay over 2,000 roosted on a power line in October and November, the roost full "fifteen minutes after the sun has disappeared" [Bartholomew 1943, pp. 5, 8]. Not part of a morning scene.

### 7.4 Flight

**Posture.**
- "In flight, cormorants hold their head up, neck slightly bent, belly hanging low, and their wingbeats are slow and labored" [AAB-O]; "Flies with a bend in its neck" [AAB-ID, photo caption].
- Photo-measured on `doccor-fly-sonstroem.jpg` (strictly side-on): the neck straight out in front, the head about **5° above** the line of the body; bill tip to tail tip 0.69 m; the tail fanned a little; the black feet tucked against the belly near the tail, not trailing. On `doccor-fly-below.jpg` the head is raised a little more above the body line, the slight kink where the neck leaves the body. **Model: the neck straight with a gentle upward bend of 5-15° at its base, the head carried level.**

**Wingbeat, speed and flap-gliding.**
- Florida birds (1.41 kg, span 1.16 m, wing area 0.179 m²): **wingbeat 5.03 ± 0.14 Hz** (172 observations, 3,116 beats), **airspeed 14.5 ± 1.8 m/s** (189 observations), wavelength 2.88 m; code A, flap-gliding in only 0-5% of observations [Pennycuick 1990, Table 3, p. 177] (read from the page image). The Great Cormorant: 4.83 Hz, 15.0 m/s [Pennycuick 2001].
- Ground speed "61 km/h" [Custer & Bunck 1992] (abstract only); an organised flock flew "twenty-four [to] twenty-eight miles per hour" (10.7-12.5 m/s, computed) [Bartholomew 1943, p. 6].
- The words disagree on gliding: "constant and laborious flappings ... Occasionally it sails for a short distance" (Cooper, in [BBR 1884, p. 153]); "slow wing strokes ... set their wings and scale along at occasional intervals" [Bent 1922, p. 243]; "the birds alternate bursts of choppy flapping with short glides" [AAB-ID]; "the cormorant is marked by uninterrupted flapping" [Owre 1967, p. 7]. Pennycuick's timing (0-5% flap-gliding) is the measurement.
- **Adopted:** **5.2 Hz** (the valley bird is heavier than Pennycuick's Florida birds; scaling the frequency as mass^(3/8) from 1.41 to 2.0 kg gives about 5.7 Hz, computed, so 5.0-5.7 brackets it), **14.5 m/s**, continuous flapping with an occasional glide of under a second.

**Height and formation.**
- Over water usually "less than five feet above the surface. [On] calm days their wings may come within six inches [of the] water" [Bartholomew 1943, p. 4]; "it drops downward at first nearly to the water and flies along close to the surface, rising in an upward curve to its next alighting place" [Bent 1922, p. 249]; "over land, it flies much higher" [BoW-B] (Snippet).
- Flocks: "fifteen [to] twenty are the rule", flying in "single file" or an offset line, larger flocks in an "asymmetrical 'V'" [Bartholomew 1943, pp. 5-6]; "in a regular V-shaped formation like geese, or in single file" [Bent 1922, p. 243]; "V-shaped flocks that shift and reform" [AAB-ID]; "irregularly shaped lines or sloppy V's" [AAB-O]. In the valley photos every flying record is a single bird (9 of 9).

**Take-off and landing.**
- From the water: "Unless facing a strong wind it experiences considerable difficulty in rising from the water, pattering along its surface for a long distance" [Bent 1922, p. 249]; "When leaving the water, the cormorants make a long up-wind run, flapping vigorously ... both feet strike the water simultaneously" [Owre 1967, p. 61]; "2-footed thrusts, synchronized with wing beats" [BoW-B] (Snippet). "Before a cormorant takes off in flight, it tends to stretch its neck in the direction it intends to fly" [AAB-LH]. `doccor-takeoff.jpg` shows the run: wings in a full downstroke, body nearly level, both feet striking, the neck stretched forward. **Estimate:** a run of 3-8 m and 1-2 s in calm air.
- From a perch: it drops at an "angle [of] about thirty degrees below horizontal ... with steadily beating wings" [Bartholomew 1943, p. 4], then flies low over the water.
- Landing on water: "a coasting, belly-landing upon the water, frequently braking upon the surface with its tail" [Owre 1967, p. 60]. On a perch: it "will puff out the orange skin on its neck and, after touchdown, give a ritual little hop" [AAB-LH].

### 7.5 Sounds

- "This seems to be an altogether silent species" (Cooper, in [BBR 1884, p. 153]); "Except for an occasional hoarse grunting croak, when alarmed, I have never heard the double-crested cormorant make any vocal sound whatever and believe it is usually silent" [Bent 1922, p. 249].
- "deep, guttural grunts that sound a bit like an oinking pig. They grunt when taking off or landing, or during mating or aggressive displays, but otherwise are generally silent" [AAB-S]; grunts are "also heard in large fishing flocks, but otherwise generally silent" [BoW-B] (Snippet). A fishing flock is heard as a "continuous splashing, swishing sound" at "200 yards" [Bartholomew 1942, p. 16].
- **For the game:** no scheduled call. The three recordings in `data/birds.json` (XC638603, XC638602, XC657206, all "call") may be used, quietly and rarely, as a grunt at take-off or landing; otherwise the cormorant's sounds are the splash of a dive and the patter of a take-off.

### 7.6 Group size and the cast for a late-September morning

- In the valley photos (section 3.2) swimming birds are single (20 of 21), perched birds are single in two-thirds of records and never more than five together, fliers single.
- Elsewhere flocks are larger: travelling flocks of 15-20 [Bartholomew 1943, p. 5]; evening roost flocks "of from ten to forty" (Pearson, in [Bent 1922, p. 250]); fishing flocks of 10-1,900 on San Francisco Bay [Bartholomew 1942]. None of that is in the valley photos.
- **Recommended cast: four birds.** Two adults and one juvenile perched on a snag or stumps standing in the water (one of them spreading its wings for part of the time), and **one bird fishing** on open water (dive-and-surface cycles, then flying to the snag to dry, when another may drop off to fish). Adults and juveniles in the valley photos run about 54:46; one juvenile in four is honest and keeps the adult the default.
- **Daily rhythm:** of a daylight hour, about 18% fishing, 18% on the water idle, 57% loafing and 8% flying (King et al. 1995, Snippet): at any moment about one bird of the four is on the water.

## 10. For the model: the short version

- **Size:** bill tip to tail tip 0.60 m swimming, 0.79 m stretched; wingspan 1.20 m; mass 2.0 kg; wing chord 315 mm; tail 165 mm; culmen 57 mm; bill 17 mm deep and 12 mm wide at the nostril, hooked 6 mm; tarsus 65 mm; outer toe 86 mm; eye 11 mm across, 86 mm behind the bill tip.
- **Proportions:** head 2.9 culmens long; hindneck 3.5 culmens along its curve; body 0.38 × 0.13 × 0.12 m (length × depth × width); tail half the wing chord.
- **Floating:** awash. Back 25 mm above the water, crown 165 mm, tail flat on the water, about 80% of the body's depth under; waterline 0.50 m including the tail; bill about 15° up.
- **Perched:** upright, body 35-45° up, tail down the perch, 0.57 m tall erect, 0.42 m with the neck drawn in.
- **Diving:** smooth forward slide (sometimes a small leap), 22 s under, 12 s up, 6-15 dives a bout; fish held crosswise and swallowed head first with forward-upward jerks, then a head-shake.
- **Drying:** wings three-quarters spread, wrists up, hands drooping, tail propped, facing into the wind; 0.5-3 minute bouts, 4-5 minutes in all; one perched bird in ten at any moment.
- **Flight:** 5.2 Hz continuous flapping, 14.5 m/s, 0.5-1.5 m over the water; neck straight with a slight upward bend at the base; take-off a 1-2 s pattering run.
- **Two paints:** black adult with a scaled grey-brown saddle and orange-yellow face; brown juvenile with a pale buff throat and breast and a dark belly. Green-turquoise eye, black feet, no crests.
- **Cast:** four birds: three on a snag (two adults, one juvenile), one fishing.
- **Sound:** silent; at most a grunt at take-off or landing.

## 12. Open questions and confidence

**High confidence**
- The late-September adult: no crests, orange-yellow face, green eye, black feet, black body with a scaled grey-brown saddle [BBR 1884; Bent 1922; Dawson 1923; 28 photographed adults].
- The young bird's pale throat and breast and dark belly [BBR 1884; Bent 1922; AAB; 24 photographed young].
- Foot-propelled surface diving; head-first swallowing of fish caught crosswise [Bartholomew 1942; Owre 1967; Bent 1922].
- Riding nearly awash [every source; the photos; the buoyancy figure].
- Wingbeat 5.0 Hz and airspeed 14.5 m/s for Florida birds [Pennycuick 1990, read from the page].

**Medium confidence**
- **Linear measurements:** culmen, wing, tail and tarsus agree across BBR, Dawson and AVONET within 5-10%; Pyle and Palmer were not read.
- **Mass 2.0 kg:** from snippets of three sexed samples and one Dunning mean; the valley's *albociliatus* is small.
- **Heights on the water:** one strictly side-on bird measured, one more looked at; the culmen scale carries ±10%.
- **Wild dive and pause times:** Ross's figures through a later paper; the rest snippets.
- **Ages in the tally:** 52 birds aged by eye on small photos, biased toward tame young birds.

**Low confidence or not verified**
- **Spread-wing durations and orientation:** measured on the Great Cormorant; this species "orientated more strongly to the wind" by a summary of an abstract.
- **Body width (0.12 m)**, inner, middle and hind toes, bill width along its length: Estimates.
- **Surface swimming speed**, take-off run length, the share of large fish: Estimates.
- **Juvenile iris colour:** brown (BoW, Snippet) or greenish-grey (BBR); green on the one close valley photo.
- **Flight neck angle:** two photos.
- All Bartholomew quotations come from a damaged scan; words in brackets were restored by the reader.

**Could not source at all**
- A measured draft, waterline or surface swimming speed for any cormorant.
- Spread-wing bout lengths for this species.
- Neck length measured on a specimen.
- Group sizes on Willamette Valley lakes beyond the photos; Marshall et al. 2003 (*Birds of Oregon*) and eBird were not read.

**Sources I could not read** (not relied on except where marked Snippet or abstract only): the Birds of the World accounts behind the paywall; Pyle 2008; Palmer 1962; Dunning 2008; Glahn & McCoy; Ross 1974; King et al. 1995; Hennemann 1983 and 1984, Grémillet et al. 2005, Ribak et al. 2005, Rijke 1968, Lovvorn & Jones 1991, Custer & Bunck 1992 beyond their abstracts; Mahoney 1984; Marshall et al. 2003.
