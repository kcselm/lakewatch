# Red-winged Blackbird: the epaulet, the song spread, the reed bed, and perching on stems

Date: 2026-10-13
Purpose: source data for the detailed Red-winged Blackbird (`rewbla`, *Agelaius phoeniceus*): the epaulet, the song display tied to the song, and the stems the birds hold. Today the reeds are flat swaying blades and give the birds nothing to hold. This round puts the birds on real cattail and bulrush stems that sway in the wind, sometimes straddling two. The scene is Willow Lake, Willamette Valley, Oregon, on a late-September morning. The effective wind is about 2.8 m/s and gusty. The player stands at (0, 1.6, 0). The reed bed is the `reeds` zone, x −16…−3, z −2…−14 (`data/locations/lake.json`), 3.6-21.3 m from the player (Computed). The blackbird's point is `reed-perch` (−8, 1.9, −5), 9.4 m away.

Optics: binoculars give 7.5° on a 1080-pixel-tall view, 0.121 mm per pixel per metre of range: **1.14 mm/px at the reed-perch**, 0.44 mm/px at the bed's near corner, 2.6 mm/px at its far corner. The telescope gives 2°, 0.032 mm/px per metre: 0.30 mm/px at the reed-perch (all Computed).

The companion document, `docs/research/2026-10-13-red-winged-blackbird.md`, owns the bird: measurements, plumages and ages, field marks, structure, resting posture, general behaviour, and the cast. This one owns three things: **the epaulet and the song display; the reed bed's stems; and how blackbirds perch on them.** Where the two overlap (mass, plumage colours), this one gives only what the display and the stems need. The companion's values win if they differ.

How to read the citations: `[Key, p. N]` points to the References section at the end. "Snippet" means I saw the claim only in a search-engine excerpt or a secondary summary and could not read the source; treat it as unverified. "Computed" means my arithmetic on cited data or standard physics (g = 9.81 m/s², ρ_air = 1.225 kg/m³), with the formula stated. "Photo-measured" means I read points by hand on a grid laid over a cited photo or video frame. It is scaled by the exposed culmen where the bill is side-on: adult male 23.6 mm (*phoeniceus*) or 24.4 mm (*caurinus*) [Ridgway 1902, pp. 331, 341]. Any other scale is stated. "Video estimate" means I timed or measured it on frames extracted from a cited CC-licensed video, at 10-15 frames a second, against a spectrogram of the same clip's soundtrack. Timings are ±0.07 s. "Estimate" means no source gives the number and I chose it, with the basis stated. "By eye" means a judgement from the photos without a measurement. **Cross-species** marks every number taken from a plant or bird other than the ones named.

## 1. Summary

- **The epaulet** has two parts. The **red is the lesser wing-coverts** ("bright poppy red or vermilion (varying to scarlet or even, more rarely, to orange-chrome)"). Below it is a **buff band, the median ("middle") coverts**. On the Willamette's race *caurinus* this band is "deep ochraceous-buff or ochraceous in winter plumage" [Ridgway 1902, pp. 330, 341].
  - On a spread wing the whole badge measures **10.1-14.9 cm², mean 12.4** (21 breeding adults, California) [Merrill et al. 2014, read through a summariser].
  - On the folded wing of a male at rest, side-on, photo-measured: **red 39 × 19 mm (5.8 cm²) over a buff band about 38 × 10 mm**, together about **50 × 22 mm (8.6 cm²)**.
  - In song the red area seen side-on grows by **+34% to +43%**. From the front, at a full spread, it grows **about ×3** (Video estimate).
- **At rest the patch can be hidden.** "The adult male Red-wing when absorbed in feeding is a plain blackbird with a pale yellow stripe on his shoulder or one with a narrow band of red. The color may even be entirely covered up by the prevailing blackness of his costume" (Townsend 1920, in [Bent 1958, p. 129]).
  - Males keep the red "concealed" when a hawk is overhead, when trespassing, when dominated, and when feeding with other males [Nero 1956a, p. 9].
  - The covering feathers are the scapulars and the surrounding black contour feathers [Metz & Weatherhead 1992, Snippet].
  - **In late September in the Willamette, by eye on about 60 adult males in 208 iNaturalist records:** red showing in about 60%, only the ochre-buff bar in about 30%, nothing in about 10%.
- **Versions.**
  - **Adult male:** scarlet-vermilion over ochre-buff. Fresh autumn black is edged buff and rusty [Dwight 1900, in Bent 1958, p. 135].
  - **First-year male:** "dull orpiment-orange, each feather with subterminal bars or spots of black", over buff "mottled with black" (Dwight, in [Bent 1958, p. 135]).
  - **Female:** lesser coverts "broadly margined with brownish gray or red, or both (often extensively red)" [Ridgway 1902, p. 331]. The colour runs from brown to red-orange and is brighter in older females [Johnsen et al. 1996, p. 356].
  - Colours read off the photos are in section 2.4.
- **The song spread.** "Usually the head is lifted or thrust forward, the tail is spread and lowered, the wings are spread, and the epaulets are raised ... The head is thrust out with the first note of the song; at the climax, the drawn-out 'lee,' the tail and wing feathers are carried to the extreme position. The closure of song usually marks the return to normal position, but often the spread display is held for some time afterwards" [Nero 1956a, p. 9].
  - At the full display "the wing-tips touch the outer tips of the tail, the bird sometimes assuming an almost disk-like form" [Nero 1956a, p. 9]. The male "spreads and droops his wings until the tips reach to or below his feet, raises his red wing patches outward and forward" (Grinnell & Storer 1924, in [Bent 1958, p. 171]).
  - Intensity is graded, from epaulets shown with no spread to the full disk.
- **Timing, from two Commons videos (4 songs; Video estimate).** The spread starts **0-0.5 s before the first note**. It is full by the start of the trill ("ree"), **0.4-0.8 s after it began**. It holds through the trill (0.55-0.8 s) and relaxes **0.2-0.9 s after the trill ends**. A whole song lasts 0.9-1.15 s; Saunders gives ½-1½ s (in [Bent 1958, p. 141]).
  - At a full spread, seen from the front, the silhouette is **35-45% wider** and the tail **about 40% wider**.
  - Seen from the side, a low spread adds **8-15% to the silhouette's area**.
  - The full tail fan is **about 110°** (100-120°, photo-measured).
- **Late September.** "Song-spread is ... frequently given off the territory and before and after the breeding season, though generally without the postural components" [Nero 1956a, p. 10]. Birds of the World says non-breeding song "is not accompanied by visual display" [Yasukawa & Searcy 2020, Snippet]. Song continues "to some extent, in the fall" [Allen 1914, p. 89].
  - **xeno-canto:** of 12 foreground recordings from the Pacific coast (BC, WA, CA) in September-October, **8 contain song**. None is from Oregon. One male at Victoria, BC, on 4 October 2021 sang **8 songs in 50 s, one every 7.2 s** (Computed from XC678807).
  - **iNaturalist:** of 208 Willamette-box records from 13 Sep-12 Oct, **5 (2.4%) show a display posture**, including Polk Co. on 24 Sep and Lane Co. on 16 Oct (by eye).
  - **Video:** a Michigan male on 16 October 2025 gave two full front-on spreads in 18 s (Video estimate).
  - **Adopt** song in bouts, with most songs given at a low grade and a full spread on few of them.
- **The reed bed.** Willamette Valley lake margins hold **broadleaf cattail** (*Typha latifolia*), **hardstem bulrush / tule** (*Schoenoplectus acutus*) and **softstem bulrush** (*S. tabernaemontani*), with **reed canarygrass** (*Phalaris arundinacea*) on the fringes [ODFW 2020, pp. 18-19; Christy 2004, pp. 141, 149].
  - **Cattail:** shoots 150-300 cm tall. The stalk is "3-7 mm thick near inflorescence". Leaves are 10-23(-29) mm wide fresh, "distal blades about equaling inflorescence". The female spike is 5-25 cm long and **24-36 mm thick in fruit**, dark brown, later "mottled with whitish patches" [FNA, Smith 2000].
  - **Late-September colour:** leaves yellow-green to tan (#9aad5b to #adae67, with some still #4a6c35). Heads dark brown (#8b7a68), a few bursting to cotton (#d2bea5) (photo-measured, Ridgefield and Vancouver, WA).
- **Stem physics** (Computed: a tapered cantilever; no cattail stalk has been measured, so stiffness is constrained by the fact that blackbirds stand on cattail tops).
  - **Adopt bending stiffness EI ≈ 0.12 N m² just under the spike.** That is 4 GPa at 5 mm, or 1 GPa at 7 mm.
  - The stalk then sways at **about 1.5 Hz alone, 0.7 Hz with a female and 0.55 Hz with a male** on the top.
  - A perched bird on a leaning stalk dips **5 cm (female) to 9 cm (male)**. A landing at 0.25-0.5 m/s swings the top **5-11 cm**.
  - The 2.8 m/s wind leans the stalk's top only **about 2 cm** (5 cm at 4.5 m/s). The leaves move far more.
  - Damping ratio **about 0.12** (crop stand-ins 0.08-0.22, **cross-species**).
  - **Bulrush culms** measured on softstem relatives (E 0.12-0.44 GPa) **cannot hold a 65 g bird above about 0.6-1 m**. Birds on tules grip low, or straddle.
- **How they hold stems** (photo-measured and by eye).
  - **One vertical stem:** both feet clasp it, one 1-3 cm above the other. The body is 50-70° from horizontal and the tail hangs beside the stem.
  - **A cattail top:** the feet clasp the top of the spike or the bare axis above it. The body is 50-55° from horizontal.
  - **Straddling two stems:** the legs splay sideways, mostly at the ankle. The feet are **about 60-65 mm apart** across two stems (one bird). Fore and aft along a stem they can be up to about 110 mm apart (one autumn bird). One leg may stand out nearly horizontal.
  - Cattail stands hold 10-44 shoots per m² and tules 38-80 culms per m² [Gucker 2008; Dickerman 1982; Bray 1984 in Esser 1995]. Nearest neighbours are therefore **5-16 cm apart** (Computed). That is in reach of a straddle in tules, and sometimes in cattail.
- **New pose controls the model needs:**
  - `epaulet`: how far the covering feathers are drawn back.
  - `epauletRaise`: the coverts erected and fanned forward.
  - `fluff`: body feathers raised.
  - `wingHold`: wings lifted from the body at the shoulder and drooped, but not spread as in flight.
  - `gape`.
  - Per-foot targets, so the two feet can stand on two stems.
  - A stem the bird is attached to, which moves the bird.

## 2. The epaulet

### 2.1 What the sources say

**Which feathers, and their colour.**
- "Adult male in summer.—Uniform deep black ... lesser wing-coverts bright poppy red or vermilion (varying to scarlet or even, more rarely, to orange-chrome); middle coverts wholly buff or ochraceous-buff (paler at tips, sometimes almost white in midsummer birds)". "Adult male in winter.—Similar to the summer male but buff of middle wing-coverts deeper (more ochraceous-buff or buffy clay color) and interscapulars and scapulars narrowly margined with rusty" [Ridgway 1902, p. 330].
- For the northwestern race, which takes in western Oregon: "adult male with buff of middle wing-coverts deeper (deep ochraceous-buff or ochraceous in winter plumage)". The range is "from British Columbia ... south through western Washington and Oregon to northern California" [Ridgway 1902, p. 341].
- Adult winter (fresh autumn) male: "lustrous greenish black, feathers of head and back, greater wing coverts and tertiaries edged more or less ... with buff and ferruginous brown ... The bright scarlet-vermilion 'shoulders' are acquired together with the rich ochraceous buff median coverts" (Dwight 1900, in [Bent 1958, p. 135]). The complete postnuptial moult is in August (same page).
- The red is the **lesser coverts**, the buff the **median coverts**. Ridgway's descriptions do not single out the marginal coverts at the leading edge. On the photos the red runs to the leading edge of the wing at the wrist (by eye, `rewbla-280266447-503347769.jpeg`, `rewbla-34439854-54203982.jpg`), so paint the marginal coverts red with the lessers.

**How it is shown and hidden.**
- "Exposure of the patch of red feathers on the male's wing is a generalized display seen on many occasions, usually in conjunction with other postures or movements. At higher levels of intensity the red coverts may be erected and even vibrated, thus greatly increasing their area and color effect. When a hawk is overhead, when a male trespasses on another territory or is being dominated, or when a male is feeding together with other males on the ground, the red coverts are kept concealed" [Nero 1956a, p. 9].
- "Nearly all Redwings on the marsh sat quietly on their perches with concealed epaulets when hawks were soaring overhead" [Nero 1956b, p. 130].
- A male visiting a neighbour's territory kept "his epaulets ... concealed and he never gave song-spread, but each time he returned to his own territory he immediately gave song-spread with fully-exposed epaulets" [Nero 1956b, p. 133].
- Townsend's three resting states are in section 1: the yellow stripe only, a narrow red band, and the patch "entirely covered up" (in [Bent 1958, p. 129]). "These patches become actually dazzling in their effect ... for these beauty spots are most effective when seen from in front" (p. 130).
- **The covering feathers.** Metz & Weatherhead clipped "the feathers covering the epaulets" of 20 territorial males, so the epaulets stayed exposed. Clipped males chased intruders more and trespassed less: "owners that conceal their coverable badges enhance their ability to trespass" [Metz & Weatherhead 1992, Snippet of the abstract]. One secondary excerpt names these as the scapular feathers. The Condor follow-up speaks of clipping "the black feathers that are used by males to conceal their epaulets" [Metz & Weatherhead 1993, Snippet]. On the photos (by eye):
  - The **scapulars** cover the red from above and behind.
  - The **breast-side and flank feathers** cover it from the front and below.
  - What is left exposed is a strip along the lower edge, usually the buff median coverts.
- Blackening the red cost territories: "Sixty-four percent of the males with black epaulets lost their territories while only eight percent of the control males lost theirs" [Smith 1972, Snippet of the abstract]. Peek (1972) and Røskaft & Rohwer (1987) found the same in experiments I could not read. Yasukawa et al.'s meta-analysis found "epaulet size has a small positive effect on male reproductive success" [Yasukawa et al. 2010, p. 31].

### 2.2 Size

- **On the spread wing:** "The wing was spread against a standardized background with a ruler" and the whole badge was traced. "Epaulet size ranged from 10.07 cm2 to 14.92 cm2 (mean = 12.39 ± 1.32 cm2; median = 12.15 cm2)", for 21 adult males at Santa Barbara, California, in April-June [Merrill et al. 2014]. I read these through a page summariser (WebFetch), so treat the wording as close but not verbatim.
- **Searcy's measures** were the distance "from the bend of the wing to the farthest extent of red plumage (length of red) and to the farthest extent of yellow plumage (length of yellow)" [Searcy 1979, p. 417]. I could not read his table of values.
- **On the folded wing, side-on** (photo-measured on frames of [Homler 2026], scale 0.20 mm/px from the culmen, 118 px for 23.6 mm). The red is a rounded wedge on the upper forewing. The buff runs along its lower edge and spreads at its rear corner. The patch's long axis slopes down and back at about 40° (by eye).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9.0 s | at rest, alert, fully exposed | **39 × 19 mm, 5.8 cm²** | about 38 × 10 mm, 2.8 cm² | **50 × 22 mm, 8.6 cm²** |

  Red plus buff on the folded wing (8.6 cm²) is about 70% of Merrill's spread-wing mean (Computed). The rest lies under the scapulars and the wing's own feathers even when the patch is fully shown.
- **From the front.** On frames of [Danese 2025], 16 October, the bird faces the camera at about 0.34 mm/px (culmen). Both patches together show **about 2.4-2.7 cm² at rest** (about 1.3 cm² a side, the red edges peeking past the breast). At the peak of a full spread they show **about 7.9 cm²**, about 4 cm² a side, standing out beyond the body's outline. That is **×3** (Video estimate).

### 2.3 How much shows at rest in late September

By eye, I went through the 208 iNaturalist records from the Willamette box (43.5-46.2° N, 122.3-123.8° W; research grade, CC-licensed photos), dated 13 September-12 October in any year. About 60 adult males were seen side-on or three-quarter.
- **Red showing (a band or the full patch): about 60%.** Examples: `rewbla-34439854-54203982.jpg` (Benton Co., 15 Oct), `rewbla-139688394-238962522.jpeg` (Hillsboro, 22 Oct), and several perched on fences at Hillsboro.
- **Only the ochre-buff bar: about 30%.** Examples: `rewbla-249411357-445838645.jpg` (Benton Co., 12 Oct) and `rewbla-407442664-748749987.jpg` (Clark Co., WA, 10 Oct).
- **Nothing showing: about 10%**, mostly birds in flocks, feeding, or in poor light.
- This matches Townsend's three states. The higher share hidden in autumn fits Nero's list: feeding with other males, flocks, hawks.
- **Adopt** for a perched autumn adult male: `epaulet` drawn from 0.6 (full) 50%, 0.35 (red band) 15%, 0.15 (buff bar only) 30%, 0 (hidden) 5%. Re-draw on landing and after each song bout (Estimate shaped to the tally).

## 3. The song spread and other displays

### 3.1 What the sources say

- **Allen 1914 (Ithaca, NY).** The *con-cur-ee* song "is always accompanied by spreading of the wings and tail feathers and by erection of practically all the body feathers, especially those of the shoulder patches" [Allen 1914, p. 89].
- **Nero 1956 (Madison, WI).** The main description is quoted in section 1 [Nero 1956a, p. 9]. He adds:
  - "The extent of song-spread, and other displays, varies with the level of motivation."
  - "this song is sometimes given with little plumage display (Fig. 1a)" [p. 10].
  - "Although song is often given by solitary males, it is given with a greater frequency and extent in the presence of other males. It appears most extensive when directed toward a particular individual" [p. 10].
  - When a female lands, the males "perform a song-spread broadly (Fig. 1g, h, i)". The territory holder "displays for several seconds after the cessation of song", an "after-song" pose. "On some occasions males held their wings spread before they sang" [pp. 18-19].
  - Resident males answered strange males "by first giving song-spread, then bill-tilting, and then flying to attack" [Nero 1956b, p. 129].
- **Saunders (Connecticut).** "When a male red-wing sings, it commonly spreads the tail, half-spreads the wings, ruffles up the feathers on its back, and lifts the red feathers on its 'shoulders,' so that they flash brilliantly with the coming of the conqueree" (in [Bent 1958, p. 141]).
- **Townsend (Massachusetts).** "he spreads his tail, slightly opens his wings, puffs out all his feathers, and sings". In posture: "standing on some prominent perch, he leans forward ... with his back hunched up" (in [Bent 1958, pp. 129-130]).
- **Grinnell & Storer (California, the bicolored race).** "The male lowers and opens his tail in wide fan shape, spreads and droops his wings until the tips reach to or below his feet, raises his red wing patches outward and forward like a pair of flaming brands, and having swelled out as large as possible, utters his curious throaty song" (in [Bent 1958, p. 171]).
- **Birds of the World.** The account (Yasukawa & Searcy 2020) is subscriber-only. A search excerpt gives the grading: Song Spread runs from an "incipient" form ("epaulets exposed as song is delivered") "to full intensity (epaulet feathers erected, wings extended fully and curved downward, tail lowered and fully spread, and all contour feathers erected)". It adds: "Lower-intensity Song Spread is most often performed spontaneously; higher-intensity display is given in response to conspecifics" [Yasukawa & Searcy 2020, Snippet].
- **Orians 1961 (California).** On territory, males gave "Approximately six vocalizations per minute ... Of these, five, mostly songs, were directly concerned with territory ... the average duration of a song is slightly less than 1.5 seconds ... Three displays concerned with territory were given per minute, averaging two seconds each" [Orians 1961, p. 304]. Once "six males engaged in vigorous communal displays on the road, walking around each other in full song spread" [p. 291].
- **Orians & Christman 1968**, the comparative study with posture drawings and intensity grades, is on the Internet Archive only as a lending-library item. I could not read it, nor Beletsky 1996.

### 3.5 Other displays

- **Bill-tilting (bill-up).** "Prominent aspects of this pose are the stretched neck with raised beak and compressed body plumage ... Although the epaulets are exposed in this display, they are never erected." It is given at territory boundaries, by both sexes, and by young [Nero 1956a, p. 12]. Map: `neckExtend` 0.9-1, `headPitch` +0.6-0.9 rad, `fluff` −0.3 (sleeked), `epauletRaise` 0, `epaulet` 0.6. Hold 2-10 s (Estimate).
- **Crouch** (male, sexual). "The body is depressed, the head is hunched and held low, the tail is spread and brought downward, and the 'shoulders' are held out from the body with the epaulets erected. The wing tips may be crossed over the back or dropped close to the sides" [Nero 1956a, p. 13]. This is breeding-season behaviour; leave it out in September.
- **Flight song.** It is "always given in flight" [Nero 1956a, p. 10]. One account describes it after a chase: the male "slows his wingbeat, spreads his tail and 'parachutes' back to his singing perch" (Beer & Tibbitts 1950, in [Nero 1956a, p. 12]). Saunders: "often, when flying from one perch to another, hovers a foot or so above the contemplated perch and sings just before alighting" (in [Bent 1958, p. 141]).
- **Female wing-flipping**, feeding young [Nero 1956a, pp. 13-16]: not in September.
- **Flocks in autumn** keep tails lifted while feeding on the ground: "Flocks of Redwing males feeding on the ground often keep their tails lifted, a gesture not at all understood" [Nero 1956a, p. 13].

### 3.6 Female song and spread

"A song-spread display resembling that of the male is commonly given by females ... In the 'complete' display the bird stands upright, with head raised, tail spread and lowered, with the red-tinged epaulets sometimes erected." Female song is "a series of high, shrill and rapid notes, slowing and descending at the end ... 'spit-a-chew-chew-chew ...'". It is "usually given to other females from prominent positions within her area" and was heard from 8 April [Nero 1956a, p. 10]. Birds of the World calls it "less exaggerated in expression than in males" [Yasukawa & Searcy 2020, Snippet].

`rewbla-109672533-184884108.jpg` (Utah, 26 March) shows a female at full spread on a cattail spike: both wings raised and spread, tail fanned. In late September, give females **no song spread** (Estimate: every source places female song and spread in the breeding season).

## 4. Song and display in late September

### 4.1 What the sources say

- "Song-spread is given commonly on the territory but it is also frequently given off the territory and before and after the breeding season, though generally without the postural components. Migrating Redwings are in constant song, especially on the roosting grounds" [Nero 1956a, p. 10].
- Song "is given by the males alone, from first arrival until the middle of July, and again, to some extent, in the fall" [Allen 1914, p. 89].
- Saunders, in Connecticut: "Ordinarily red-wings do not sing at all in the fall, but once, October 31, 1937, I found a small flock of males, several of them singing" (in [Bent 1958, p. 142]).
- Birds of the World: in the non-breeding season "singing occurs in roosts throughout the day, but is not accompanied by visual display" [Yasukawa & Searcy 2020, Snippet].
- California's Sacramento Valley, in autumn: after the main morning feeding "the birds returned to the cattails where they rested, preened, sang, and bathed for much of the remainder of the day" [Orians 1961, p. 289]. Territories were set up "early in January in north-central California" [p. 290].
- **The Willamette Valley** (southern Benton County, 1941-48). Territories were established "in the latter part of March". "Although the Redwing has been considered a permanent resident in western Oregon, the bulk of the local summering population moved out of the study area in late July. Thus there was a period of approximately four weeks when only occasional individuals were seen before a large Redwing population was again present" [Evenden 1950, p. 43].
  - Late September is therefore the season of returned, fresh-plumaged birds in flocks, months before territories form.
- The northwestern race is "mainly migratory, though a few spend the winter as far north as western Washington" [Bent 1958, p. 168]. It "winters throughout its range" (same page). eBird-type frequencies in the game's own data put the species on 67-100% of Willow Lake checklists in every month (`data/birds.json`, `monthly`).

### 4.6 Adopted for late September (Estimate)

- **Song in bouts.** 3-10 songs at **6-12 s** intervals (the BC bout's 7.2 s), then 2-8 minutes quiet (preening, feeding, moving). More in the first two hours after sunrise and before dusk, matching the xeno-canto times.
- **Grade mix.** Realistic (Estimate from "generally without the postural components" and the 2.4% of photos with display): **grade 0 60%, grade 1 30%, grade 2 8%, grade 3 2%**.
- **For play** (compressed): **35 / 35 / 20 / 10%**, so a player watching one bout for a minute usually sees one medium or full spread.
- **First-year males** display at half the adult rate (Estimate; Nero saw them display fully, but only on spring territories). **Females** none (section 3.6).
- **What raises the grade:** another male landing within 5 m, or one flying over (Nero: "most extensive when directed toward a particular individual"). Add 1 grade, capped at 3 (Estimate).

## 5. The reed bed in late September

### 5.1 What grows on Willamette Valley lake margins

- **Fern Ridge Lake** (Lane Co.): "Although dominated by reed canarygrass (Phalaris arundinacea), the lake's emergent marsh includes hundreds of acres of hardstem bulrush (Schoenoplectus acutus) and cattail (Typha latifolia)"; "flooded in early April and remain flooded typically through mid-October". "Patches of cattail marsh are located intermittently along the edges of the lake. Bulrushes dominate deeper water emergent zones as large patches and circular clones extending out into lake open water" [ODFW 2020, pp. 18-19].
- **Northwestern Oregon plant associations.** The *Typha latifolia* association is found "throughout at lower elevations", in "shallow depressions, marshes, edges of lakes ... usually a monotype of Typha latifolia", cover 40-65% [Christy 2004, p. 149].
  - The *Schoenoplectus acutus* association "typically forms extensive, nearly monotypic stands" with "dense litter from the previous year's stand" [p. 141].
  - The softstem (*S. tabernaemontani*) association "appears to be more common in the interior valleys of western Oregon ... obscured ... in the Willamette Valley by plantings of Schoenoplectus acutus for wildlife habitat" (same page).
  - "Perennially wet marshes are usually too wet for the noxious grass Phalaris arundinacea to become established" [p. 7].
- **Willamette Valley NWR Complex** (Finley, Ankeny, Baskett Slough): permanent wetlands aimed at "40-60% cover of native emergent plants (e.g., bulrushes, cattails)", with reed canarygrass "often around the wetland fringes" [USFWS 2011, pp. 2-22, 4-3].
- **Benton County, 1940s.** Blackbird nests were placed in sedges, spirea, Oregon ash, spikerush and cattail, with one "placed in reed canary grass" [Evenden 1950, p. 42].
- Narrowleaf cattail (*T. angustifolia*) is "exotic, naturalized" in Oregon, and the hybrid *T. × glauca* "exotic, naturalized?" [Carr, Oregon Flora Image Project]. Neither is reported from the mid-valley in what I read.
- **Adopt** a bed of **broadleaf cattail with tule patches on its water side**, and a fringe of reed canarygrass on its land side (Estimate following [ODFW 2020; Christy 2004]).

### 5.3 Hardstem bulrush (tule), and the others

- **Hardstem bulrush.** Culms "cylindric, 1-4 m × 2-10 mm". The Oregon variety *occidentalis* ("Common tule") has culms "dark to light green, 1-4 m × 4-12 mm, firm to soft". Its inflorescence is pushed aside by an erect bract 1-9 cm long; the "Spikelets [are] 4-190, 6-24 × 4 mm" and the scales "reddish to orange-brown to straw-colored" [Smith 2002 (FNA), pp. 48-49].
  - The USDA guide gives "1 to 2 cm (0.4 to 0.8 in) thick and 1 to 3 m (3 to 10 ft) tall". "Seeds ripen in late August to September" [Tilley 2012, p. 2].
  - Density is 38-59 green culms per m², with up to 80 dead [Bray 1984, in Esser 1995]. FNA gives no taper. **Adopt** 2.2 m tall, 15 mm at the base tapering to 5 mm under the head, grey-green #6f8150 with straw-brown tips and a rusty-brown spikelet cluster 4-6 cm long at the top (colours Estimate).
- **Softstem bulrush:** "0.5-3 m × 2-10 mm" [Smith 2002]; 600-700 shoots per m² in tidal stands [Heuner et al. 2015].
- **Reed canarygrass:** "2 to 7 feet (0.6-2 m) tall"; panicle "2 to 12 inches (5-30 cm)"; "beige over time"; heads and stems "die back" after seeding [Waggy 2010; USDA PHAR3]. Stems are hollow, 5-10 mm across (one specimen, Snippet). They are too weak and low for a perched blackbird in September; keep them as a fringe of tan grass 1-1.5 m tall.

### 5.5 Biomechanics: what has been measured

- **No *Typha* flowering stalk has a measured stiffness, frequency or damping that I or the helper search could find.** Measured *Typha* mechanics is on leaves:
  - "The flexural strength and flexural modulus of Typha leaves vary from 0.71 MPa to 7.12 MPa and from 2.16 MPa to 269.73 MPa", and the second moment of area at the leaf base is "114.64 ~ 152.05 times larger than that at the apex" [Liu et al. 2017].
  - The leaf's internal fibre cables have Young's modulus "22·8 ± 7·4 × 10⁹ Pa" [Witztum & Wayne 2014, read through a summariser].
- **Other emergents (cross-species).**
  - "Near the base of the stalk of Scirpus rosthornii, the elastic modulus is about 1 GPa ... The elastic modulus of the other four plants changes in the range of 0.1-1.0 GPa" (including *S. tabernaemontani* and *Typha orientalis* leaves) [Zhao et al. 2015].
  - Softstem bulrush: "Young's modulus was four times higher for S. maritimus (4.4 10⁸ N m⁻²) than for S. tabernaemontani (1.2 10⁸ N m⁻²)" and "with 0.017 N m², stems of S. maritimus were about twice as stiff as the ones of S. tabernaemontani (0.009 N m²)". These were basal stems 8.9 mm across and 1.14 m tall, "too flexible" to break cleanly [Heuner et al. 2015].
  - *Phragmites* culm walls: E 16-23 GPa in flowering culms, 9-17 GPa in non-flowering ones [Ostendorp 1995, Table 1, p. 91].
- **Sway and damping (cross-species, crops).**
  - Free-oscillation frequencies: alfalfa 1.05 Hz and wheat 2.5 Hz. Wind-driven waves over the canopy ("honami") run at the plants' own frequency, "independently of the wind velocity" [Py et al. 2006, Table 1, p. 430].
  - Maize, oats and oilseed rape: 0.7, 1.1 and 2.6 Hz, with "Mean damping ratios for maize and oats ... 0.13 and 0.11"; oilseed rape 0.13-0.22 [Joseph et al. 2020].
  - Alfalfa ξ ≈ 0.34, which is a damping ratio of about 0.17 [Doaré et al. 2004; conversion by the helper search].
  - Plants with leaves damp more [Speck & Spatz 2004, abstract].
- **What the photos add.** Blackbirds stand on the tops of cattail stalks, just under or on the spike, in every season (section 6). The stalks bend visibly but do not fold (by eye: `rewbla-283237509-508916202.jpg`, `rewbla-283475542-509367785.jpg`, `rewbla-282523985-507592401.jpg`). A stalk that buckled under a 65 g bird at 1.85 m could not be photographed like this. That bounds its stiffness from below.

### 5.6 The stem model (Computed)

**Method.**
- **Geometry.** The stem is a tapered cantilever clamped at the bed.
  - **Cattail:** the stalk is 16 mm across at the base, 10 mm at 1.0 m (the scape inside its sheaths; the sheaths count only for drag) and 5 mm under the spike at 1.85 m. The spike runs 1.85-2.0 m, is 15 × 2.8 cm and weighs 12 g (Computed: a cylinder at 130 kg/m³). Stem density is 600 kg/m³ (Estimate).
  - **Tule:** 15 mm at the base tapering to 5 mm at 2.2 m, density 250 kg/m³, a 4 g head.
- **Bending.** I = πd⁴/64. The deflection w(z) is the double integral of M/EI.
  - **Stiffness at the perch:** k = 1/w(perch) under a unit side load there.
  - **Buckling load:** P_cr is the Rayleigh quotient ∫EI w''² / ∫w'² on that shape.
  - **Frequency:** Rayleigh with the static-deflection shape under the stem's, spike's and bird's own weights, reduced by √(1 − P/P_cr) for the gravity load on a top-heavy stem.
- **Loads.** Drag is ½ρC_D A U² (C_D 1.0 on the spike, 1.2 on the stalk). The wind falls from its full value at the top to 60% at 0.9 m, in the leaves.
- **Dip.** A bird on a stem leaning 8°, its centre of mass 25 mm off the stem's axis, loads it sideways by W sin 8° and by the couple W × 0.025 m. This is amplified by 1/(1 − P/P_cr).
- **Landing swing.** A bird arriving with horizontal speed v gives amplitude A = v·m_b/(m_b + m_eff)/ω_b, with m_eff = k/ω₀² and ω_b the loaded angular frequency.
- **Masses.** Male 65 g, female 42 g. Searcy's captive autumn males weighed 71.2 g (adults) and 71.5 g (first-year) [1979, p. 419]; the companion document owns masses.

| 8 GPa (0.25 N m²) | 3.76 N/m | 432 g | 2.21 Hz | 1.13 Hz | 0.93 Hz | 2 / 4 cm | 3.3 / 6.6 cm | 0.9 / 2.4 cm |

**Cattail, bird gripping the stalk at 1.4 m:** at E 4 GPa, k is 6.7 N/m and the stalk sways at 1.12 Hz (42 g) or 0.96 Hz (65 g). The dip is 1.3 / 2.2 cm and the landing swing 2-4 cm.

**Adopt EI ≈ 0.12 N m² under the spike** (E 4 GPa on 5 mm, equivalently 1 GPa on 7 mm, the thick end of FNA's 3-7 mm).
- E 2 GPa or less at 5 mm makes a male fold the stalk, which the photos rule out.
- The measured emergent moduli (0.1-1 GPa) fit only if the stalk is near 7 mm.
- *Phragmites*' 9-23 GPa is an upper bound. At 8 GPa a stalk would barely move under a bird, which also disagrees with the photos (by eye).

**Tule:**

| E | Perch | Buckles at | f₀ alone → with 65 g | Dip, 65 g | Wind lean at the top, 2.8 m/s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.44 GPa (measured on *S. maritimus*) | 0.6 m | 92 g | 0.55 → 0.20 Hz | 5 cm | 24 cm |
| 0.44 GPa | 1.2 m | 37 g: **folds under any blackbird** | — | — | 24 cm |
| 2 GPa ("very firm" var. *acutus*) | 1.2 m | 169 g | 1.24 → 0.73 Hz | 3.5 cm | 5.4 cm |
| 2 GPa | 0.8 m | 294 g | 1.27 → 1.11 Hz | 1 cm | 5.4 cm |

With measured softstem stiffness, a tule culm cannot hold a blackbird above about 0.6-1 m. On the water side of the bed, birds should take tules low, bend one over, or **straddle two**.

**Wind, in game terms.**
- A flowering stalk leans about 2 cm at its top in the steady 2.8 m/s and about 5 cm in gusts to 4.5 m/s. It oscillates about that lean at its own frequency: 1.5 Hz unloaded, 0.5-0.7 Hz with a bird.
- Following the crop result [Py et al. 2006], neighbouring stems move in step in bands that run downwind. The scene's gust front already has bands 18 m apart moving at 2.9 m/s (`wind.ts`).
- The leaves are far softer than the stalk (EI at the tip near 10⁻⁴ N m²: 2 mm⁴ at 0.27 GPa or less [Liu et al. 2017], Computed). They should lean and flutter much more than the stalk carrying the bird.

**Damping. Adopt ζ = 0.12** (crops 0.08-0.22, **cross-species**; "Plants with leaves have a significantly higher damping" [Speck & Spatz 2004, abstract]). Each cycle the swing falls to e^(−2πζ) = **0.47** of the last. A landing swing is down to 10% after 3 cycles: 5.5 s with a male on a top perch (Computed).

### 5.7 Spacing (Computed)

For stems scattered at random at density λ per m², the mean distance to the nearest neighbour is 0.5/√λ (Clark-Evans):
- **cattail shoots:** 10-44 per m² → **7.5-16 cm**;
- **flowering cattail stalks:** 2-6 per m² → **20-35 cm**;
- **tule culms:** 38-80 per m² → **5.6-8.1 cm**.

A blackbird's straddle spans 4-11 cm (section 6.3). Two neighbouring tules are usually in reach. Two cattail flowering stalks rarely are, but a flowering stalk and a leafy shoot or dead stalk beside it often are.

## 6. How blackbirds perch on stems

### 6.1 What the sources say

- Allen's Plate XI is titled "Redwings near the nesting site illustrating their method of perching on the vertical cat-tails" [Allen 1914, Plate XI, facing p. 98]. I could not make out the birds' stance in the scan.
- On autumn roosts, quoting Nuttall: "towards the close of September ... they begin to roost in the reeds ... upon these they perch or cling, so as to obtain a support above the surrounding waters of the marsh" [Allen 1914, p. 103]. At night, "the sudden crackling of a flag under its unaccustomed weight startles a cry from some frightened bird" [p. 80].
- "Every available perch, not so high as to be conspicuous, is filled with birds down to the water's surface" [Allen 1914, p. 80].
- Approaching a female, males "slowly hop along the branches toward her (usually with erect epaulets), and then fly down with song-spread" [Nero 1956a, p. 18]. In bill-tilting on a tree, "the uppermost bird moved down and even hung down to display to the lower bird" [p. 12].
- "Red-wings have sturdy legs with muscles designed to allow them to do the 'splits' if they are perched on two different cattails at once" [de Kort 2014, attributed to the Cornell Lab; secondary, no primary source given].
- An iNaturalist observer under a straddling male: "When birds spread their legs, most of the spreading is at the ankle" (observation 288754636; an observer's remark, not a source).
- I found **no published measurement** of stance, leg splay, or how often blackbirds straddle.

### 6.2 Gripping one stem

- **Vertical stem, side-on** (`rewbla-333301108-605079409.jpg`, Palomar Mountain CA, male on a vertical cattail stalk; `rewbla-364816575-666010467.jpg`; `rewbla-150096365-258742722.jpeg`). By eye:
  - Both feet clasp the stem, toes wrapped from both sides.
  - The feet are one above the other, **1-3 cm apart**, the upper foot on the bird's side toward the head.
  - The tarsi angle down and forward from the belly to the stem. The body axis is **50-70° from horizontal**. The breast sits 2-4 cm off the stem; the tail hangs beside it, nearly parallel.
  - The body is often rolled 10-30° about the stem's axis toward the side the bird faces.
- **On the top of a cattail** (`rewbla-280663755-504102240.jpg`, `rewbla-282523985-507592401.jpg`, `rewbla-283470801-509357952.jpg`, `rewbla-34439854-54203982.jpg`, `rewbla-95423989-158415397.jpeg`). The feet clasp the top of the spike, or the bare male axis above it (`rewbla-283475542`, one foot on a 4-5 mm axis). The body axis is **50-55° from horizontal** on the two side-on birds (photo-measured: nape to tail tip). A bird on a flat perch stands at 34° (photo-measured, [Homler 2026]).
- **Gripping the spike's side** (`rewbla-282498300-507542486.jpg`): one foot high on the spike, one lower, the bird hanging off its side.

Reach (Computed, rough): tarsus 29.5 mm and middle toe 21.8 mm for an adult *caurinus* male [Ridgway 1902, p. 341], plus a tibia of about 35 mm (Estimate). Each foot can reach about 60 mm from the hip, so 100-120 mm between the feet at the limit. Adopt a **comfortable straddle of 40-80 mm**, up to 110 mm. The feet sit at heights up to 40 mm apart. The body rolls toward the lower foot by about half the slope of the line between the feet (by eye).

**How often:** no source counts it. In the 311 "cattail" and 132 "display" iNaturalist records I looked through, straddles were few: by eye perhaps 1 in 30 of birds perched in reeds. **Adopt** a straddle on 1 perch in 6 in the tules and 1 in 15 in the cattail (Estimate, raised for play because it is a striking pose).

### 6.4 Moving between stems

- **Short flights** of 1-5 m between stems, with 2-4 wingbeats and a stall: the tail fans, the feet reach forward, and the bird lands with a small lift of the wings (by eye on the photos; no source times it).
- **Hops and sidling.** "slowly hop along the branches" [Nero 1956a, p. 18]. In a dense stand, switching to a stem within reach is a step: one foot moves first, so the bird straddles for 0.2-0.5 s, then the other follows (Estimate).
- **Climbing a stem:** foot over foot, 2-3 cm a step, about 3 steps a second (Estimate; Nero's "moved down and even hung down" shows it can).
- **Leaving:** a stem loaded by a bird springs back when it lets go. The stem's top moves the dip distance (5-9 cm) back past upright, then decays at ζ 0.12 (Computed from section 5.6).

### 6.5 The landing dip, as a sequence (Computed, with Estimates)

1. The bird arrives braking. Horizontal speed at touchdown is **0.25-0.5 m/s** (Estimate: perching birds stall onto stems). The vertical speed goes into the stem's axis and is absorbed by the legs.
2. Over **0.15-0.3 s** the stem top swings away by **5-11 cm** (cattail top perch, male) or 2-4 cm (mid-stalk), and the stem settles to the static dip (5-9 cm).
3. It oscillates about that at **0.55-0.7 Hz** (top perch), the amplitude falling to 47% each cycle. It is still within 1 cm after about 4-5 s.
4. The bird's body counter-rotates to keep its head steady: the head moves by under a third of the stem top's motion (Estimate; birds stabilise their heads, as the kingfisher document notes for hovering).

**What should change the design:**
1. **The reeds need real stems.** Today's flat 6 cm blades, 2.2 m tall, are about 2-3 times as wide as cattail leaves (10-23, rarely 29 mm), and there is no flowering stalk. The bed needs:
   - narrower twisted leaves;
   - a separate set of thin flowering stalks, 5-7 mm under a 15 × 2.8 cm dark-brown spike at 1.85-2.0 m;
   - tules (round grey-green culms with a rusty head) on the water side.
2. **The stalk a bird stands on must move much less than the leaves.** The shader leans every blade's tip 0.16-0.32 m. A flowering stalk leans about 2 cm in this wind and sways at about 1.5 Hz. With a bird on it, it sways at 0.55-0.7 Hz.
   - The bird must ride its stem: a CPU-side oscillator per holdable stem, with the bird's feet attached.
   - The instanced, shader-swayed stalks around it must not drift away from it. Either the holdable stalks are separate meshes, or the shader and the CPU use the same formula.
3. **Song should come in bouts, not every 8-25 s.** In late September most songs should show little or no spread, and a full spread should be occasional. The full spread is compressed for play to about 1 song in 10.
4. **Tie the display to `rewbla-XC703912.mp3`'s note onsets** (2.80 / 5.18 / 9.38 s). Do not use `rewbla-XC469000.mp3` as the song: it is a series of *chack*-type notes, perhaps female chatter. That clip also has "multiple birds singing", so only one of its songs should be the focal bird's. Trim it, or schedule the spread on one of them.
5. **The bird needs six new pose controls:** `epaulet`, `epauletRaise`, `fluff`, `wingHold`, `gape`, and per-foot targets (section 8.1). The existing `wingSpread` and `dry` open the flight wing and cannot make a folded wing held out and drooped.
6. **Tules cannot carry a blackbird near their tops.** Straddles and low perches belong on the water side of the bed.
7. **Female versions do not display in September.**

**Still to find:**
- Orians & Christman 1968 (posture drawings and intensity scoring); Beletsky 1996; the Birds of the World account's Behavior and Sounds sections. All three are behind a lending library or a subscription.
- A measured flexural rigidity, natural frequency or damping for a *Typha* flowering stalk or a hardstem bulrush culm.
- An Oregon recording or video of autumn song with display.
- The share of cattail shoots that flower.
- Any count of straddling.

**Cross-species substitutions, all of them:**
- Stiffness of other emergent stems: *Schoenoplectus tabernaemontani*, *Bolboschoenus maritimus*, *Scirpus rosthornii*, *Phragmites australis*, and *Typha* and *Typha orientalis* leaves [Heuner et al. 2015; Zhao et al. 2015; Ostendorp 1995; Liu et al. 2017].
- Sway frequency, wind waves and damping from field crops: alfalfa, wheat, maize, oats, oilseed rape [Py et al. 2006; Doaré et al. 2004; Joseph et al. 2020].
- The damping effect of leaves from giant reed, *Arundo donax* [Speck & Spatz 2004].
